14 — Modular Combinatorics and q-Analogues

This chapter begins where the familiar formula

\[\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\]

stops being a legal modular computation. A factorial table modulo a prime \(p\) only supports division while every denominator is nonzero, hence while the relevant factorial arguments are below \(p\). The two warning signs are \(n\ge p\) and a composite modulus. The cure is to separate the powers of each prime (which are not invertible) from the remaining unit part (which is).

The notation \(v_p(x)\) means “the exponent of \(p\) in \(x\)”; for example, \(v_2(40)=3\). It is the bookkeeper that tells us exactly when a binomial coefficient becomes zero modulo a prime power.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Read units, valuations, and CRT, binomial counting, and, for subspaces, linear algebra. The advanced practice also uses convolution and Stirling transforms. Learn to compute binomials when factorials vanish, distinguish divisibility from residue value, derive a subspace count by counting bases, and recognize when a q-product is a polynomial whose displayed divisions are illegal.

Throughout, \(n\ge0\) and \(0\le k\le n\) are integers; set \(\binom nk=0\) outside that range. A prime is denoted \(p\), its power modulus \(Q=p^e\) with \(e\ge1\), and a vector-space field size \(q\). For a nonzero integer \(a\), \(v_p(a)\) counts its factors of \(p\); \(v_p(0)=+\infty\) if needed, but the factorial arguments below are always nonnegative and their factorials nonzero. All divisions by factorials in exact counting occur over integers or rationals first; modular inverses are a separate computational step.

1. Lucas and Kummer

For prime \(p\), write \(n=\sum n_i p^{i}\) and \(k=\sum k_i p^{i}\), where each digit is in \([0,p)\). Lucas gives

\[\binom nk\equiv\prod_i\binom{n_i}{k_i}\pmod p.\]

Here is the proof. In \(\mathbb F_p[x]\), the middle coefficients of \((1+x)^p\) are divisible by \(p\): for \(0<j<p\), the numerator of \(\binom pj\) has one factor \(p\) and its denominator has none. Thus \((1+x)^p=1+x^p\), and iterating gives

\[(1+x)^n=\prod_i((1+x)^{p^i})^{n_i} =\prod_i(1+x^{p^i})^{n_i}.\]

To obtain \(x^k\), choose \(x^{k_i p^i}\) from factor \(i\). Each chosen digit is less than \(p\), so this representation is unique and carries never occur in this expansion. The coefficient is the product in Lucas’s formula. Thus one huge binomial coefficient becomes a product of tiny digit-level ones. For example, \(5=(101)_2\) and \(2=(010)_2\), so Lucas includes \(\binom 0 1=0\) and therefore \(\binom52\equiv0\pmod2\).

Kummer says \(v_p\binom nk\) is the number of carries in base \(p\) when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\). It supplies more information than the yes/no answer modulo \(p\): a carry count of \(c\) says the coefficient is divisible by \(p^c\). In the example above, \(2+(5-2)=(10)_2+(11)_2=(101)_2\) has one carry, agreeing with \(v_2\binom52=1\). A digit with \(k_i>n_i\) detects that some borrowing is necessary, but borrows can propagate through further positions. For example, \(8-1=(1000)_2-(0001)_2\) has three borrows, although only one original digit satisfies \(k_i>n_i\). Accordingly \(v_2\binom81=3\). Lucas is the value theorem; Kummer is the divisibility theorem.

For the derivation, let \(s_p(n)\) be the sum of base-\(p\) digits. Counting how many multiples of \(p,p^2,\ldots\) occur in \(n!\) gives

\[v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\lfloor n/p^j\rfloor =\sum_i n_i(1+p+\cdots+p^{i-1}) =\frac{n-s_p(n)}{p-1}.\]

Subtract the factorial valuations to obtain

\[v_p\binom nk=\frac{s_p(k)+s_p(n-k)-s_p(n)}{p-1}.\]

Each carry replaces \(p\) units in one digit by one in the next, decreasing the digit sum by \(p-1\); count every carry, including propagated ones. This proves Kummer. With a table through \(p-1\), Lucas queries cost \(O(\log_p(n+1))\) after \(O(p)\) preprocessing and memory. That table is impractical for a huge prime; computing small digit binomials by products instead costs the sum of the smaller digit arguments. Lucas changes the scale but does not guarantee a fast digit-binomial routine.

2. Prime powers and composite moduli

For \(Q=p^{e}\), first use Legendre’s formula

\[v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\left\lfloor\frac n{p^j}\right\rfloor.\]

Then represent a factorial as \(n!=p^vU(n)\), where \(U(n)\) is not divisible by \(p\) and is therefore invertible modulo \(p^e\). Compute this p-free factorial by multiplying only terms not divisible by \(p\) in complete blocks, then recursing on \(\lfloor n/p\rfloor\): the recursive call accounts for the factors left after removing one \(p\) from every multiple of \(p\). This is the reason a prime-power factorial routine is more than a factorial table. Then

\[\binom nk=p^{v_p(n!)-v_p(k!)-v_p((n-k)!)} U(n)U(k)^{-1}U(n-k)^{-1}\pmod {p^e}.\]

If the exponent is at least \(e\), the answer is zero modulo \(p^e\). Otherwise all inverted parts are units and the displayed expression is valid. For a composite modulus \(m\), factor \(m\) into coprime prime powers, solve one local problem per factor, and use CRT to select the unique residue modulo \(m\) with those local residues. Never cancel numerator and denominator directly in \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\): a nonzero residue can still be a non-unit, such as \(2\) modulo \(6\).

The exact unit-factorial recurrence

Define \(U(n)=n!/p^{v_p(n!)}\) as an integer, then reduce it modulo \(Q\). Let \(A(t)=\prod_{1\le j\le t,\ p\nmid j}j\pmod Q\) for \(0\le t\le Q\). The factors in \(n!\) not divisible by \(p\) contribute a product \(V(n)\). Each multiple \(pj\) contributes the p-free part of \(j\), so

\[U(n)=V(n)U(\lfloor n/p\rfloor),\qquad V(n)\equiv A(Q)^{\lfloor n/Q\rfloor}A(n\bmod Q)\pmod Q.\]

The second equality groups full periods of length \(Q\): corresponding factors are congruent, and divisibility by \(p\) repeats. Stop at \(U(0)=1\). The recursion invariant is that every factor has had its entire p-power removed, including factors recursively contributed by multiples of \(p\). A full table costs \(O(Q)\) time and memory. With modular binary exponentiation at each level, a query costs \(O(\log_p(n+1)\log(n+1))\) modular operations; special formulas for the period product improve this but are not required. For huge \(Q\) this preprocessing is unavailable: use a problem-specific bound, factorwise products for small \(k\), or an independently specified prime-power routine. Factoring a composite answer modulus has its own cost and contract.

NoteExample: a nonzero binomial despite zero factorials

Compute \(\binom62\) modulo 8. The factorial valuations are \(4,1,3\), so the net exponent is zero. The exact unit factorials are \(U(6)=45\), \(U(2)=1\), \(U(4)=3\). Hence the residue is \(5\cdot1^{-1}\cdot3^{-1} \equiv5\cdot3=7\pmod8\), agreeing with 15. Ordinary factorial residues would contain zeros and nonunits and could not be divided. For \(\binom42=6\), the valuation is one and the remaining unit residue is 3, giving \(2\cdot3=6\) modulo 8. Valuation alone would not identify the unit.

3. Gaussian binomial coefficients

Here \(q\) is either a formal variable or the size of a finite field. When \(q\) is a prime power, the Gaussian (or q-) binomial coefficient counts \(r\)-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_q^{n}\):

\[\binom nr_q=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{q^{n-i}-1}{q^{r-i}-1}.\]

Assume \(0\le r\le n\), with value 1 for \(r=0\); set it to zero for out-of-range \(r\). Field size \(q\) is a positive prime power; the answer modulus, if any, is another number. The product first states an exact integer count, not a formula for arbitrary modular division.

To see the product, count ordered independent vectors in two ways. The first vector has \(q^n-1\) choices, the next has \(q^n-q\) choices, and so on; divide by the number \((q^r-1)(q^r-q)\cdots(q^r-q^{r-1})\) of ordered bases of one fixed \(r\)-subspace. The same polynomial is the inversion generating polynomial for binary strings with \(r\) ones, and the area generating polynomial for partitions inside an \(r\times(n-r)\) rectangle. Thus \(\binom42_q= 1+q+2q^2+q^3+q^4\) records how many such objects have each statistic value.

To connect binary strings, define an inversion as a pair of positions with a 1 before a 0. Splitting by the first bit: a leading 0 leaves a length-\(n-1\) string with \(r\) ones; a leading 1 leaves \(r-1\) ones and adds \(n-r\) inversions. This gives the same recurrence below and the same boundary values, proving the polynomial identity by induction. Record, for each successive 1, how many zeros follow it; these counts form a nonincreasing list bounded by \(n-r\). Its Ferrers rows form a partition inside an \(r\times(n-r)\) rectangle, and their sum is the inversion count. For \(n=4,r=2\), the strings \(0011,0101,0110,1001,1010,1100\) have respectively \(0,1,2,2,3,4\) inversions. At \(q=2\), their weighted total is 35, the number of 2-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_2^4\); the six strings themselves are not the 35 subspaces.

The recurrence

\[\binom nr_q=\binom{n-1}r_q+q^{n-r}\binom{n-1}{r-1}_q\]

remains valid even when the product formula appears to divide by zero. Fix a hyperplane \(H\cong\mathbb F_q^{n-1}\). An \(r\)-subspace is either contained in \(H\), giving the first term, or meets \(H\) in an \((r-1)\)-subspace; the latter case has \(q^{n-r}\) extensions, giving the second term. It is often the safest way to compute a whole table. To justify the extensions, fix \(W=U\cap H\) of dimension \(r-1\). In the quotient vector space \(V/W\), an extension \(U/W\) is a line outside the hyperplane \(H/W\). There are \(q^{n-r+1}-q^{n-r}\) vectors outside that hyperplane, and each line contains \(q-1\) such nonzero vectors. Division gives \(q^{n-r}\) lines. The recurrence with the boundary values computes a table through \(n,r\) in \(O(nr)\) ring operations with \(O(r)\) rolling memory, assuming powers of \(q\) are available; evaluate rows in an order preserving both prior-row entries. For a fixed numerical \(q\) modulo a prime \(P\), precompute q-integers and q-factorials only while their factors are invertible. If some \(q^i-1\) vanishes modulo \(P\), ordinary division is invalid even though the Gaussian binomial itself remains defined. Use the division-free recurrence, a proved q-Lucas theorem when its hypotheses match, or cancel cyclotomic-factor exponents before evaluation.

Define the q-integer \([i]_q=1+q+\cdots+q^{i-1}\) directly as a polynomial. At \(q=1\), it is \(i\), although the expression \((q^i-1)/(q-1)\) is \(0/0\). Thus at \(q=1\) the q-factorial method reduces to ordinary factorials when \(1,\ldots,n\) are units, and the Gaussian coefficient equals \(\binom nr\). When \(q\ne1\) and \(q-1\) is a unit, a q-integer vanishes exactly when \(q^i=1\). For example at \(q=2\) modulo 7, \([3]_q=7\) vanishes; the Gaussian polynomial \(\binom32_q=1+q+q^2\) is still defined and evaluates to zero. Do not “cancel zeros” after evaluation. The optional q-Lucas/cyclotomic methods require their own hypotheses; the division-free recurrence is the complete safe contract taught here.

4. Small characteristic

Factorial-scaled EGF formulas require \(1,\ldots,N\) to be invertible. When \(N\ge p\), use base-\(p\) digit structure, recurrences that avoid division, or algorithms specifically designed for small characteristic. This affects Stirling rows, Bell numbers, FPS integration, and q-factorials—not just \({n\choose k}\).

For example, over \(\mathbb F_2\), the derivative of every polynomial has zero coefficient at \(x\): differentiating \(a_2x^2\) gives \(2a_2x=0\). Thus there is no formal antiderivative of \(x\). A failed division by 2 can signify nonexistence, not merely a missing trick for computing an otherwise unique answer. Conversely a Gaussian binomial has an integer polynomial definition and survives a failed product denominator. Determine which situation you are in before choosing a fallback.

5. Rank distributions as a counting model

How many \(a\times b\) matrices over \(\mathbb F_q\) have rank \(r\)? Their column space is an \(r\)-subspace of \(\mathbb F_q^a\), with \(\binom ar_q\) choices. For a fixed basis of that space, the \(b\) columns form an \(r\times b\) coordinate matrix of full row rank. Choose its \(r\) independent rows successively, giving

\[N_{a,b}(r)=\binom ar_q\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(q^b-q^i), \qquad 0\le r\le\min(a,b).\]

Changing the chosen basis is not another matrix choice: coordinates transform with it, so fixing one basis for each subspace counts each matrix once. For binary \(2\times2\) matrices, ranks 0,1,2 occur \(1,9,6\) times, totaling \(16=2^4\). This is the bridge from subspaces to random linear maps and XOR sequence counting. A naive rank sum/table can still be too slow; the advanced problems require compressing the products or recognizing a convolution.

Decision checklist

  • Prime modulus and \(n<p\): factorial tables.
  • Small prime and huge \(n\): Lucas; use Kummer to reason about zeros.
  • Prime power: valuation plus unit factorial.
  • General composite modulus: prime powers plus generalized CRT.
  • q-analogue: identify whether it counts subspaces, inversions, or partitions.

Before coding, write down the modulus factorization, the largest factorial argument, and every quantity you intend to invert. Those three lines usually choose the method and expose an invalid factorial-table solution immediately.

Exercises with hints and solutions

1. Concept check: a Lucas value and a carry chain

Find \(\binom{10}{3}\bmod3\) and \(v_2\binom81\) without forming factorials. Hint: write base-3 digits for the first and count every carry in the second.

NoteSolution

\(10=(101)_3\) and \(3=(010)_3\), so the middle Lucas factor is \(\binom01=0\); the result is 0, agreeing with 120. In binary, \(1+7=(001)_2+(111)_2=(1000)_2\) carries at all three low positions, giving valuation 3. Counting only original-digit violations misses the propagation through the zero digits of 8.

2. Application: assemble a composite residue

Compute \(\binom62\) modulo 72 using prime powers. Hint: \(72=8\cdot9\).

NoteSolution

Modulo 8 the worked example gives 7. Modulo 9, valuations of \(6!,2!,4!\) are \(2,0,1\), so the binomial has one factor 3. Unit factorials are \(U(6)=80\equiv8\), \(U(2)=2\), \(U(4)=8\). Their unit ratio is \(8\cdot5\cdot8\equiv5\pmod9\), giving \(3\cdot5\equiv6\). Now write \(x=7+8t\). Modulo 9, \(7-t=6\) yields \(t=1\pmod9\), hence \(x=15\pmod{72}\). The CRT moduli are coprime even though 72 is composite.

3. Advanced: subspaces avoiding a vector

In \(\mathbb F_q^n\), fix a nonzero vector \(v\). How many \(r\)-subspaces do not contain \(v\)? Hint: first count those containing its one-dimensional span.

NoteSolution

Subspaces containing \(\langle v\rangle\) correspond bijectively to \((r-1)\)-subspaces of \(V/\langle v\rangle\), so the count avoiding \(v\) is \(\binom nr_q-\binom{n-1}{r-1}_q=q^r\binom{n-1}r_q\). For the latter identity, project an avoiding subspace to the quotient: its image is an \(r\)-subspace, and its lifts are graphs of the \(q^r\) linear maps from that subspace to \(\langle v\rangle\), after fixing a complementary hyperplane. For \(q=2,n=3,r=1\), there are \(7-1=6\) lines avoiding \(v\). For \(r=0\) the zero subspace always avoids a nonzero vector.

4. Synthesis: forbid an even zero-XOR subset

Among subsets of \(\mathbb F_2^N\), count those with no nonempty even-sized subset of XOR zero. Derive a count by cardinality. Hint: append one parity bit to every chosen vector.

NoteSolution

Map \(x\) to \((x,1)\in\mathbb F_2^{N+1}\). A dependence among these vectors is exactly an even zero-XOR subset. For \(r\) previously independent augmented vectors, their span has \(2^r\) elements. If \(r\ge1\), exactly half have last coordinate one: the last-coordinate map is a nonzero linear functional, so its two fibers both have size \(2^{r-1}\). The next allowed vector therefore has \(2^N-2^{r-1}\) choices. The first has \(2^N\) choices. For cardinality \(s\ge1\) the unordered count is

\[\frac{2^N\prod_{r=1}^{s-1}(2^N-2^{r-1})}{s!}, \qquad 1\le s\le N+1.\]

Add one for the empty set. Division by \(s!\) is exact over integers; the ARC146 C modulus and indexing bounds also make it a unit. Prefix products and inverse factorials evaluate all cardinalities in \(O(N)\) field operations. For \(N=2\), the counts are \(1,4,6,4\), totaling 15; the full four-element set is the only forbidden subset of the four-vector universe.

5. Synthesis: nonzero forbidden XOR versus zero

Count ordered length-\(m\) sequences in \(\mathbb F_2^k\) with no nonempty subsequence XOR equal to a specified \(x\). Derive a rank-sum expression for \(x\ne0\) and a separate formula for \(x=0\). Hint: use the preceding avoiding subspace count and full-row-rank coordinate matrices.

NoteSolution

For \(x\ne0\), the condition is \(x\) outside the span. The exact count is

\[\sum_{r=0}^{\min(m,k-1)}2^r\binom{k-1}r_2 \prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^m-2^i).\]

Each term chooses an avoiding \(r\)-subspace and a sequence spanning it. For \(x=0\), the empty subsequence is excluded, so the vectors must be independent: the count is \(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^k-2^i)\) when \(m\le k\), otherwise zero. Treating zero as an ordinary forbidden span vector would wrongly return zero for every instance. For \(k=1,x=1\), only the all-zero sequence survives; for \(k=1,x=0\), only the length-one sequence (1) survives among positive lengths. These formulas are the mathematical reduction, not a complete multi-query algorithm for CF1603 F’s largest bounds: its product/rank-sum evaluations need batch preprocessing and further algebraic simplification. Use the official contest tutorial linked on its statement for that optimization.

Summary and reference sheet

Setting Formula or method Hypotheses and resource
Binomial modulo \(p\) Lucas digit product Prime; \(O(p)\) table or explicit digit-query cost
Binomial valuation Carry count / factorial valuations \(0\le k\le n\); count propagated carries
Modulo \(p^e\) \(p^v\) times unit-factorial ratio Only units inverted; full-period table \(O(p^e)\)
General modulus Prime powers then CRT Factorization available; local moduli coprime
Subspace count Gaussian binomial Field size a prime power; polynomial evaluation separate
q-binomial at singular products Division-free recurrence Any commutative coefficient ring; \(O(nr)\)
Rank-\(r\) matrices \(\binom ar_q\prod_{i<r}(q^b-q^i)\) \(0\le r\le\min(a,b)\)

The final sanity check is semantic: are you counting ordered bases, subspaces, ordered sequences, or unordered sets? Each requires a different symmetry factor, and that factor’s modular inverse may not exist.
