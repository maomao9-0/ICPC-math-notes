06 — Fast Polynomial Algorithms

A polynomial is a finite coefficient array, but it is useful to think of it as a compact description of size-indexed choices: \([x^k]A(x)\) is the answer for total size \(k\). This chapter explains why the fast operations work and when their algebraic preconditions hold. It assumes your library already supplies tested convolution and basic polynomial operations.

1. Multiplication is adding independent sizes

For \(C(x)=A(x)B(x)\),

\[c_k=\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j. \tag{1}\]

Each summand chooses an \(i\)-sized object from \(A\) and a \((k-i)\)-sized object from \(B\). This “split a total” meaning is why convolution occurs in counting, DP merging, and formal power series.

The transform idea is simple: evaluate polynomials at a specially chosen set of points. At every point \(z\), \((AB)(z)=A(z)B(z)\), so multiplication becomes pointwise. An FFT uses complex roots of unity; an NTT uses roots of unity in a finite field. Interpolation returns to coefficient form.

Transform contract

For an NTT of length \(L=2^s\) modulo prime \(p\), we need \(L\mid p-1\) so the field has a primitive \(L\)th root of unity. The result length must cover every possible degree, so \(L\ge |A|+|B|-1\). For example, \(998244353=119\cdot2^{23}+1\) supports powers of two through \(2^{23}\).

If the answer is modulo an arbitrary \(m\), do not pretend one NTT prime computes it. Convolve under one or more NTT-friendly primes, reconstruct the integer coefficient by CRT (or use Garner to perform the same reconstruction directly modulo \(m\) when the bounded coefficients are nonnegative), then reduce. Garner avoids storing a huge reconstructed integer; it does not remove the reconstruction-range requirement. For signed coefficients, centered CRT must also determine whether the representative means \(c\) or \(c-P\), where \(P\) is the product of auxiliary primes; a direct Garner residue needs the corresponding sign correction modulo \(m\). The number of primes is determined by a proved bound on the integer coefficients: for a signed coefficient of absolute value at most \(B\), centered CRT needs a product of moduli greater than \(2B\). Three primes are a convention, not a proof. Complex FFT instead needs coefficient splitting and a rounding-error bound.

2. Division: turn the high-degree end into a series inverse

Given \(A=BQ+R\) with \(\deg R<\deg B\), ordinary long division removes the highest remaining term one at a time. Reversal makes those highest terms become the low-degree terms of a formal power series, where inversion is fast. If \(\deg A<\deg B\), there is nothing to divide: return \(Q=0,R=A\).

Let \(k=\deg A-\deg B+1\). Define \(\operatorname{rev}_k(F)\) by reading from \(F\)’s actual leading coefficient downward, retaining \(k\) coefficients and padding with zeros only after its constant term. Then

\[ \operatorname{rev}_k(Q) \equiv \operatorname{rev}_k(A)\operatorname{rev}_k(B)^{-1}\pmod{x^k}. \tag{2} \]

Why is this legal? The leading coefficient of \(B\) becomes the constant coefficient of \(\operatorname{rev}_k(B)\); it can be inverted precisely when the leading coefficient is a unit (equivalently nonzero over a field). Terms from \(R\) land beyond the first \(k\) reversed coefficients and disappear modulo \(x^k\). Reverse the quotient back, then compute \(R=A-BQ\).

This costs \(O(M(n))\) with fast multiplication. Over a general ring it requires the leading coefficient of \(B\) to be a unit. In particular, modulo a composite number a nonzero leading coefficient might not be invertible, so this division contract can fail.

3. Product trees and multipoint evaluation

To evaluate \(f\) at points \(x_0,\ldots,x_{m-1}\), build a balanced tree. A leaf stores \(x-x_i\); an internal node stores the product of its children:

\[P_v(x)=\prod_{i\text{ below }v}(x-x_i).\]

At the root, replace \(f\) by its remainder modulo \(P_{root}\). A polynomial remainder modulo \(x-x_i\) is its value at \(x_i\), so recursively passing the remainder modulo each child reaches \(f(x_i)\) at every leaf. This is the same divide-and-conquer idea as a segment tree: a node stores the polynomial that vanishes at all points in its interval.

If \(\deg f=O(m)\), fast division at each level costs \(O(M(m)\log m)\) in the balanced case. For a higher-degree \(f\), first reducing it modulo \(P_{root}\) adds the cost of that division. For a small number of points, Horner’s rule is simpler and often faster; use a product tree only when the asymptotic saving matters.

4. Interpolation reverses evaluation

Now suppose the points \(x_i\) are pairwise distinct in the coefficient field and the desired values \(y_i\) are known. Let

\[P(x)=\prod_i(x-x_i).\]

The polynomial \(P(x)/(x-x_i)\) vanishes at every \(x_j\) except \(x_i\). Dividing it by its value there, \(P'(x_i)\), creates a basis polynomial that is \(1\) at \(x_i\) and \(0\) at every other sample. Therefore

\[f(x)=\sum_i y_i\frac{P(x)}{(x-x_i)P'(x_i)}. \tag{3}\]

This formula is not magic: each summand is responsible for exactly one sample. Evaluate \(P'\) at all points via the product tree, place \(w_i=y_i/P'(x_i)\) at leaves, and merge children as

\[F_v=F_LP_R+F_RP_L.\]

At the root this is (3), in \(O(M(n)\log n)\). If points repeat, \(P'(x_i)=0\) and ordinary interpolation is undefined; repeated-value data needs the stronger Hermite-interpolation problem.

For values at the \(n+1\) consecutive points \(0,\ldots,n\), the denominators simplify to \(i!(-1)^{n-i}(n-i)!\). Prefix and suffix products of \((t-j)\) evaluate (3) at one arbitrary \(t\) in \(O(n)\). Factorials must be invertible, so normally \(n<MOD\).

5. Taylor shift: substitute \(x+c\) in one convolution

We want the coefficients of \(f(x+c)\), not just its value at one point. Expanding each monomial gives

\[[x^k]f(x+c)=\sum_{i\ge k}a_i\binom ikc^{i-k}. \tag{4}\]

This says degree \(i\) contributes to every lower degree \(k\). Expand the binomial coefficient and reverse the \(a_i i!\) array; (4) becomes one convolution with the array \(c^j/j!\), followed by division by \(k!\). If \(n=\deg f\), put \(u_{n-i}=a_i i!\) and \(v_j=c^j/j!\). Then

\[\frac{(u*v)_{n-k}}{k!}= \sum_{i\ge k}a_i\binom ikc^{i-k},\]

so \((u*v)_{n-k}\) is \(k!\) times the shifted coefficient of \(x^k\); the final division by \(k!\) is essential. For example, if \(f(x)=x^2\) and \(c=1\), then the convolution entries at indices \(2,1,0\) are \(1,2,2\), and division by \(0!,1!,2!\) gives the coefficients \(1,2,1\) of \((x+1)^2\). The reversal lines up \(i-k\) as a convolution index. As with binomial transforms, this needs the factorials through the working degree to be units; it is normally used over a prime modulus larger than that degree.

6. Many factors: keep merge sizes balanced

For \(\prod_i A_i(x)\), multiplying factors one after another repeatedly makes a large polynomial absorb a tiny one. A divide-and-conquer product tree, or a priority queue that merges the two smallest degrees, keeps products balanced. For total output degree \(N\), this is typically \(O(M(N)\log k)\).

If the question only asks through degree \(D\), truncate after each merge. In a knapsack-style product such as \(\prod_i(1+x^{w_i})\), coefficients above \(D\) can never influence lower coefficients later, so retaining them only wastes time and memory.

7. Failure checklist and sources

  • Before an NTT, verify both the required length and the root-of-unity condition.

  • Before a polynomial inverse, verify a nonzero/invertible constant term; before division, verify the leading coefficient condition instead.

  • Normalize negative coefficients in the target ring.

  • For CRT convolution, prove the reconstruction range covers a coefficient.

  • Interpolation requires distinct sample points; consecutive-point formulas additionally need nonzero factorial denominators.

  • Check a small product, quotient/remainder identity, and interpolation samples independently of the fast routine.
