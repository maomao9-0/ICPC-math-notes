15 — Mixed Mathematics Capstones

The chapter with no technique

Every chapter so far handed you the topic in its title. That is the one luxury a real contest never gives. A World Finals statement is about tiles, tokens, or roads; it never says “use a formal logarithm here” or “invert this over the divisor poset”. By the time you recognize the technique, the hard part is already done.

So this chapter contains no new mathematics. It trains the missing step: turning a story into a precise mathematical object, and continuing to sharpen that object until one of the earlier chapters becomes unavoidable. “Sum a function over all pairs” becomes a written sum over \((i,j)\) — and only then does grouping by \(\gcd(i,j)\) suggest Möbius inversion. “Count drawings up to relabelling” becomes a named group acting on a named set — and only then does Burnside apply. That intermediate model is the thing worth practising.

A capstone attempt should therefore begin without opening the topic tags in this repository, and the model should be written down and argued over as a team before anyone starts coding.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Complete the relevant branches of the learning path: modular algebra, divisor identities, symmetry, polynomials, FPS, transforms, and discrete sums. This is a synthesis workshop, so its structure is a sequence of attempts and editorial arguments. The objective is to produce a model, a complete reduction, a complexity bound, and an independent small oracle without being told the technique first. Existing chapter problems are intentionally revisited.

For an untagged attempt, use only this statement list and stop reading the chapter until your scheduled hint time: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. The circuit below is instructor guidance and deliberately exposes prerequisites.

Recognition protocol

Before coding, write answers to these questions. A good answer is a sentence or equation which could be checked on a three-element example, not a technique name such as “probably NTT.”

  1. What is the mathematical object: residue class, multiplicative function, polynomial/FPS, labelled class, group action, linear operator, random variable, or lattice sum?
  2. Which operation is expensive: pairwise product, convolution, coefficient extraction, repeated transition, divisor sum, or enumeration of symmetric copies?
  3. Which change of basis makes that operation pointwise or triangular?
  4. What is the coefficient ring, and which divisions/roots actually exist?
  5. What brute-force oracle can test the first 20–100 cases?

The ordered set below revisits a few chapter problems in a deliberately tag-free setting and adds one synthesis problem. Treat each as a 3–5 hour team session, not a normal single-person rating drill. Before reading a hint, insist on a proposed invariant: “this table stores …,” “this coefficient counts …,” or “these two configurations are equivalent because ….”

Timed-attempt rules

  • Do not inspect tags, hints, or editorials for the first 90 minutes.
  • At 90 minutes, compare the three best models you considered. For each, say exactly which object it counts and why it is insufficient or promising. Open only Hint 1 if none gives the target complexity.
  • At 150 minutes, open Hint 2 and require a written derivation before coding.
  • At 210 minutes, open Hint 3. Stop after five hours and study the official analysis; continuing an unproductive implementation is not endurance training.
  • Re-solve from a blank file six weeks later. A remembered code template does not count unless you can re-derive its contract.

Postmortem rubric

Score each dimension from 0 to 2:

Dimension 0 1 2
Model No useful object Right family after hint Named it from statement clues
Derivation Copied Reconstructed with gaps Complete and checked
Complexity No viable bound Correct after prompting Targeted before implementation
Contract Missed ring/precondition Fixed during debugging Stated before coding
Verification Samples only Hand tests Independent brute-force/random oracle

A score of 8/10 on the delayed re-solve is the mastery gate. A lower score does not mean “memorize the solution”; return to the chapter that supplied the first missing derivation and redo a tiny example by hand.

Worked miniature: models before machinery

Count triples of nonnegative integers \((x,y,z)\) with \(x+y+z=N\) and \(x\) even. For \(N=3\), the possible \(x\) values 0 and 2 contribute 4 and 2 pairs, giving 6. A direct model is \(\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}(N-2j+1)\). Writing \(h=\lfloor N/2\rfloor\), this equals \((h+1)(N+1-h)\) in \(O(1)\) integer arithmetic. A second model assigns weights \(t^{2j}\) to \(x\) and \(t^y,t^z\) to the others, obtaining \([t^N](1-t^2)^{-1}(1-t)^{-2}\). Both count the same objects, but the closed sum is cheaper for one query. Dividing the unrestricted count \(\binom{N+2}{2}\) by two fails at \(N=3\): it gives 5 instead of 6 because even and odd values of \(x\) do not have equal numbers of completions. Technique recognition includes deciding when an advanced tool is unnecessary.

Capstone circuit

1. AtCoder AGC 047 C — Product Modulo (Mastery)

  • Statement · official editorial · English/Japanese
  • Prerequisites: cyclic multiplicative groups, primitive roots, convolution.
  • Why here: the hard step is recognizing that a group isomorphism turns a distribution of products into additive convolution.
Hint 1 — recognition Pairs involving zero contribute zero; after separating them, nonzero residues modulo the fixed prime form a cyclic group.
Hint 2 — reduction Map each nonzero value to its discrete-log index under a primitive root; multiplication becomes cyclic addition of indices.
Hint 3 — algorithm Convolve the frequency array and fold indices modulo \(p-1\). Convert each exponent back to its residue, then correct ordered-pair and diagonal contributions to obtain the required unordered-pair sum.
NoteEditorial guidance: exact convolution and pair correction

Let \(g\) generate the nonzero residues modulo the statement’s prime \(p\). Build \(f_e=\#\{i:A_i=g^e\bmod p\}\) for \(0\le e<p-1\), excluding zeros. The ordinary integer convolution \(h=f*f\) counts ordered index pairs by exponent sum. Therefore the answer is

\[\frac12\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2p-4}h_j(g^{j\bmod(p-1)}\bmod p) -\sum_i(A_i^2\bmod p)\right).\]

Every diagonal occurs once in the first sum; every pair of different indices occurs twice. Division by 2 is exact integer division: the final sum is not modulo \(p\). Coefficients can be as large as \(n^2\), so one convolution modulus need not recover them. Use an exact integer-convolution contract or sufficient CRT range and width for the final weighted sum. Time is \(O(p\log p+n)\) with a bounded number of transform primes, space \(O(p+n)\) or \(O(p)\) while streaming the diagonal sum. For the toy prime 7 and values \(2,3,0\), the only nonzero unordered product is 6. Reducing the final sum modulo the input prime or forgetting diagonal subtraction solves a different problem.

2. Codeforces 338D — GCD Table (Capstone)

  • Statement · official analysis · English/Russian
  • Prerequisites: LCM bounds, linear congruences, generalized CRT.
  • Why here: a local gcd pattern must first be converted into compatible global row/column congruences, then checked against construction bounds.
Hint 1 Choose a row divisible by every requested entry.
Hint 2 Each desired gcd imposes a congruence on the starting column modulo that entry.
Hint 3 Merge non-coprime congruences, then verify every gcd explicitly; CRT consistency alone is not the full proof.
NoteEditorial guidance: why the smallest row suffices

Index the desired entries by \(0\le t<k\) and set \(L=\operatorname{lcm}_t a_t\). Any feasible row \(r\) is divisible by \(L\), so reject when \(L>n\), stopping LCM multiplication before overflow. If \(r,j\) works, then \(a_t\mid L\) and \(L\mid r\) imply \(a_t\mid\gcd(L,j+t)\mid\gcd(r,j+t)=a_t\). Thus row \(L\) works at the same column.

The necessary congruences are \(j\equiv-t\pmod{a_t}\). Merge them by generalized CRT. The merged modulus is \(L\); choose its least positive representative \(j\) (residue zero means \(j=L\)). All larger representatives have the same gcds with \(L\) and worse width bounds. Check \(j+k-1\le m\) and every exact gcd; these checks are sufficient by construction. Compatible congruences alone can fail: desired entries \((2,1,3)\) yield \(L=6,j=4\), but the third gcd is \(\gcd(6,6)=6\), not 3. This also explains why choosing a larger multiple of \(L\) cannot repair it. Cost is \(O(k\log L)\) arithmetic steps with bounded intermediate integers, excluding bit-complexity factors.

3. Codeforces 1097F — Alex and a TV Show (Mastery)

  • Statement · official Hello 2019 editorial · English/Russian
  • Prerequisites: divisor zeta/Möbius transforms and arithmetic over \(\mathbb F_2\).
  • Why here: it combines a non-obvious coordinate system with a representation chosen specifically for a million online operations.
Hint 1 Query parity suggests storing every multiset in a vector space over \(\mathbb F_2\).
Hint 2 Find a transform in which multiset union is addition and the pairwise-GCD product is pointwise multiplication.
Hint 3 Use divisor-incidence vectors packed into bitsets; derive the inverse coefficient needed to recover one exact value.
NoteEditorial guidance: transformed online multisets

Let \(M\) bound all values and \(c(v)\) be multiplicity parity. Store \(z(d)=\sum_{d\mid v}c(v)\) in \(\mathbb F_2\), the parity of divisible entries. Union becomes coordinatewise addition, hence bitwise XOR. For a pairwise-gcd product, \(d\mid\gcd(a,b)\) precisely when \(d\) divides both arguments, so its \(z(d)\) is the product of the two \(z(d)\) values, hence bitwise AND. A singleton \(v\) initializes bits at the divisors of \(v\).

Möbius inversion recovers one value: \(c(v)=\sum_{k\le M/v}\mu(k)z(kv)\) modulo 2. Thus precompute a bit mask containing \(kv\) for squarefree \(k\); query the parity of the popcount after AND with that mask. Each operation costs \(O(M/w)\) machine words in a dense bitset implementation, with \(O((n+M)M/w)\) words for mutable states and precomputed masks; \(w\) is the word width. All destination assignments must read the old source states, including aliasing inputs. For \(\{2,3\}\), divisible parities at 1,2,3 are \(0,1,1\); inversion gives \(c(1)=0+1+1=0\), illustrating why the first transformed bit is total parity, not multiplicity of 1.

4. Codeforces 438E — The Child and Binary Tree (Capstone)

  • Statement · GF/FPS derivation · English
  • Prerequisites: combinatorial decomposition, NTT, FPS inverse and square root.
  • Why here: the combinatorial equation is short; recognizing and safely computing its algebraic solution is the real test.
Hint 1 Write a generating function for the allowed root weights and decompose a tree at its root.
Hint 2 The two ordered child slots produce a quadratic functional equation.
Hint 3 Select the formal root with the correct constant term and evaluate it using FPS square root and inverse, truncated after every operation.
NoteEditorial guidance: the correct formal root

Let \(C(x)=\sum_{c\text{ allowed}}x^c\) and let \(T(x)\) count nonempty trees. Positive vertex weights give \(C(0)=T(0)=0\). Each root selects its weight and two ordered, possibly empty, child slots, giving \(T=C(1+T)^2\). Set \(F=1+T\). Then \(F=1+CF^2\) and rationalizing its quadratic solution gives

\[F=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1-4C}},\qquad \sqrt{1-4C}(0)=1.\]

The other square-root branch has the wrong constant term. This form avoids dividing by \(C\), a series with zero constant term. Over the problem’s field, 2 is invertible and the square root with constant 1 is uniquely liftable; the denominator has constant 2. Truncate modulo \(x^{m+1}\), then output positive coefficients of \(F\). With quasi-linear polynomial multiplication, Newton square root and inverse take \(O(\mathcal M(m))\) under the usual regularity assumption, or the safe \(O(\mathcal M(m)\log m)\) generic bound. For allowed weights 1 and 2, expanding the combinatorial equation gives \(T=x+3x^2+9x^3+\cdots\), an independent check before invoking FPS machinery.

5. Codeforces 995F — Cowmpany Cowmpensation (Capstone)

  • Statement · official tutorial · English/Russian
  • Prerequisites: tree counting, polynomial-valued DP, finite differences or interpolation.
  • Why here: it requires proving that a huge-domain answer is a low-degree polynomial before any fast evaluation is possible.
Hint 1 Parameterize a subtree by the largest label it may use.
Hint 2 Children multiply; adding the parent takes a prefix sum, so track how the polynomial degree changes.
Hint 3 Compute enough consecutive values and evaluate the resulting bounded-degree polynomial at the large argument.
NoteEditorial guidance: subtree degree is the resource

Let \(F_v(d)\) count legal salaries in \(v\)’s subtree, all at most \(d\). If \(v\) has salary \(t\), every child subtree has the bound \(t\). Therefore

\[F_v(d)=\sum_{t=1}^d\prod_{u\text{ child of }v}F_u(t),\quad F_v(0)=0.\]

A leaf has \(F_v(d)=d\). Inductively, the product degree is at most the sum of child subtree sizes, and finite summation raises it by one; hence degree at most \(|\text{subtree}(v)|\). Compute values for all \(d=0,\ldots,n\) in a postorder traversal, using pointwise products then prefix sums. This costs \(O(n^2)\) field operations and \(O(n^2)\) straightforward storage. Interpolate the root at \(D\) in \(O(n)\) with consecutive samples. The problem’s prime is greater than \(n\), so the factorial denominators are units. A root with two leaf children has \(F(d)=\sum_{t=1}^d t^2\), giving 5 at \(d=2\). The huge salary bound is a polynomial argument, not a DP dimension.

6. AtCoder ABC 284 Ex — Count Unlabeled Graphs (Capstone)

  • Statement · official editorial, JA
  • Prerequisites: Burnside, conjugacy classes of \(S_n\), integer partitions, induced permutation actions, surjective colorings.
  • Why here: applying Burnside is easy; compressing \(n!\) group elements and counting both vertex-color and edge orbits is the mastery step.
Hint 1 Model an object as a surjective \(K\)-coloring of labelled vertices together with an edge subset, acted on by relabeling vertices.
Hint 2 Group vertex permutations by cycle type rather than enumerating them.
Hint 3 For each vertex cycle type, fixed colorings are constant on vertex cycles and invariant edge sets are constant on pair cycles. Enforce that every color appears before averaging.
NoteEditorial guidance: two induced orbit counts

For a permutation with cycle lengths \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_c\), a fixed coloring assigns one of the \(K\) distinguished colors to each cycle and uses all colors. Its count is \(U(c,K)=\sum_{j=0}^K(-1)^j\binom Kj(K-j)^c\): each summand excludes a chosen set of missing colors, and a coloring missing \(r>0\) colors has total weight \((1-1)^r=0\). The convention \(0^0=1\) handles empty index sets.

For edges between two vertex cycles of lengths \(a,b\), a pair returns after \(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\) steps, giving \(ab/\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\gcd(a,b)\) orbits. Within a length-\(a\) cycle, circular distances \(1,\ldots,\lfloor a/2\rfloor\) give that many orbits. Thus

\[e(\lambda)=\sum_i\lfloor\lambda_i/2\rfloor +\sum_{i<j}\gcd(\lambda_i,\lambda_j).\]

Each edge orbit is either wholly absent or present, giving \(2^{e(\lambda)}\). If \(m_j\) cycles have length \(j\), define \(z_\lambda=\prod_j j^{m_j}m_j!\). Burnside grouped by cycle type yields \(\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}U(c,K)2^{e(\lambda)}/z_\lambda\). The answer prime exceeds \(n\), licensing all denominators. Enumerating partitions and evaluating pair gcds costs \(O(p(n)n^2+nK)\) with surjection counts precomputed by their Stirling recurrence; \(p(n)\) is the partition count. For \(n=3,K=1\), types \(1+1+1,2+1,3\) contribute \(8/6,4/2,2/3\), totaling 4. For \(K=2\) the same sum is 12. Dividing labelled graphs by \(n!\) directly would ignore nontrivial stabilizers.

7. Codeforces 914G — Sum the Fibonacci (Capstone)

  • Statement · official tutorial · English/Russian
  • Prerequisites: ranked subset convolution and AND/OR/XOR transforms.
  • Why here: the expression must be separated into several distributions, each diagonalized by a different transform, then recombined without losing ordered multiplicities.
Hint 1 Replace indices by a frequency distribution over their bitmask values.
Hint 2 Construct the OR-with-disjointness, singleton, and XOR distributions separately.
Hint 3 Apply the appropriate transform to each stage, include the Fibonacci weights at the right time, and audit whether tuples are ordered.
NoteEditorial guidance: three weighted distributions

Let \(f[s]\) count input occurrences on \(B\) bits. Build \(A[u]=\sum_{x\mathbin{\vert}y=u,\ x\mathbin{\&}y=0}f[x]f[y]\) by ranked subset convolution, \(C[v]=f[v]\), and \(D[w]=\sum_{x\mathbin{\oplus}y=w}f[x]f[y]\) by XOR convolution. These describe the first pair, middle index, and final pair of the five-tuple. Indices may coincide: the statement imposes no distinctness correction.

Multiply each distribution at mask \(s\) by the ordinary Fibonacci number \(F_s\) modulo the answer prime. Now combine the three arrays by AND convolution, using superset zeta transforms, pointwise triple multiplication, and inverse superset transform. If the result is \(H\), answer \(\sum_{i=0}^{B-1}H[2^i]\): these and only these masks have one set bit. Fibonacci weights belong before the AND convolution because they depend on three separate masks, not only their final intersection. Complexity is \(O(B^2 2^B+n)\) time and \(O(B2^B)\) memory; the answer modulus is odd, so the XOR transform length \(2^B\) is a unit. For inputs 1 and 2, there are two disjoint ordered first pairs (OR 3), two unequal final pairs (XOR 3), and two middle choices, each contributing \(F_3^2F_1=4\) or \(F_3^2F_2=4\): total 32. Filtering repeated indices would erase valid terms.

8. Luogu P3768 — A Simple Math Problem (Capstone)

  • Statement, ZH · solution index, ZH, login required
  • Prerequisites: gcd extraction, totient identities, weighted summatory functions, Du Jiao recursion, quotient blocks.
  • Why here: several familiar identities must be composed before the desired summatory function even becomes visible.
Hint 1 Extract the gcd in the double sum and rewrite the coprime part with a totient identity.
Hint 2 Identify the weighted totient prefix that the quotient blocks require.
Hint 3 Derive a convolution recurrence for that prefix and evaluate polynomial block sums with explicitly licensed denominators.
NoteEditorial guidance: weighted totient summation

Use \(g=\sum_{d\mid g}\varphi(d)\) inside the desired double sum. Interchange finite sums and write multiples as \(i=da,j=db\):

\[\sum_{i,j\le n}ij\gcd(i,j) =\sum_{d\le n}d^2\varphi(d) \left(\sum_{a\le\lfloor n/d\rfloor}a\right)^2.\]

Thus quotient blocks need \(S(x)=\sum_{d\le x}d^2\varphi(d)\). Let \(f(d)=d^2\varphi(d)\) and \(g(d)=d^2\). Their Dirichlet convolution is \((f*g)(m)=m^2\sum_{d\mid m}\varphi(d)=m^3\). Summing through \(x\) gives

\[S(x)=\left(\frac{x(x+1)}2\right)^2 -\sum_{l=2}^x l^2 S(\lfloor x/l\rfloor).\]

Group equal quotients and use exact square-sum differences for each block’s coefficient. Precompute \(S\) through a cutoff \(T\), memoize larger quotient arguments, and use the summatory-sieve chapter’s bound \(O(T+n/\sqrt T)\) arithmetic operations for \(T\ge\sqrt n\), giving \(O(n^{2/3})\) at \(T\asymp n^{2/3}\); memory includes \(O(T)\) prefix storage. The outer quotient sum costs \(O(\sqrt n)\) lookups after recursion. Here the official statement specifies a prime greater than \(5\cdot10^8\), so 2 and 6 are units; in a generalized composite-modulus version, cancel integer factors before reduction. At \(n=2\), direct terms are \(1,2,2,8\), totaling 13; the transformed terms are \(1\cdot3^2+4\cdot1^2=13\).

Original synthesis exercises

A. Reuse the model, change the observable

In Problem 1’s setting, find the number of unordered index pairs whose product residue belongs to a given set \(R\), rather than their sum of residues. Hint: change the final weight, not the convolution.

NoteSolution

Use the same exponent-pair distribution, weight exponent \(j\) by \([g^{j\bmod(p-1)}\in R]\), subtract diagonal indicators, and divide by two. If \(0\in R\), add pairs with at least one zero: for \(z\) zero inputs this is \(z(n-z)+\binom z2\). Separating zero was valid for a residue sum because its weight was zero; it is not automatically valid for a new observable.

B. A weighted root breaks the guessed degree

Change the salary count so that each assignment is weighted by the product of all salaries. What degree bound replaces subtree size? Hint: conditioning on the root salary contributes an additional factor of \(t\).

NoteSolution

The recurrence becomes \(G_v(d)=\sum_{t=1}^d t\prod_uG_u(t)\). A leaf has degree 2; inductively, multiplication, the root factor, and summation give degree at most \(2|\text{subtree}(v)|\). Use \(2n+1\) samples and require the corresponding factorial denominators to be units. Reusing the unweighted \(n+1\) samples would silently interpolate the wrong polynomial.

Revision checklist

The invariant to retain from each capstone is respectively: ordered exponent pair counts; the minimal LCM row; divisible multiplicity parity; a root with two ordered slots; subtree degree; vertex and pair orbits; independently weighted mask distributions; a weighted divisor convolution. Before coding, write the invariant in full and specify the coefficient domain, index bounds, symmetry factor, and small oracle. A method name alone omits the proof.

Completion criterion

Finish at least six of the eight, including one of Problems 6–8, under the timed rules. For the remaining problems, a complete postmortem after studying the editorial is preferable to copying an implementation. Then run one five-hour virtual ICPC or Universal Cup contest and classify every unsolved mathematics problem against the course dependency map.
