01 — Modular Algebra and Finite Fields

This chapter turns congruences into algorithms. The recurring contest signal is that values are huge but only their residue, multiplicative order, or compatibility across several moduli matters. Work through the sections in order; the final four topics assume factorization from Chapter 02.

1. Congruences as algebra

Write a ≡ b (mod m) when m | (a-b). Addition and multiplication descend to residue classes, but division is conditional: a has an inverse modulo m exactly when gcd(a,m)=1. Extended Euclid gives integers x,y with

\[ax+my=\gcd(a,m).\]

Thus, for g=gcd(a,m), the linear congruence ax ≡ b (mod m) has no solution if g∤b; otherwise divide by g, invert a/g modulo m/g, and obtain one residue class modulo m/g (hence g solutions modulo m).

Recognition. A repeated step on a cycle, synchronized clocks, or an equation with a coefficient that may not be invertible is a linear congruence, not “ordinary modular division.”

2. CRT, including non-coprime moduli

To merge x≡a (mod m) and x≡b (mod n), substitute x=a+mt. Then

\[mt\equiv b-a\pmod n.\]

It is solvable iff gcd(m,n) | b-a; all solutions form one class modulo lcm(m,n). Fold constraints one at a time, normalize each merged residue, and guard the LCM against overflow.

For pairwise coprime prime powers, CRT is a ring isomorphism Z/(∏p_i^{e_i})Z ≅ ∏ Z/(p_i^{e_i})Z. This is why composite-modulus equations should often be solved locally and recombined.

3. Finite fields and multiplicative order

For prime p, every nonzero residue is invertible: F_p^× is cyclic of order p-1. Consequently:

  • Fermat: a^(p-1)=1 for a≠0.
  • ord_p(a) | p-1.
  • A polynomial of degree d over a field has at most d roots unless it is zero.
  • x^k=a can be converted into a linear congruence in exponents after choosing a primitive root.

For composite moduli, nonzero does not imply invertible, polynomial root bounds may fail, and the unit group need not be cyclic. Always identify where primality is used.

The order of a modulo m, assuming gcd(a,m)=1, starts at φ(m) (or the Carmichael exponent λ(m)) and removes prime factors q whenever a^(ord/q)=1. A primitive root has order φ(m); primitive roots exist exactly for m∈{1,2,4,p^k,2p^k} with odd prime p.

4. Valuations and lifting

The p-adic valuation v_p(n) is the exponent of p in n. It linearizes products:

\[v_p(ab)=v_p(a)+v_p(b),\qquad v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\left\lfloor n/p^j\right\rfloor.\]

For sums, v_p(a+b)≥min(v_p(a),v_p(b)), with equality if the two valuations differ. LTE supplies sharper forms. The most used one is: for odd prime p, p|(x-y), and p∤xy,

\[v_p(x^n-y^n)=v_p(x-y)+v_p(n).\]

Use valuations when divisibility by a huge power or the number of trailing prime factors is asked. For binomial coefficients,

\[v_p\binom nk=\sum_{j\ge1}\left(\lfloor n/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor k/p^j\rfloor-\lfloor(n-k)/p^j\rfloor\right),\]

equivalently the number of carries when adding k and n-k in base p (Kummer).

Hensel lifting upgrades a root modulo p^k to modulo p^(k+1). For f(r)=0 mod p^k, try r'=r+t p^k; after linearization solve f(r)/p^k + t f'(r) ≡ 0 (mod p). If f'(r)≠0 mod p, the lift is unique. If the derivative vanishes, there may be zero or p lifts—never assume uniqueness.

5. Discrete logarithms

Given a^x≡b (mod m), baby-step giant-step (BSGS) meets in the middle. With n=ceil(sqrt(m)), store baby steps a^q, then scan b(a^{-n})^j; a collision gives x=q+nj. This field/group version needs gcd(a,m)=1 and costs O(sqrt(m)) time and memory.

When gcd(a,m)>1, extended BSGS repeatedly strips g=gcd(a,m). At each stripping step, require g|b, divide m,b by g, and maintain the accumulated multiplier; check small exponents along the way. This is subtle enough that a known-tested template is preferable in a contest.

Pohlig–Hellman is better when the group order is smooth: solve the logarithm modulo each prime power dividing the order, then CRT the exponent. Its hard part is the largest prime factor, roughly O(√q) group operations.

6. Modular roots

For odd prime p, quadratic residues satisfy Euler’s criterion a^((p-1)/2)∈{±1}. Tonelli–Shanks writes p-1=q·2^s with odd q, finds a non-residue, then cancels the current error inside the 2-power subgroup. It runs in O(log p + s^2) modular multiplications.

For general x^k=a (mod p), write nonzero values as powers of a primitive root: g^(kt)=a. A discrete logarithm converts this to kt≡log_g(a) (mod p-1). It has gcd(k,p-1) solutions if compatible. Specialized root algorithms avoid a full discrete log; use them when constraints exceed BSGS.

7. Exponent towers and efficient k-th roots

Euler reduction a^e mod m = a^(e mod phi(m)) mod m is valid without qualification only when gcd(a,m)=1. For a tower, recurse through the Carmichael chain m, lambda(m), lambda(lambda(m)), ... and carry an additional boolean saying whether the true exponent has reached the reduction threshold. For a prime power dividing the base, compare the tower with the valuation threshold; for the unit part, reduce by the local Carmichael exponent, then CRT.

For x^k=a mod p, the discrete-log reduction in Section 6 is conceptual but may be too slow. Fast algorithms split p-1 into its gcd(k,p-1) part, invert the coprime exponent part, and extract roots inside the remaining smooth subgroup. Always return and verify all roots or the required canonical root; existence is equivalent to a^((p-1)/g)=1, where g=gcd(k,p-1) for a!=0.

Contest checklist and pitfalls

  • Normalize negative residues; C++ % may be negative.
  • Never use Fermat inversion unless the modulus is prime and the denominator nonzero.
  • Protect multiplication and LCM with __int128; check promised bounds before casting back.
  • CRT consistency is a divisibility test, not pairwise coprimality.
  • Distinguish a primitive root of the whole multiplicative group from an nth root of unity.
  • In BSGS, clarify whether the smallest solution is required and handle x=0, a=0, and non-coprime cases.
  • Hensel’s derivative-zero branch and p=2 require separate analysis.

Ordered practice

Levels are curriculum-relative: Foundation, Advanced, Mastery, Capstone. Try each problem before opening its hints.

1. Throne — AtCoder ABC186 E

  • Level: Foundation · Native: 500 points · Language: English/Japanese
  • Trains: linear congruences, solvability, extended Euclid.
  • Editorial: official contest editorial
Hint 1 — recognition After x moves the position condition is one congruence in x.
Hint 2 — reduction Solve Kx ≡ -S (mod N) and test the gcd condition.
Hint 3 — algorithm Divide all three quantities by gcd(K,N), then use an extended-Euclid inverse.

2. Power Pair — AtCoder ABC212 G

  • Level: Advanced · Native: 600 points · Language: English/Japanese
  • Prerequisites: cyclic F_p^×, Euler φ, divisor enumeration.
  • Editorial: official contest editorial
Hint 1 Classify each nonzero x by its multiplicative order.
Hint 2 An element of order d reaches exactly the subgroup it generates; count elements of each order.
Hint 3 Sum a divisor expression involving φ(d) over d|(P-1) and remember (0,0).

3. Moodular Arithmetic — Codeforces 603B

  • Level: Mastery · Native: 1800 · Language: English/Russian
  • Trains: functional graphs induced by multiplication and multiplicative order.
  • Editorial: official Round 334 tutorial
Hint 1 The equation links values of the function along orbits of x→kx.
Hint 2 Handle k=0,1 separately; otherwise the nonzero residues split into equal cycles.
Hint 3 The cycle length is ord_p(k); count free choices per orbit and the forced behavior at zero.

4. GCD Table — Codeforces 338D

  • Level: Capstone · Native: 2900 · Language: English/Russian
  • Trains: converting gcd requirements into congruences; generalized CRT with overflow constraints.
  • Editorial: official problem analysis
Hint 1 Seek a row equal to a common multiple of every requested gcd.
Hint 2 The starting column must satisfy one congruence modulo each a_i; merge them even though the moduli overlap.
Hint 3 Construct a bounded common multiple, CRT the column constraints, then explicitly verify every gcd and table boundary.

5. Product Transformation — Codeforces 852F

  • Level: Capstone · Native: 2200 · Language: English/Russian
  • Trains: multiplicative order, binomial exponent structure, computation modulo a prime order.
  • Editorial: official Bubble Cup X booklet, Problem F
Hint 1 Write each array entry as a power of a and inspect how one transformation changes the exponent vector.
Hint 2 Pascal’s recurrence shows that the final exponents are prefix sums of one row of binomial coefficients.
Hint 3 Reduce exponents modulo the given prime multiplicative order; precompute factorials and inverses in that field.
