01 — Modular Algebra and Finite Fields

Why this topic matters

This chapter turns congruences into algorithms. The recurring contest signal is that values are huge but only their residue, multiplicative order, or compatibility across several moduli matters. A congruence is an equality after we deliberately forget multiples of a modulus; it is not ordinary arithmetic with a mysterious % attached. Work through the sections in order; the final four topics assume factorization from Chapter 02.

For every division below, first ask one question: is the denominator a unit in this ring? In modular arithmetic, that question is the difference between a valid derivation and a plausible-looking wrong answer.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Read proofs and Bézout. This chapter teaches you to derive linear-congruence and CRT conditions, distinguish rings from fields, prove exponent-period facts, and select a root or logarithm algorithm with an explicit domain. Factorization is treated as supplied data when needed; Chapter 02 explains how to obtain it.

Definitions and notation

Throughout, \(m\ge1\) is an integer modulus, \(p\) is prime, and exponents in algorithmic queries are nonnegative unless stated otherwise. A residue class modulo \(m\) contains all integers with the same remainder; its representative is chosen in \([0,m)\). Modulo one there is just one class, so return zero directly rather than passing it to field algorithms.

A commutative ring supports addition, subtraction, and multiplication with their familiar associative, commutative, and distributive laws, and identities \(0,1\). A unit has a multiplicative inverse. A field is a nonzero commutative ring where every nonzero element is a unit. The ring \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\) records residues; it is a field exactly when \(m\) is prime. A group has an associative operation, an identity, and inverses for all elements; the units of any ring form a group under multiplication.

1. Congruences as algebra

For a positive modulus \(m\), write \(a ≡ b \pmod{m}\) when \(m \mid (a-b)\): \(a\) and \(b\) leave the same remainder after division by \(m\). Thus \(17≡5\pmod {12}\), and replacing one by the other cannot change a later sum or product modulo \(12\). Addition and multiplication descend to residue classes, but division is conditional: \(a\) has an inverse modulo \(m\) exactly when \(\gcd(a,m)=1\). Extended Euclid gives integers \(x,y\) with

\[ax+my=\gcd(a,m).\]

This identity explains division: when \(g=1\), it reads \(ax+my=1\), so \(ax≡1\) and \(x\) is the inverse of \(a\). More generally, the values of \(ax\) modulo \(m\) are exactly the multiples of \(g=\gcd(a,m)\). Therefore \(ax ≡ b \pmod{m}\) has no solution if \(g∤b\); otherwise divide by \(g\), invert \(a/g\) modulo \(m/g\), and obtain one residue class modulo \(m/g\) (hence \(g\) solutions modulo \(m\)).

Example. \(6x≡8\pmod {14}\) becomes \(3x≡4\pmod7\), so \(x≡6\pmod7\). Modulo \(14\) this means the two solutions \(6\) and \(13\). By contrast, \(6x≡5\pmod {14}\) is impossible because every left side is even modulo \(14\).

Recognition. A repeated step on a cycle, synchronized clocks, or an equation with a coefficient that may not be invertible is a linear congruence, not “ordinary modular division.”

2. CRT, including non-coprime moduli

To merge \(x≡a \pmod{m}\) and \(x≡b \pmod{n}\), start from the first statement: every such integer has the form \(x=a+mt\). Substituting it into the second gives

\[mt\equiv b-a\pmod n.\]

It is solvable iff \(\gcd(m,n) \mid b-a\); all solutions form one class modulo \(\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\). This is a consistency check, not an optional optimization. For example, \(x≡1\pmod6\) and \(x≡3\pmod8\) are compatible because \(2\mid(3-1)\); substituting gives \(6t≡2\pmod8\), then \(t≡3\pmod4\), hence \(x≡19\pmod {24}\). The pair \(x≡1\pmod6\), \(x≡2\pmod8\) is impossible: their parities already disagree. Fold constraints one at a time, normalize each merged residue, and guard the LCM against overflow.

For pairwise coprime prime powers, CRT is a ring isomorphism \(\mathbb Z/(\prod_i p_i^{e_i})\mathbb Z \cong \prod_i \mathbb Z/(p_i^{e_i})\mathbb Z\). This is why composite-modulus equations should often be solved locally and recombined.

3. Finite fields and multiplicative order

For prime \(p\), every nonzero residue is invertible, so the nonzero residues form a multiplicative group \(\mathbb F_p^\times\). This group happens to be cyclic: there is a generator \(g\) whose powers list every nonzero residue once, and its order is \(p-1\). Consequently:

  • Fermat: \(a^{p-1}=1\) for \(a≠0\).
  • \(\operatorname{ord}_p(a) \mid p-1\).
  • A nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) over a field has at most \(d\) roots.
  • \(x^{k}=a\) can be converted into a linear congruence in exponents after choosing a primitive root.

For composite moduli, nonzero does not imply invertible, polynomial root bounds may fail, and the unit group need not be cyclic. Always identify where primality is used.

The order of \(a\) is the first positive exponent \(d\) for which \(a^d=1\); it is the length of the cycle \(1,a,a^2,\ldots\). For example, \(2\) modulo \(7\) has order \(3\) because \(2,4,1\) repeat. Assuming \(\gcd(a,m)=1\), start at \(\varphi(m)\) (or the Carmichael exponent \(\lambda(m)\)) and remove a prime factor \(q\) whenever \(a^{\operatorname{ord}/q}=1\). A primitive root has order \(\varphi(m)\); for \(m\ge2\), primitive roots exist exactly for \(m\in\{2,4,p^{k},2p^{k}\}\) with odd prime \(p\) and \(k\ge1\). Some sources add \(m=1\) by a vacuous convention; it has no relevance to modular algorithms.

To understand the classification, lift a generator modulo odd \(p\) to one modulo \(p^2\). Among its \(p\) lifts, at most one has \((p-1)\)st power one modulo \(p^2\): expanding \((g+tp)^{p-1}\) makes the next digit a nonconstant linear function of \(t\). Choose another lift. The valuation formula proved in the next section shows its order modulo \(p^k\) is \(p^{k-1}(p-1)\). CRT identifies units modulo \(2p^k\) with those modulo \(p^k\). Conversely, two distinct odd-prime components, or an odd-prime component and a factor four, have even-sized unit groups: no element of their product can have order equal to the product of the sizes, because its order divides their smaller lcm. Modulo \(2^k\) for \(k\ge3\), the four distinct roots \(1,-1,1+2^{k-1},-1+2^{k-1}\) of \(x^2=1\) rule out cyclicity, since a cyclic even-order group has exactly two such elements. Moduli two and four are checked directly.

Why the group and root facts hold

A nonzero degree-\(d\) polynomial over a field has at most \(d\) distinct roots. A root \(r\) permits division by the monic polynomial \(x-r\), leaving zero remainder. Every other root is a root of the quotient because \(x-r\) is nonzero there and can be cancelled. Induct on the degree. Modulo \(8\), however, the degree-two polynomial \(x^2-1\) has four roots \(1,3,5,7\): field hypotheses matter.

For a unit \(a\) of order \(d\), the powers \(1,a,\ldots,a^{d-1}\) are distinct. Every element in a finite group belongs to a block obtained by multiplying these \(d\) elements by some \(b\). Two blocks coincide or are disjoint: one common element lets us cancel and express one multiplier through the other. Thus \(d\) divides the group size. If \(a^e=1\), division of \(e\) by \(d\) shows its remainder must be zero, so \(d\mid e\).

Here is a proof of cyclicity. Let \(G\) be a finite subgroup of the nonzero elements of a field, and let \(D\) be the lcm of its element orders. For each prime power \(q^e\) dividing \(D\) exactly, some element has order divisible by \(q^e\); raising it to the other factor of its order gives an element of order \(q^e\). Products of commuting elements of coprime orders have order equal to the product: a common power of the two elements has order dividing both, so must be the identity. Repeating this constructs an element of order \(D\). Every element of \(G\) is a root of \(x^D-1\), so \(|G|\le D\) by the root bound; the constructed cyclic subgroup has \(D\) elements, so \(D\le |G|\). Equality proves a generator exists. For \(\mathbb F_p^\times\), its order is \(p-1\).

The Euler totient \(\varphi(m)\) counts units modulo \(m\). Multiplication by a unit permutes them; multiplying all entries and cancelling their product proves \(a^{\varphi(m)}=1\). The Carmichael exponent \(\lambda(m)\) is the lcm of all unit orders, hence the least positive exponent giving one for every unit. It divides \(\varphi(m)\), so using \(\varphi(m)\) is always a valid alternative when a smaller period is unnecessary.

4. Valuations and lifting

The \(p\)-adic valuation \(v_p(n)\) is the exponent of \(p\) in the prime factorization of a nonzero integer \(n\): for instance \(v_2(40)=3\). It is useful to set \(v_p(0)=+\infty\) when comparing valuations of sums. It turns a question about a large product into a question about addition of exponents:

\[v_p(ab)=v_p(a)+v_p(b),\qquad v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\left\lfloor n/p^j\right\rfloor.\]

For sums, \(v_p(a+b)≥\min(v_p(a),v_p(b))\), with equality if the two valuations differ: factor out the smaller power, and the remaining sum is nonzero modulo \(p\). LTE supplies sharper forms when cancellation needs to be measured. The most used one is: for an odd prime \(p\), positive integer \(n\), distinct integers \(x,y\), \(p\mid(x-y)\), and \(p\nmid xy\),

\[v_p(x^{n}-y^{n})=v_p(x-y)+v_p(n).\]

Why the factorial sum holds. There are \(\lfloor n/p\rfloor\) multiples contributing at least one \(p\), \(\lfloor n/p^2\rfloor\) contributing an additional \(p\), and so on. Each integer of valuation \(e\) is counted exactly \(e\) times; stop once \(p^j>n\).

Proof sketch for the stated LTE form. Write \(x=y+p^t u\) with \(t=v_p(x-y)\ge1\) and \(p\nmid u\). For positive \(s\) coprime to \(p\), the binomial expansion of \(x^s-y^s\) has first term \(s y^{s-1}p^t u\) of valuation \(t\) and all other terms of larger valuation. For exponent \(p\), the first term has valuation \(t+1\), intermediate terms have valuation at least \(2t+1\), and the last has valuation \(pt>t+1\) for odd \(p\). Thus exponent \(p\) raises the valuation by one. Write \(n=p^e s\) and repeat. The odd-prime hypothesis prevents a tie at \(p=2,t=1\).

Use valuations when divisibility by a huge power or the number of trailing prime factors is asked. For binomial coefficients,

\[v_p\binom nk=\sum_{j\ge1}\left(\lfloor n/p^{j}\rfloor-\lfloor k/p^{j}\rfloor-\lfloor(n-k)/p^{j}\rfloor\right),\]

equivalently the number of carries when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p\) (Kummer).

For an integer polynomial \(f\in\mathbb Z[x]\), Hensel lifting upgrades a root modulo \(p^{k}\) to modulo \(p^{k+1}\). We only need to decide its next base-\(p\) digit, so try \(r'=r+t p^{k}\) with \(t\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\). If \(f(r)≡0\pmod {p^k}\), the first-order expansion gives

\[\frac{f(r)}{p^k}+t f'(r)\equiv0\pmod p.\]

The quotient is an integer because \(r\) was already a root modulo \(p^k\). If \(f'(r)\not\equiv0\pmod p\), this linear equation has one \(t\), hence one lift. If the derivative vanishes, it has either no solution or every \(t\) is a solution, giving zero or \(p\) lifts—never assume uniqueness.

5. Discrete logarithms

Given \(a^{x}≡b \pmod{m}\) with \(a\) a unit, baby-step giant-step (BSGS) searches an exponent by writing the representative \(0\le x<\operatorname{ord}(a)\) as \(x=q+nj\), where \(0\le q<n\) and \(n=\lceil\sqrt{\operatorname{ord}(a)}\rceil\). Then \(a^q=b(a^{-n})^j\). Store all small “baby” powers \(a^q\) and scan the “giant” right sides; an equality gives \(x=q+nj\). Retain the smallest baby exponent for each stored value and verify every reported candidate. If the order is not known, taking \(n=\lceil\sqrt m\rceil\) is safe for a unit modulo \(m\), since the order is at most \(\varphi(m)<m\). This group version needs \(\gcd(a,m)=1\) and costs \(O(\sqrt{\operatorname{ord}(a)})\) time and memory when the order is used. It is a meet-in-the-middle search, not a logarithm in the numerical sense. If giant steps are scanned by increasing \(j\), the table retains the smallest \(q\) for each value, and candidates \(q+nj\ge\operatorname{ord}(a)\) are discarded, the first verified collision is the smallest nonnegative solution. Other scan or hash conventions should collect and minimize the normalized candidates.

The search succeeds only when \(b\) belongs to the cyclic subgroup generated by \(a\). For example, modulo \(7\) the powers of \(2\) are \(1,2,4\); therefore \(2^x\equiv3\pmod 7\) has no solution even though both sides are units.

When \(\gcd(a,m)>1\), ordinary BSGS cannot invert \(a\). The extension maintains \(k a^{x-t}\equiv b\pmod m\) for the still-unexamined exponents \(x\ge t\); initially \(k=1,t=0\). First test \(k=b\) for \(x=t\). For \(x>t\), set \(g=\gcd(a,m)\): if \(g\nmid b\), no remaining exponent works. Otherwise divide \(m,b\) by \(g\), update \(k\leftarrow k(a/g)\bmod m\) using the old \(g\), and increase \(t\). These are integer divisions of the equation, so the invariant is preserved. When the reduced modulus becomes one, the smallest remaining exponent works. When \(\gcd(a,m)=1\), \(k\) is also a unit: any prime shared with the original base has by then been removed from the modulus. Solve \(a^{x-t}=b/k\) using ordinary BSGS. Always check exponent zero first, including \(a=0\); the convention here is \(a^0=1\) even for zero. The stripping phase takes at most \(O(\log m)\) steps.

Pohlig–Hellman is better when the known order \(L=\prod_i q_i^{e_i}\) has small prime factors. Raise \(a,b\) to \(L/q_i^{e_i}\) to project to order \(q_i^{e_i}\). If the low \(j\) base-\(q_i\) digits of the logarithm are known, divide away their contribution and raise to \(q_i^{e_i-1-j}\); this leaves a logarithm in the subgroup of order \(q_i\), revealing the next digit. CRT recombines the exponent residues. BSGS for each digit gives \(O(\sum_i e_i(\log L+\sqrt{q_i}))\) group operations and reusable \(O(\max_i\sqrt{q_i})\) table space, excluding factorization. The largest prime factor matters, but the digit and exponentiation costs also count.

6. Modular roots

For odd prime \(p\) and \(a\ne0\), a quadratic residue is a value \(a\) for which some \(x\) satisfies \(x^2=a\). Euler’s criterion gives \(a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv1\pmod p\) exactly for residues, and \(-1\) for non-residues. Tonelli–Shanks writes \(p-1=q\cdot2^{s}\) with odd \(q\), finds a quadratic non-residue, then cancels the current error inside the \(2\)-power subgroup. Given a non-residue, it runs in \(O(\log p+s^{2})\) modular multiplications; random sampling finds one in expected constant trials.

Euler’s criterion follows from a generator \(g\): \(a=g^t\) is a square iff \(t\) is even, and \(g^{(p-1)/2}=-1\) because it has order two. Thus the criterion has no false positives in an odd prime field.

For the Tonelli–Shanks invariant, put \(r=a^{(q+1)/2}\), \(t=a^q\), \(c=z^q\), and \(M=s\), where \(z\) is a non-residue. Then \(r^2=at\), \(c\) has order \(2^M\), and \(t\) has order dividing \(2^{M-1}\) when \(a\) is a square. If \(t\ne1\), find the least \(i\) with \(t^{2^i}=1\); then \(1\le i<M\). Put \(b=c^{2^{M-i-1}}\) and replace \((r,t,c,M)\) by \((rb,tb^2,b^2,i)\). The relation \(r^2=at\) is preserved. Both \(t\) and \(b^2\) have \(2^{i-1}\)st power \(-1\), so their product has order dividing \(2^{i-1}\); \(c\) now has order \(2^i\). Thus the invariant holds with a strictly smaller \(M\), and termination at \(t=1\) gives a root. The repeated squarings over decreasing \(M\) cost \(O(s^2)\). Sampling a non-residue adds expected \(O(\log p)\) multiplications, since half the nonzero field elements are non-residues.

For example, \(a=10,p=13\) has \(q=3,s=2\), and \(z=2\) is a non-residue. Initially \(r=9,t=12,c=8\). The least \(i\) is one, so \(b=8\) and the new \(r=7,t=1\). The roots are \(7\) and \(6\); their squares are \(10\) modulo \(13\).

For the excluded prime \(p=2\), the nonzero multiplicative group has one element, and both field elements \(0\) and \(1\) are their own square roots. Euler’s criterion and Tonelli–Shanks are not needed there.

For a positive integer \(k\), first separate \(a=0\) in \(x^{k}=a \pmod{p}\): its only root in a field is \(x=0\). For nonzero \(a\), write values as powers of a primitive root: \(g^{kt}=a\). A discrete logarithm converts this to \(kt≡\log_g(a) \pmod{p-1}\). It has \(\gcd(k,p-1)\) solutions if compatible. Specialized root algorithms avoid a full discrete log; use them when constraints exceed BSGS.

7. Exponent towers and efficient k-th roots

Euler reduction \(a^{e} \bmod m = a^{e \bmod \varphi(m)} \bmod m\) is valid without qualification only when \(\gcd(a,m)=1\): otherwise reducing a large exponent can turn a multiple of a prime into a nonmultiple. For example, \(2^4≡0\pmod4\) but \(2^{4\bmod\varphi(4)}=2^0≡1\pmod4\). For a tower, recurse through the Carmichael chain \(m, \lambda(m), \lambda(\lambda(m)), \ldots\), but a residue alone is not enough: the caller must also know whether the true exponent exceeds a requested small cap. Compute that fact with saturating arithmetic instead of inferring it from the reduced residue. For a prime-power modulus \(p^r\), first separate \(e=0\), which gives \(1\). If \(p^r\mid a\) and \(e>0\), the residue is \(0\). Otherwise write \(a=p^v u\) with \(p\nmid u\) and \(0\le v<r\). If \(v=0\), reduce the exponent modulo \(\lambda(p^r)\). If \(v>0\), then \(a^e\equiv0\pmod{p^r}\) as soon as \(e\ge\lceil r/v\rceil\); below that threshold the exponent is small enough to retain exactly. Solve this independently for every \(p^r\mid m\) and combine the residues by CRT. This local rule is the reason the extra “is the exponent large enough?” information is necessary.

For \(x^k=a\bmod p\) with \(a\ne0\), existence is equivalent to \(a^{(p-1)/g}=1\), where \(g=\gcd(k,p-1)\). Indeed, writing \(a=u^b\) for a generator \(u\), both conditions say \(g\mid b\). If \(g=1\), invert \(k\) modulo \(p-1\) and return \(a^{k^{-1}\bmod(p-1)}\). If \(g>1\), the general discrete-log reduction is valid, but one must not assume the relevant subgroup is smooth: \(g\) may contain a large prime. Specialized root extraction needs its own proved contract; it is not obtained merely by splitting off the gcd.

Worked synthesis: a composite-modulus exponent

Compute \(6^{\,2^{10}}\bmod72\). Factor \(72=8\cdot9\). Modulo \(8\), the base has valuation \(v_2(6)=1\), so any exponent at least three gives zero. Modulo \(9\), \(v_3(6)=1\), so any exponent at least two gives zero. Since \(2^{10}\) exceeds both caps, CRT gives zero modulo \(72\) without storing the power of six. The tempting reduction modulo \(\varphi(8)=4\) changes the exponent to zero and would incorrectly return one modulo \(8\). The missing information is the exponent’s size relative to the valuation threshold.

For a height-\(h\) tower of nonnegative integer bases, define \(0^0=1\) locally if zero bases are allowed. A capped evaluator returns \(\min(E,C)\), where \(E\) is the true exponent and \(C\) the requested cap. Repeated multiplication stops as soon as the cap is reached; for base at least two, only \(O(\log C)\) multiplications are needed at a level. Bases zero and one need explicit branches. Combine these size certificates with modular recursion; the work depends on the tower height and the factorization/period chain, not on writing the enormous tower value.

Contest translation: algorithm contracts

Task Required facts Cost excluding factorization
Linear congruence \(m>0\), test \(\gcd(a,m)\mid b\) \(O(\log m)\) integer divisions
CRT merge Positive moduli, consistency test, enough integer width One Euclidean solve per merge
Element order A unit, a factored known multiple of its order One modular power per attempted factor removal
BSGS A unit and an order bound \(L\) Expected \(O(\sqrt L)\) time with hashing, \(O(\sqrt L)\) memory
Tonelli–Shanks Odd prime, nonzero quadratic residue Expected \(O(\log p+s^2)\) modular multiplications
Hensel digit Integer polynomial, root modulo \(p^j\), \(j\ge1\) One linear congruence per root branch

Sorting rather than hashing BSGS entries adds a logarithmic lookup/sort factor. Finding a primitive root requires the factorization of \(p-1\) and tests \(g^{(p-1)/q}\ne1\) for every distinct prime \(q\mid p-1\); these tests suffice because every proper divisor of \(p-1\) divides at least one \((p-1)/q\). The cost includes the number of candidates tried.

Contest checklist and pitfalls

  • Normalize negative residues; C++ % may be negative.
  • Never use Fermat inversion unless the modulus is prime and the denominator nonzero.
  • Protect multiplication and LCM with __int128; check promised bounds before casting back.
  • CRT consistency is a divisibility test, not pairwise coprimality.
  • Distinguish a primitive root of the whole multiplicative group from an \(n\)th root of unity.
  • In BSGS, clarify whether the smallest solution is required and handle \(x=0\), \(a=0\), and non-coprime cases.
  • Hensel’s derivative-zero branch and \(p=2\) require separate analysis.

TipEditorial guidance: transformation as a shift

Write \(A_i=a^{e_i}\), initially \(e_i=1\). One operation sends \(e_i\) to \(e_i+e_{i+1}\), with the final exponent unchanged. If \(S\) shifts the exponent vector one position toward smaller indices and fills the end with zero, then \(S^N=0\) and \(M\) operations give \((I+S)^M\). The binomial theorem yields \(e_i=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(M,N-i)}\binom Mj\) for one-based \(i\). Compute these prefix sums in the prime-order field, then exponentiate the original base modulo \(Q\). If every needed \(j\) is below that prime, the incremental binomial product divides only by those \(j\); it does not require \(M!\) to be invertible. For indices crossing the characteristic, use the Lucas/prime-power contracts.

Exercises with solutions

1. Roots can branch — concept check

Lift all roots of \(x^2=1\) modulo \(4\) to modulus \(8\). Why does the simple-root Hensel rule not apply?

TipHint and solution

The roots modulo four are \(1,3\). Their candidate lifts are \(1,5\) and \(3,7\); all four square to one modulo eight. The derivative \(2x\) vanishes modulo two, so each existing root can have either zero or two lifts. By contrast, \(x^2=3\) has a root modulo two but none modulo four.

2. Root counting through exponents — derivation

Find all solutions of \(x^4=4\) modulo \(7\), and explain why there are exactly two.

TipHint and solution

The primitive root \(3\) has powers \(1,3,2,6,4,5\). Thus \(4=3^4\), and \(4t\equiv4\pmod6\) gives \(t\equiv1\pmod3\): \(t=1,4\). The roots are \(3,4\). The count is \(\gcd(4,6)=2\); zero is not a root.

3. Synchronization with a deadline — modelling

A machine is ready at times \(t\equiv1\pmod6\) and another at \(t\equiv3\pmod8\). Find the first common ready time at least \(50\).

TipHint and solution

CRT gives \(t=19+24k\). Impose \(19+24k\ge50\), so \(k\ge\lceil31/24\rceil=2\) and the answer is \(67\). Choosing the normalized CRT residue alone answers a different question. For a time window \([A,B]\), the feasible \(k\) interval is \([\lceil(A-19)/24\rceil,\lfloor(B-19)/24\rfloor]\).

4. Count valid exponents without a logarithm — synthesis

For prime \(p\) and a unit \(a\) of known order \(d\), count integers \(0\le x<X\) satisfying \(a^{kx}=1\), where \(k\ge1\) and \(X\ge0\).

TipHint and solution

The condition is \(d\mid kx\), hence \(d/\gcd(d,k)\mid x\). Let \(D=d/\gcd(d,k)\). For \(X=0\) the count is zero; otherwise it is \(1+\lfloor(X-1)/D\rfloor\). No discrete logarithm is needed because the target is the identity. With \(a=2,p=7,d=3,k=2,X=10\), valid exponents are \(0,3,6,9\).

Summary and reference sheet

Division modulo \(m\) means inversion of a unit. Linear congruences are solvable iff their gcd obstruction vanishes; compatible CRT constraints form one class modulo the lcm. Field polynomial root bounds imply cyclicity of finite multiplicative subgroups. Exponents reduce modulo an element’s order only for units. Valuations turn prime-power vanishing into an exponent threshold. Hensel chooses the next digit by a derivative congruence; a zero derivative requires a branching test.
