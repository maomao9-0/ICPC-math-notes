01 — Modular Algebra and Finite Fields

This chapter turns congruences into algorithms. The recurring contest signal is that values are huge but only their residue, multiplicative order, or compatibility across several moduli matters. A congruence is an equality after we deliberately forget multiples of a modulus; it is not ordinary arithmetic with a mysterious % attached. Work through the sections in order; the final four topics assume factorization from Chapter 02.

For every division below, first ask one question: is the denominator a unit in this ring? In modular arithmetic, that question is the difference between a valid derivation and a plausible-looking wrong answer.

1. Congruences as algebra

For a positive modulus \(m\), write \(a ≡ b \pmod{m}\) when \(m \mid (a-b)\): \(a\) and \(b\) leave the same remainder after division by \(m\). Thus \(17≡5\pmod {12}\), and replacing one by the other cannot change a later sum or product modulo \(12\). Addition and multiplication descend to residue classes, but division is conditional: \(a\) has an inverse modulo \(m\) exactly when \(\gcd(a,m)=1\). Extended Euclid gives integers \(x,y\) with

\[ax+my=\gcd(a,m).\]

This identity explains division: when \(g=1\), it reads \(ax+my=1\), so \(ax≡1\) and \(x\) is the inverse of \(a\). More generally, the values of \(ax\) modulo \(m\) are exactly the multiples of \(g=\gcd(a,m)\). Therefore \(ax ≡ b \pmod{m}\) has no solution if \(g∤b\); otherwise divide by \(g\), invert \(a/g\) modulo \(m/g\), and obtain one residue class modulo \(m/g\) (hence \(g\) solutions modulo \(m\)).

Example. \(6x≡8\pmod {14}\) becomes \(3x≡4\pmod7\), so \(x≡6\pmod7\). Modulo \(14\) this means the two solutions \(6\) and \(13\). By contrast, \(6x≡5\pmod {14}\) is impossible because every left side is even modulo \(14\).

Recognition. A repeated step on a cycle, synchronized clocks, or an equation with a coefficient that may not be invertible is a linear congruence, not “ordinary modular division.”

2. CRT, including non-coprime moduli

To merge \(x≡a \pmod{m}\) and \(x≡b \pmod{n}\), start from the first statement: every such integer has the form \(x=a+mt\). Substituting it into the second gives

\[mt\equiv b-a\pmod n.\]

It is solvable iff \(\gcd(m,n) \mid b-a\); all solutions form one class modulo \(\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\). This is a consistency check, not an optional optimization. For example, \(x≡1\pmod6\) and \(x≡3\pmod8\) are compatible because \(2\mid(3-1)\); substituting gives \(6t≡2\pmod8\), then \(t≡3\pmod4\), hence \(x≡19\pmod {24}\). The pair \(x≡1\pmod6\), \(x≡2\pmod8\) is impossible: their parities already disagree. Fold constraints one at a time, normalize each merged residue, and guard the LCM against overflow.

For pairwise coprime prime powers, CRT is a ring isomorphism \(\mathbb Z/(\prod_i p_i^{e_i})\mathbb Z \cong \prod_i \mathbb Z/(p_i^{e_i})\mathbb Z\). This is why composite-modulus equations should often be solved locally and recombined.

3. Finite fields and multiplicative order

For prime \(p\), every nonzero residue is invertible, so the nonzero residues form a multiplicative group \(\mathbb F_p^\times\). This group happens to be cyclic: there is a generator \(g\) whose powers list every nonzero residue once, and its order is \(p-1\). Consequently:

  • Fermat: \(a^{p-1}=1\) for \(a≠0\).
  • \(\operatorname{ord}_p(a) \mid p-1\).
  • A nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) over a field has at most \(d\) roots.
  • \(x^{k}=a\) can be converted into a linear congruence in exponents after choosing a primitive root.

For composite moduli, nonzero does not imply invertible, polynomial root bounds may fail, and the unit group need not be cyclic. Always identify where primality is used.

The order of \(a\) is the first positive exponent \(d\) for which \(a^d=1\); it is the length of the cycle \(1,a,a^2,\ldots\). For example, \(2\) modulo \(7\) has order \(3\) because \(2,4,1\) repeat. Assuming \(\gcd(a,m)=1\), start at \(\varphi(m)\) (or the Carmichael exponent \(\lambda(m)\)) and remove a prime factor \(q\) whenever \(a^{\operatorname{ord}/q}=1\). A primitive root has order \(\varphi(m)\); for \(m\ge2\), primitive roots exist exactly for \(m\in\{2,4,p^{k},2p^{k}\}\) with odd prime \(p\) and \(k\ge1\). Some sources add \(m=1\) by a vacuous convention; it has no relevance to modular algorithms.

4. Valuations and lifting

The \(p\)-adic valuation \(v_p(n)\) is the exponent of \(p\) in the prime factorization of a nonzero integer \(n\): for instance \(v_2(40)=3\). It is useful to set \(v_p(0)=+\infty\) when comparing valuations of sums. It turns a question about a large product into a question about addition of exponents:

\[v_p(ab)=v_p(a)+v_p(b),\qquad v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\ge1}\left\lfloor n/p^j\right\rfloor.\]

For sums, \(v_p(a+b)≥\min(v_p(a),v_p(b))\), with equality if the two valuations differ: factor out the smaller power, and the remaining sum is nonzero modulo \(p\). LTE supplies sharper forms when cancellation needs to be measured. The most used one is: for an odd prime \(p\), positive integer \(n\), distinct integers \(x,y\), \(p\mid(x-y)\), and \(p\nmid xy\),

\[v_p(x^{n}-y^{n})=v_p(x-y)+v_p(n).\]

Use valuations when divisibility by a huge power or the number of trailing prime factors is asked. For binomial coefficients,

\[v_p\binom nk=\sum_{j\ge1}\left(\lfloor n/p^{j}\rfloor-\lfloor k/p^{j}\rfloor-\lfloor(n-k)/p^{j}\rfloor\right),\]

equivalently the number of carries when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p\) (Kummer).

For an integer polynomial \(f\in\mathbb Z[x]\), Hensel lifting upgrades a root modulo \(p^{k}\) to modulo \(p^{k+1}\). We only need to decide its next base-\(p\) digit, so try \(r'=r+t p^{k}\) with \(t\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\). If \(f(r)≡0\pmod {p^k}\), the first-order expansion gives

\[\frac{f(r)}{p^k}+t f'(r)\equiv0\pmod p.\]

The quotient is an integer because \(r\) was already a root modulo \(p^k\). If \(f'(r)\not\equiv0\pmod p\), this linear equation has one \(t\), hence one lift. If the derivative vanishes, it has either no solution or every \(t\) is a solution, giving zero or \(p\) lifts—never assume uniqueness.

5. Discrete logarithms

Given \(a^{x}≡b \pmod{m}\) with \(a\) a unit, baby-step giant-step (BSGS) searches an exponent by writing the representative \(0\le x<\operatorname{ord}(a)\) as \(x=q+nj\), where \(0\le q<n\) and \(n=\lceil\sqrt{\operatorname{ord}(a)}\rceil\). Then \(a^q=b(a^{-n})^j\). Store all small “baby” powers \(a^q\) and scan the “giant” right sides; an equality gives \(x=q+nj\). Retain the smallest baby exponent for each stored value and verify every reported candidate. If the order is not known, taking \(n=\lceil\sqrt m\rceil\) is safe for a unit modulo \(m\), since the order is at most \(\varphi(m)<m\). This group version needs \(\gcd(a,m)=1\) and costs \(O(\sqrt{\operatorname{ord}(a)})\) time and memory when the order is used. It is a meet-in-the-middle search, not a logarithm in the numerical sense. If giant steps are scanned by increasing \(j\), the table retains the smallest \(q\) for each value, and candidates \(q+nj\ge\operatorname{ord}(a)\) are discarded, the first verified collision is the smallest nonnegative solution. Other scan or hash conventions should collect and minimize the normalized candidates.

The search succeeds only when \(b\) belongs to the cyclic subgroup generated by \(a\). For example, modulo \(7\) the powers of \(2\) are \(1,2,4\); therefore \(2^x\equiv3\pmod 7\) has no solution even though both sides are units.

When \(\gcd(a,m)>1\), extended BSGS repeatedly strips \(g=\gcd(a,m)\). At each stripping step, require \(g|b\), divide \(m,b\) by \(g\), and maintain the accumulated multiplier; check small exponents along the way. This is subtle enough that a known-tested template is preferable in a contest.

Pohlig–Hellman is better when the group order is smooth: solve the logarithm modulo each prime power dividing the order, then CRT the exponent. Its hard part is the largest prime factor, roughly \(O(\sqrt{q})\) group operations.

6. Modular roots

For odd prime \(p\) and \(a\ne0\), a quadratic residue is a value \(a\) for which some \(x\) satisfies \(x^2=a\). Euler’s criterion gives \(a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv1\pmod p\) exactly for residues, and \(-1\) for non-residues. Tonelli–Shanks writes \(p-1=q\cdot2^{s}\) with odd \(q\), finds a quadratic non-residue, then cancels the current error inside the \(2\)-power subgroup. Given a non-residue, it runs in \(O(\log p+s^{2})\) modular multiplications; random sampling finds one in expected constant trials.

For the excluded prime \(p=2\), the nonzero multiplicative group has one element, and both field elements \(0\) and \(1\) are their own square roots. Euler’s criterion and Tonelli–Shanks are not needed there.

For a positive integer \(k\), first separate \(a=0\) in \(x^{k}=a \pmod{p}\): its only root in a field is \(x=0\). For nonzero \(a\), write values as powers of a primitive root: \(g^{kt}=a\). A discrete logarithm converts this to \(kt≡\log_g(a) \pmod{p-1}\). It has \(\gcd(k,p-1)\) solutions if compatible. Specialized root algorithms avoid a full discrete log; use them when constraints exceed BSGS.

7. Exponent towers and efficient k-th roots

Euler reduction \(a^{e} \bmod m = a^{e \bmod \varphi(m)} \bmod m\) is valid without qualification only when \(\gcd(a,m)=1\): otherwise reducing a large exponent can turn a multiple of a prime into a nonmultiple. For example, \(2^4≡0\pmod4\) but \(2^{4\bmod\varphi(4)}=2^0≡1\pmod4\). For a tower, recurse through the Carmichael chain \(m, \lambda(m), \lambda(\lambda(m)), \ldots\), but a residue alone is not enough: the caller must also know whether the true exponent exceeds a requested small cap. Compute that fact with saturating arithmetic instead of inferring it from the reduced residue. For a prime-power modulus \(p^r\), first separate \(e=0\), which gives \(1\). If \(p^r\mid a\) and \(e>0\), the residue is \(0\). Otherwise write \(a=p^v u\) with \(p\nmid u\) and \(0\le v<r\). If \(v=0\), reduce the exponent modulo \(\lambda(p^r)\). If \(v>0\), then \(a^e\equiv0\pmod{p^r}\) as soon as \(e\ge\lceil r/v\rceil\); below that threshold the exponent is small enough to retain exactly. Solve this independently for every \(p^r\mid m\) and combine the residues by CRT. This local rule is the reason the extra “is the exponent large enough?” information is necessary.

For \(x^{k}=a \bmod p\), the discrete-log reduction in Section 6 is conceptual but may be too slow. Fast algorithms split \(p-1\) into its \(\gcd(k,p-1)\) part, invert the coprime exponent part, and extract roots inside the remaining smooth subgroup. Always return and verify all roots or the required canonical root; existence is equivalent to \(a^{(p-1)/g}=1\), where \(g=\gcd(k,p-1)\) for \(a\ne 0\).

Contest checklist and pitfalls

  • Normalize negative residues; C++ % may be negative.
  • Never use Fermat inversion unless the modulus is prime and the denominator nonzero.
  • Protect multiplication and LCM with __int128; check promised bounds before casting back.
  • CRT consistency is a divisibility test, not pairwise coprimality.
  • Distinguish a primitive root of the whole multiplicative group from an \(n\)th root of unity.
  • In BSGS, clarify whether the smallest solution is required and handle \(x=0\), \(a=0\), and non-coprime cases.
  • Hensel’s derivative-zero branch and \(p=2\) require separate analysis.

  • Level: Capstone · Native: 2900 · Language: English/Russian
