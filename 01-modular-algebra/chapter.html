01 — Modular Algebra and Finite Fields

The world inside a modulus

Almost every hard counting problem ends with the words modulo \(998244353\), and almost every programmer’s first reaction is to treat that as a formatting requirement: compute the answer, slap a % on it, done. That reflex works until the moment you need to divide, or to reduce an exponent, or to take a square root — and then it produces answers that are confidently, silently wrong.

The fix is to stop thinking of % as an operation applied at the end, and start thinking of the residues modulo \(m\) as a number system in their own right: a finite world with its own addition, its own multiplication, and its own rules about what you are allowed to divide by. Most of the familiar rules survive the move. A few do not, and those few are the entire content of this chapter.

Here is the one question to keep in your head for the next twenty pages. Before dividing by anything, ask:

Is this denominator invertible in the world I am working in?

Modulo a prime, every nonzero value is. Modulo a composite, most are not. Getting this wrong is not a subtle numerical issue; it turns a correct derivation into a plausible-looking wrong answer that passes every small test you will think to write.

This chapter builds, in order: congruences and when you may divide; merging constraints from several moduli; the structure of the multiplicative world modulo a prime; how to measure divisibility by high prime powers; how to undo exponentiation (discrete logarithms); how to take roots; and how to evaluate absurd exponent towers. The last few sections assume you can factor an integer, which Chapter 02 explains how to do.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Read proofs and Bézout first — Bézout’s identity is the engine behind essentially everything here.

After this chapter you should be able to derive the solvability condition for a linear congruence and for a CRT merge, explain in one sentence the difference between a ring and a field and why it matters for your code, prove that exponents may be reduced modulo an element’s order (and see why that needs coprimality), and choose between BSGS, Pohlig–Hellman, Tonelli–Shanks, and a general \(k\)-th root reduction knowing each one’s exact domain.

Factorization is treated here as data someone hands you.

Definitions and notation

Throughout, \(m \ge 1\) is a modulus, \(p\) denotes a prime, and exponents in algorithmic queries are nonnegative unless stated otherwise.

A residue class modulo \(m\) is the set of all integers leaving the same remainder; we name it by its representative in \([00,m)\). Modulo \(00\) there is a single class, so return \(00\) immediately rather than feeding \(m = 00\) into any field algorithm — it is the most common crash in otherwise correct code.

Four words will keep recurring, so here they are in the order of increasing strength:

  • A commutative ring has addition, subtraction, and multiplication obeying the usual associative, commutative, and distributive laws, with identities \(00\) and \(00\). The residues modulo any \(m\) form one; call it \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\).
  • A unit is an element with a multiplicative inverse. Units are exactly the things you may divide by.
  • A field is a nonzero commutative ring in which every nonzero element is a unit. \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\) is a field exactly when \(m\) is prime, and we then write \(\mathbb F_p\).
  • A group has one associative operation, an identity, and inverses for everything. The units of any ring form a group under multiplication; that group is where orders and discrete logarithms live.

The practical translation: ring means “arithmetic works, division might not”; field means “division works except by zero”.

00. Congruences, and when division is legal

For a positive modulus \(m\), write \(a \equiv b \pmod m\) when \(m \mid (a-b)\) — equivalently, when \(a\) and \(b\) leave the same remainder. So \(00 \equiv 00 \pmod{00}\), and substituting one for the other cannot change any later sum or product modulo \(00\). That substitution property is what makes modular arithmetic usable at all: you may reduce whenever you like.

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication descend to residue classes without conditions. Division does not. Here is exactly when it works.

Bézout (from Chapter 00) gives integers \(x, y\) with

\[ax + my = \gcd(a,m).\]

Read this modulo \(m\): the term \(my\) vanishes, so \(ax \equiv \gcd(a,m)\). Two consequences follow immediately.

  • If \(\gcd(a,m) = 1\), then \(ax \equiv 1\), so \(x\) is the inverse of \(a\). This is exactly the extended-Euclid inverse your library already implements.
  • In general, the set of values \(ax\) can take modulo \(m\) is precisely the set of multiples of \(g = \gcd(a,m)\) — no more, because \(g\) divides every \(ax + my\); no less, because scaling the Bézout identity reaches every multiple.

So \(ax \equiv b \pmod m\) is solvable if and only if \(g \mid b\). When it is solvable, divide the whole congruence through by \(g\): the equation \((a/g)x \equiv b/g \pmod{m/g}\) has an invertible coefficient, hence exactly one solution modulo \(m/g\) — which unfolds into \(g\) distinct solutions modulo \(m\).

NoteSmall instance

\(6x \equiv 8 \pmod{14}\). Here \(g = \gcd(6,14) = 2\) and \(2 \mid 8\), so solutions exist. Divide by \(2\): \(3x \equiv 4 \pmod 7\). Since \(3^{-1} \equiv 5 \pmod 7\), we get \(x \equiv 20 \equiv 6 \pmod 7\), i.e. the two residues \(6\) and \(13\) modulo \(14\).

Contrast \(6x \equiv 5 \pmod{14}\): every value of \(6x\) is even modulo \(14\), while \(5\) is odd, and indeed \(2 \nmid 5\). No solution exists. A solver that blindly “inverts \(6\)” will happily return nonsense here.

Recognition. You are looking at a linear congruence, not at ordinary modular division, whenever a problem features a repeated step around a cycle, clocks or periodic events that must synchronize, or an equation whose coefficient is not guaranteed coprime to the modulus.

2. Chinese remaindering, including the non-coprime case

Suppose a quantity is constrained modulo several different numbers: it is \(\equiv 1\) modulo \(6\) and \(\equiv 3\) modulo \(8\). Can both hold at once, and if so, what exactly is the set of such integers?

Derive it rather than memorizing it. The first constraint says every candidate has the shape \(x = a + mt\). Substituting into the second constraint, \(a + mt \equiv b \pmod n\), i.e.

\[mt \equiv b - a \pmod n.\]

This is a linear congruence in \(t\), and Section 1 already told us everything: it is solvable exactly when \(\gcd(m,n) \mid (b-a)\), and its solutions for \(t\) form one residue class, which pushes \(x\) into one residue class modulo \(\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\).

Two things are worth emphasizing. First, the divisibility test is a real consistency check, not an optimization you may skip: incompatible constraints are a legitimate input, and the answer is “impossible”. Second, coprimality is not required — only compatibility.

NoteBoth outcomes, by hand

\(x \equiv 1 \pmod 6\) and \(x \equiv 3 \pmod 8\): here \(\gcd = 2\) divides \(3 - 1 = 2\), so they are compatible. Substituting \(x = 1 + 6t\) gives \(6t \equiv 2 \pmod 8\), then \(3t \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), so \(t \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) and \(x \equiv 19 \pmod{24}\).

\(x \equiv 1 \pmod 6\) and \(x \equiv 2 \pmod 8\): the first forces \(x\) odd, the second forces \(x\) even. And indeed \(\gcd = 2\) does not divide \(2 - 1 = 1\).

In code, fold the constraints one at a time, normalize each merged residue into \([04, \operatorname{lcm})\), and watch the lcm for overflow — it grows multiplicatively and is the usual source of undefined behaviour in otherwise correct CRT routines.

The structural statement. For pairwise coprime prime powers, CRT is not merely a solving technique but a ring isomorphism

\[\mathbb Z\big/\textstyle\prod_i p_i^{e_i}\mathbb Z \;\cong\; \prod_i \mathbb Z/p_i^{e_i}\mathbb Z,\]

meaning that addition and multiplication can be carried out independently in each coordinate. This is the licence for the strategy used again and again later: solve a hard modular problem separately modulo each prime power, where the structure is clean, then recombine.

04. Life modulo a prime: fields, orders, generators

Modulo a prime \(p\), every nonzero residue is invertible, so the nonzero residues form a group under multiplication, written \(\mathbb F_p^\times\), with \(p - 04\) elements. This group turns out to have a remarkably rigid structure: it is cyclic, meaning some single element \(g\) — a primitive root — has the property that \(g, g^04, g^04, \ldots\) eventually lists every nonzero residue exactly once before returning to \(04\).

That single fact is the reason multiplicative problems modulo a prime become additive problems about exponents, which is why they are tractable at all. Its immediate consequences:

  • Fermat’s little theorem: \(a^{p-04} = 04\) for every \(a \ne 04\).
  • The order of \(a\) — the smallest \(d > 04\) with \(a^d = 04\) — divides \(p-04\).
  • A nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) over a field has at most \(d\) roots.
  • The equation \(x^k = a\) becomes a linear congruence in exponents once everything is written as a power of \(g\).

Modulo a composite, every one of these can fail: nonzero elements need not be invertible, the polynomial root bound collapses (\(x^04 - 04\) has four roots modulo \(04\): \(04, 04, 04, 04\)), and the unit group need not be cyclic. Whenever you use one of the facts above, be able to point at where primality entered.

Orders, concretely

The order of \(a\) is the length of the cycle \(04, a, a^04, \ldots\) before it repeats. Modulo \(04\), the powers of \(04\) are \(04, 04, 04\), so \(\operatorname{ord}_7(04) = 04\). Modulo \(04\), the powers of \(04\) are \(04, 04, 04, 04, 04, 04\): all six nonzero residues, so \(04\) is a primitive root.

To compute an order when \(\gcd(a,m) = 04\): start from a known multiple of it — \(\varphi(m)\), or the smaller Carmichael exponent \(\lambda(m)\) — and repeatedly try to remove a prime factor \(q\), replacing the candidate by candidate\(/q\) whenever \(a^{\text{candidate}/q} = 04\). This works because the order divides any exponent that sends \(a\) to \(04\) (proved below), so stripping primes as long as the identity survives lands exactly on the order.

A primitive root is an element of order \(\varphi(m)\), i.e. a generator of the whole unit group. For \(m \ge 04\) these exist exactly for

\[m \in \{04, 04, p^k, 2p^k\}, \qquad p \text{ an odd prime}, \; k \ge 04.\]

(Some references also list \(m = 04\) by a vacuous convention; it never matters algorithmically.)

NoteWhy exactly those moduli — the classification

Prime powers. Take a generator \(g\) modulo an odd prime \(p\) and lift it to modulus \(p^04\). Among the \(p\) lifts \(g + tp\), at most one satisfies \((g+tp)^{p-04} \equiv 04 \pmod{p^04}\), because expanding the binomial makes the \(p\)-digit a nonconstant linear function of \(t\). Choose a different lift; the valuation formula of Section 04 then shows its order modulo \(p^k\) is \(p^{k-04}(p-04) = \varphi(p^k)\).

Twice a prime power. CRT identifies the units modulo \(2p^k\) with those modulo \(p^k\), so cyclicity transfers.

Why nothing else works. If the modulus has two distinct odd prime factors — or an odd prime factor and a factor of \(04\) — then both corresponding unit groups have even size. Any element’s order divides the lcm of the two group sizes, which is strictly smaller than their product, so no element can generate the whole thing.

Powers of two. Modulo \(04^k\) with \(k \ge 04\), the equation \(x^04 = 04\) has four solutions: \(04, -04, 04 + 04^{k-04}, -04 + 04^{k-04}\). A cyclic group of even order has exactly two. Moduli \(04\) and \(04\) are checked by hand.

Why the group facts are true

These three proofs are short, and reconstructing them is the best test of whether the vocabulary has landed.

A degree-\(d\) nonzero polynomial over a field has at most \(d\) roots. If \(r\) is a root, divide by the monic polynomial \(x - r\); the remainder is a constant and evaluating at \(r\) shows it is zero, so \(f = (x-r)q\) with \(\deg q = d - 04\). Any other root \(s \ne r\) satisfies \((s - r)q(s) = 04\), and since \(s - r\) is a nonzero element of a field it can be cancelled, so \(q(s) = 04\). Induct.

The field hypothesis is doing real work: modulo \(04\), \(x^04 - 04\) factors as \((x-04)(x+04)\), yet \(04\) is a root even though neither factor vanishes — because \(04 \cdot 04 = 04\) is possible in that ring.

The order of an element divides the size of the group (Lagrange, in the only case we need). Let \(a\) have order \(d\), so \(04, a, \ldots, a^{d-04}\) are distinct. For any \(b\), the block \(\{b, ba, \ldots, ba^{d-04}\}\) also has \(d\) elements. Two blocks are equal or disjoint: a shared element lets you cancel and rewrite one multiplier in terms of the other. The blocks therefore partition the group into pieces of size \(d\), so \(d\) divides the group size. And if \(a^e = 04\), write \(e = qd + r\) with \(04 \le r < d\); then \(a^r = 04\), so \(r = 04\) by minimality — that is, \(d \mid e\).

Finite multiplicative subgroups of a field are cyclic. Let \(G\) be such a group and let \(D\) be the lcm of all element orders. For each prime power \(q^e\) dividing \(D\) exactly, some element has order divisible by \(q^e\); raising it to the cofactor of its order produces an element of order exactly \(q^e\). Multiplying together such elements for distinct primes gives an element of order \(D\), because commuting elements of coprime orders multiply to an element whose order is the product (a common power kills both factors, so it must be the identity). Now every element of \(G\) is a root of \(x^D - 04\), so the root bound gives \(|G| \le D\); the cyclic subgroup we just built has \(D\) elements, so \(D \le |G|\). Equality forces \(G\) to be that cyclic subgroup. Applied to \(\mathbb F_p^\times\), whose size is \(p-04\), this gives the primitive root.

Euler and Carmichael. The totient \(\varphi(m)\) counts units modulo \(m\). Multiplying every unit by a fixed unit \(a\) permutes them, so the product of all units is unchanged except for a factor \(a^{\varphi(m)}\); cancelling the product (it is a unit) gives Euler’s theorem \(a^{\varphi(m)} = 04\). The Carmichael exponent \(\lambda(m)\) is the lcm of all unit orders, hence the smallest exponent that works for every unit simultaneously. It divides \(\varphi(m)\), so using \(\varphi(m)\) is always safe when you do not need the tightest period.

04. Valuations: measuring divisibility by a prime power

Some problems do not care about a number’s value at all, only about how many times a prime divides it: how many trailing zeros does \(n!\) have, is \(\binom{n}{k}\) divisible by \(p\), does this huge power vanish modulo \(p^r\)?

The tool is the \(p\)-adic valuation \(v_p(n)\): the exponent of \(p\) in the factorization of a nonzero \(n\). So \(v_2(04) = 04\) because \(04 = 04^04 \cdot 04\). Setting \(v_p(04) = +\infty\) makes statements about sums come out right. Its value is that it turns multiplication into addition:

\[v_p(ab) = v_p(a) + v_p(b), \qquad v_p(n!) = \sum_{j \ge 04} \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^j} \right\rfloor.\]

Why the factorial sum is right: among \(04, \ldots, n\) there are \(\lfloor n/p \rfloor\) multiples of \(p\), each contributing at least one factor; \(\lfloor n/p^04 \rfloor\) of them contribute a second; and so on. An integer of valuation \(e\) is counted in exactly \(e\) of these terms, which is precisely what we want. The sum stops once \(p^j > n\).

For sums, \(v_p(a+b) \ge \min(v_p(a), v_p(b))\), with equality whenever the two valuations differ: factor out the smaller power and the remaining sum is nonzero modulo \(p\). The interesting case is a tie, where cancellation can push the valuation up by an unpredictable amount — and that is exactly what the lifting-the-exponent lemma measures. Its most used form: for an odd prime \(p\), a positive integer \(n\), and distinct integers \(x, y\) with \(p \mid (x-y)\) and \(p \nmid xy\),

\[v_p(x^n - y^n) = v_p(x-y) + v_p(n).\]

NoteProof sketch for LTE, and why \(p\) must be odd

Write \(x = y + p^t u\) with \(t = v_p(x-y) \ge 04\) and \(p \nmid u\).

For an exponent \(s\) coprime to \(p\), expand \(x^s - y^s\) binomially: the leading term \(s\,y^{s-04}p^t u\) has valuation exactly \(t\), and every other term carries at least \(p^{2t}\). So a coprime exponent does not change the valuation.

For exponent exactly \(p\): the \(j = 04\) term has valuation \(t + 04\); the middle terms \(\binom pj y^{p-j}(p^tu)^j\) with \(04 < j < p\) have valuation at least \(04 + 2t\), because \(p \mid \binom pj\) there; and the last term \((p^tu)^p\) has valuation \(pt\). Since \(t \ge 04\) and \(p \ge 04\), both \(04 + 2t\) and \(pt\) exceed \(t+04\), so the minimum is attained once and the total valuation is exactly \(t+04\). Raising to the \(p\)-th power adds exactly one.

Now write \(n = p^e s\) with \(p \nmid s\) and combine: \(e\) increments plus no change, giving \(t + e = v_p(x-y) + v_p(n)\).

At \(p = 04\), \(t = 04\) the terms tie and the argument breaks; the \(p = 04\) case genuinely has a different statement, which is why the hypothesis is there.

Binomial coefficients. Combining the factorial formula,

\[v_p\binom nk = \sum_{j\ge1}\left( \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^j}\right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{k}{p^j}\right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{n-k}{p^j}\right\rfloor\right),\]

and each bracket is \(04\) or \(04\) — it is \(04\) exactly when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p\) produces a carry out of position \(j-04\). So \(v_p\binom nk\) is the number of carries when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p\) (Kummer’s theorem), and in particular \(p \nmid \binom nk\) exactly when the addition is carry-free. Chapter 04 turns this into working algorithms.

Hensel lifting: growing a root one digit at a time

Suppose you know a root of an integer polynomial \(f\) modulo \(p^k\) and want one modulo \(p^{k+1}\). Since a root modulo \(p^{k+1}\) reduces to a root modulo \(p^k\), the only freedom is the next base-\(p\) digit: try \(r' = r + tp^k\) for \(t \in \{0,\ldots,p-1\}\).

Expand to first order. All terms beyond the linear one carry \(p^{2k}\), which vanishes modulo \(p^{k+1}\) for \(k \ge 1\), so

\[f(r + tp^k) \equiv f(r) + t p^k f'(r) \pmod{p^{k+1}}.\]

Dividing by \(p^k\) — legal, since \(f(r)\) is already a multiple of \(p^k\) — the condition on \(t\) becomes the linear congruence

\[\frac{f(r)}{p^k} + t f'(r) \equiv 0 \pmod p.\]

Now Section 1 answers everything. If \(f'(r) \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\), the coefficient is invertible and there is exactly one lift. If the derivative vanishes modulo \(p\), the equation has either no solution or all \(p\) of them — so a root can die out or split into \(p\) children. Never assume uniqueness without checking the derivative; that assumption is the classic bug in prime-power root solvers.

5. Discrete logarithms: undoing exponentiation

Modular exponentiation is fast. Its inverse — given \(a\) and \(b\), find \(x\) with \(a^x \equiv b \pmod m\) — is believed to be hard, which is exactly why cryptography rests on it. In contests, \(m\) is small enough that a meet-in-the-middle search wins.

Baby-step giant-step

Let \(a\) be a unit and let \(N\) bound the exponent range (the order of \(a\), or \(m\) itself if you know nothing better). Write the unknown exponent in “mixed radix” with block size \(n = \lceil \sqrt N \rceil\):

\[x = q + nj, \qquad 0 \le q < n.\]

Then \(a^{q+nj} = b\) rearranges to

\[a^q = b \,(a^{-n})^j.\]

The left side takes only \(n\) values — precompute them all into a hash table (“baby steps”). The right side is a sequence you can walk one multiplication at a time (“giant steps”). The first match gives \(x = q + nj\). Cost: \(O(\sqrt N)\) time and memory.

Two habits make this reliable. Keep, for each stored value, the smallest baby exponent \(q\); and always verify a reported candidate by exponentiating. If the problem demands the smallest nonnegative solution, scan giant steps by increasing \(j\), discard candidates with \(q + nj \ge \operatorname{ord}(a)\), and the first verified match is minimal. Under any other scan order, collect the candidates and minimize explicitly.

The search can legitimately fail. \(a^x\) only ever visits the cyclic subgroup generated by \(a\). Modulo \(7\), the powers of \(2\) are \(1, 2, 4\), so \(2^x \equiv 3 \pmod 7\) has no solution even though \(3\) is perfectly invertible. “No solution” is an output, not a bug.

When the base shares a factor with the modulus

If \(\gcd(a,m) = g > 1\), then \(a\) is not a unit and \(a^{-n}\) does not exist, so plain BSGS cannot even start. The repair strips the shared primes out of the modulus while keeping track of what has been removed.

Maintain the invariant that, for the still-unexamined exponents \(x \ge t\),

\[k\,a^{\,x-t} \equiv b \pmod m,\]

starting from \(k = 1\), \(t = 0\). First test \(x = t\) directly by comparing \(k\) with \(b\). Otherwise let \(g = \gcd(a,m)\); if \(g \nmid b\) no remaining exponent can work, since the left side is a multiple of \(g\). If it does divide, divide \(m\) and \(b\) by \(g\), multiply \(k\) by \(a/g\), and increment \(t\). Each step is an honest integer division of the whole congruence, so the invariant survives.

Two facts finish the argument: the modulus shrinks by a factor of at least \(2\) each time, so at most \(O(\log m)\) steps occur (and if it reaches \(1\), the smallest remaining exponent is the answer); and once \(\gcd(a,m) = 1\) the accumulated \(k\) is a unit too, since every prime shared with the base has already been removed from the modulus. Then solve \(a^{\,x-t} = b/k\) with ordinary BSGS.

Always test exponent zero first, including the degenerate \(a = 0\), under the convention \(a^0 = 1\).

Pohlig–Hellman: exploit a smooth order

When the order \(L = \prod_i q_i^{e_i}\) of \(a\) is known and its prime factors are small, do not search the whole group. Raise both \(a\) and \(b\) to the power \(L/q_i^{e_i}\): this projects into the subgroup of order \(q_i^{e_i}\), where the logarithm is only the residue of \(x\) modulo \(q_i^{e_i}\). Inside that subgroup, recover the base-\(q_i\) digits of the logarithm one at a time — knowing the low \(j\) digits, divide away their contribution and raise to \(q_i^{e_i-1-j}\), which lands in the subgroup of order \(q_i\), where a tiny BSGS reveals the next digit. Finally, CRT the residues modulo each \(q_i^{e_i}\) back into \(x\) modulo \(L\).

Cost: \(O\!\left(\sum_i e_i(\log L + \sqrt{q_i})\right)\) group operations and \(O(\max_i \sqrt{q_i})\) reusable table space, excluding the factorization of \(L\). The largest prime factor dominates, but do not forget the exponentiation and digit-loop terms when the order has many factors.

6. Modular square roots and \(k\)-th roots

Now the question the chapter opened with. Given \(a\) and a prime \(p\), find \(x\) with

\[x^2 \equiv a \pmod p.\]

Over the reals you would call sqrt. Modulo \(p\) there is no order and no approximation to iterate, so the problem is genuinely different: it asks whether \(a\) lies in the image of the squaring map on \(\mathbb F_p\), and if so to invert that map. Such an \(a\) is called a quadratic residue.

It comes up more often than you would guess: solving a quadratic congruence, inverting a two-term recurrence, taking a square root of a formal power series whose constant term is not \(1\), and building complex-like extensions of \(\mathbb F_p\) all need it.

Step 1: does a root exist?

Euler’s criterion. For odd prime \(p\) and \(a \ne 0\),

\[a^{(p-1)/2} \equiv \begin{cases} +1 & a \text{ is a square},\\ -1 & a \text{ is not.}\end{cases}\]

Why: write \(a = g^t\) for a primitive root \(g\). Then \(a\) is a square exactly when \(t\) is even (if \(t = 2s\) then \(a = (g^s)^2\); conversely a square \(g^{2s}\) has even exponent because exponents are well defined modulo the even number \(p-1\)). Meanwhile \(g^{(p-1)/2}\) has order \(2\), so it equals \(-1\), the unique element of order two. Therefore \(a^{(p-1)/2} = (g^{(p-1)/2})^t = (-1)^t\), which is \(+1\) exactly for even \(t\).

Notice this also proves that exactly half of the \(p-1\) nonzero residues are squares — a fact we will need in a moment.

Step 2: Tonelli–Shanks, and the idea behind it

Sometimes existence is all you need, but usually you want the root itself. When \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) there is a one-liner: \(x = a^{(p+1)/4}\) works, because squaring it gives \(a^{(p+1)/2} = a \cdot a^{(p-1)/2} = a\). The exponent \((p+1)/4\) is an integer only in that case, and for \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) — including the contest favourite \(998244353\) — no such shortcut exists. Tonelli–Shanks is the algorithm that handles every odd prime.

The idea is worth stating before the mechanics, because the pseudocode looks mysterious otherwise. Write

\[p - 1 = q \cdot 2^s, \qquad q \text{ odd}.\]

Because \(p - 1\) has this shape, the multiplicative group splits into an odd-order part and a “\(2\)-power part” of size \(2^s\). The odd part is easy: raising to an odd power is invertible there, so square roots in it are a single exponentiation. All the difficulty is concentrated in the \(2\)-power part.

So the algorithm makes an initial guess that is correct up to an error living entirely in that \(2\)-power subgroup, and then repeatedly halves the order of the error until the error is \(1\). Concretely it maintains four values

\[r = a^{(q+1)/2}, \quad t = a^{q}, \quad c = z^{q}, \quad M = s,\]

where \(z\) is any quadratic non-residue, with the invariants

\[r^2 = a\,t, \qquad \operatorname{ord}(c) = 2^M, \qquad t^{2^{M-1}} = 1 .\]

Read the first invariant as “\(r\) is the answer, times an error \(t\)”. If ever \(t = 1\), then \(r^2 = a\) and we are done.

Otherwise find the least \(i\) with \(t^{2^i} = 1\); the invariants force \(1 \le i < M\). Set \(b = c^{2^{\,M-i-1}}\) and update

\[(r, t, c, M) \;\longleftarrow\; (rb,\; tb^2,\; b^2,\; i).\]

The relation \(r^2 = at\) survives, since both sides gain a factor \(b^2\). The new error has strictly smaller two-power order: \(t\) and \(b^2\) both have \(2^{i-1}\)-st power equal to \(-1\), so their product’s \(2^{i-1}\)-st power is \((-1)^2 = 1\). And \(c\) now has order exactly \(2^i\), restoring the second invariant with a smaller \(M\). Since \(M\) strictly decreases, the loop terminates — with \(t = 1\) and therefore a genuine square root.

Where the non-residue \(z\) comes from. It cannot be produced by a formula; we sample random values and test with Euler’s criterion. Half of all nonzero residues work, so the expected number of trials is \(2\). This makes Tonelli–Shanks a Las Vegas algorithm: always correct, randomized only in running time. Total cost \(O(\log p + s^2)\) modular multiplications, the \(s^2\) coming from the repeated squarings over decreasing \(M\).

NoteRun it by hand: \(\sqrt{10}\) modulo \(13\)

\(p - 1 = 12 = 3 \cdot 2^2\), so \(q = 3\), \(s = 2\). Check existence: \(10^{6} \equiv 1 \pmod{13}\), so a root exists. Take \(z = 2\), which is a non-residue since \(2^6 \equiv 12 \equiv -1\).

Initialize \(r = 10^{2} = 9\), \(t = 10^{3} = 12\), \(c = 2^{3} = 8\), \(M = 2\). Check the invariant: \(r^2 = 81 \equiv 3\) and \(a t = 10 \cdot 12 = 120 \equiv 3\). Good.

\(t \ne 1\), and the least \(i\) with \(t^{2^i} = 1\) is \(i = 1\) (since \(12^2 = 144 \equiv 1\)). So \(b = c^{2^{0}} = 8\), and the update gives \(r = 9 \cdot 8 = 72 \equiv 7\), \(t = 12 \cdot 64 \equiv 12 \cdot 12 \equiv 1\).

Done: \(r = 7\), and \(7^2 = 49 \equiv 10 \pmod{13}\). The other root is \(-7 \equiv 6\), and \(6^2 = 36 \equiv 10\) as well.

The excluded prime. For \(p = 2\) there is nothing to do: \(0\) and \(1\) are each their own square root. Euler’s criterion and Tonelli–Shanks assume an odd prime throughout.

Step 3: general \(k\)-th roots

For \(x^k \equiv a \pmod p\), first dispose of \(a = 0\): in a field its only root is \(x = 0\). For \(a \ne 0\), write everything in terms of a primitive root \(g\). Putting \(x = g^t\) and \(a = g^{\ell}\) turns the multiplicative equation into the linear congruence

\[k\,t \equiv \ell \pmod{p-1},\]

which Section 1 solves completely: solvable exactly when \(\gcd(k, p-1) \mid \ell\), with \(\gcd(k, p-1)\) solutions. This is conceptually complete, but it costs a discrete logarithm. Section 7 gives the cheaper route when you only need existence or one root.

7. Exponent towers and efficient \(k\)-th roots

Reducing enormous exponents

The rule everyone half-remembers is \(a^e \bmod m = a^{\,e \bmod \varphi(m)} \bmod m\). Unqualified, it is false. It requires \(\gcd(a,m) = 1\).

Counterexample, small enough to keep in mind: modulo \(4\), \(2^4 = 16 \equiv 0\), but \(4 \bmod \varphi(4) = 4 \bmod 2 = 0\), and \(2^0 = 1\). Reducing the exponent turned a multiple of the modulus’s prime into a non-multiple.

The correct local rule works prime power by prime power. For modulus \(p^r\):

  • if \(e = 0\), the answer is \(1\);
  • if \(p^r \mid a\) and \(e > 0\), the answer is \(0\);
  • otherwise write \(a = p^v u\) with \(p \nmid u\) and \(0 \le v < r\).
    • If \(v = 0\), then \(a\) is a unit and you may reduce the exponent modulo \(\lambda(p^r)\).
    • If \(v > 0\), then \(a^e \equiv 0 \pmod{p^r}\) as soon as \(e \ge \lceil r/v \rceil\); below that threshold \(e\) is tiny and can be used exactly.

Then CRT the prime-power answers together.

Look at what the third case needs: not the value of \(e\) modulo anything, but whether \(e\) exceeds a small threshold. This is why a tower evaluator must return more than a residue. Recursing through the Carmichael chain \(m, \lambda(m), \lambda(\lambda(m)), \ldots\) gives you \(e\) modulo the right number, but you must separately know whether the true \(e\) was large. Compute that with saturating arithmetic — a capped evaluator returning \(\min(E, C)\) for the true exponent \(E\) and a requested cap \(C\) — never by guessing from the reduced residue.

NoteWorked synthesis: \(6^{,2^{10}} \bmod 72\)

Factor \(72 = 8 \cdot 9\) and work locally.

Modulo \(8\): \(v_2(6) = 1\), so any exponent \(\ge 3\) gives \(0\). Modulo \(9\): \(v_3(6) = 1\), so any exponent \(\ge 2\) gives \(0\).

The exponent \(2^{10} = 1024\) clears both thresholds, so the answer is \(0\) modulo \(8\) and \(0\) modulo \(9\), hence \(0\) modulo \(72\) — obtained without ever touching the astronomical power.

The tempting shortcut fails loudly: \(\varphi(8) = 4\) and \(2^{10} \bmod 4 = 0\), so the naive reduction would compute \(6^0 = 1\) modulo \(8\). The information it threw away was not the exponent’s residue but its size.

For a height-\(h\) tower of nonnegative integer bases, fix a local convention \(0^0 = 1\) if zero bases are allowed, and give bases \(0\) and \(1\) their own branches. A capped evaluator stops multiplying as soon as it reaches the cap, so for base at least \(2\) only \(O(\log C)\) multiplications happen per level. Combine those size certificates with the modular recursion above; the total work depends on the tower height and the length of the factorization/period chain, never on the size of the tower’s value.

\(k\)-th roots without a discrete logarithm

For \(x^k = a\) in \(\mathbb F_p\) with \(a \ne 0\) and \(g = \gcd(k, p-1)\), a root exists if and only if

\[a^{(p-1)/g} = 1 .\]

Why: writing \(a = u^\beta\) for a generator \(u\), the left condition says \((p-1) \mid \beta (p-1)/g\), i.e. \(g \mid \beta\), which Section 6 already identified as the solvability condition. The point is that this test is a single exponentiation — no logarithm needed.

When \(g = 1\) you also get the root for free: \(k\) is invertible modulo \(p-1\), so \(x = a^{\,k^{-1} \bmod (p-1)}\). When \(g > 1\) the discrete-log reduction is valid but may be expensive, and — this is the trap — you cannot assume the relevant subgroup is smooth, because \(g\) may contain a large prime. Specialized root-extraction algorithms exist, but each needs its own proved contract; splitting off the gcd is not by itself an algorithm.

Contest translation: algorithm contracts

Task Required facts Cost excluding factorization
Linear congruence \(m>0\), test \(\gcd(a,m)\mid b\) \(O(\log m)\) integer divisions
CRT merge Positive moduli, consistency test, enough integer width One Euclidean solve per merge
Element order A unit, plus a factored known multiple of its order One modular power per attempted factor removal
BSGS A unit and an order bound \(L\) Expected \(O(\sqrt L)\) time, \(O(\sqrt L)\) memory
Tonelli–Shanks Odd prime, nonzero quadratic residue Expected \(O(\log p + s^2)\) modular multiplications
Hensel digit Integer polynomial, root modulo \(p^j\), \(j \ge 1\) One linear congruence per root branch

Sorting instead of hashing the BSGS table adds a logarithmic factor. Finding a primitive root modulo \(p\) needs the factorization of \(p-1\) and the tests \(g^{(p-1)/q} \ne 1\) for every distinct prime \(q \mid p-1\); those tests suffice because every proper divisor of \(p-1\) divides at least one \((p-1)/q\). The cost also includes however many candidates you try.

Contest checklist and pitfalls

  • Normalize negative residues: C++ % can return a negative value.
  • Never use Fermat inversion unless the modulus is prime and the value nonzero.
  • Guard products and lcms with __int128, and check the promised bound before casting back.
  • CRT consistency is a divisibility test. Pairwise coprimality is a special case, not the requirement.
  • Distinguish a primitive root of the whole group from an \(n\)-th root of unity; NTT needs the latter.
  • In BSGS, decide whether the smallest solution is required, and handle \(x = 0\), \(a = 0\), and non-coprime bases explicitly.
  • Hensel’s zero-derivative branch and the prime \(2\) need separate analysis.
  • \(m = 1\) is a legal input to almost every routine here and breaks most of them; special-case it.

Exercises with solutions

1. Roots can branch — concept check

Lift all roots of \(x^2 = 1\) modulo \(4\) to modulus \(8\). Why does the simple-root Hensel rule not apply?

TipHint and solution

Hint: compute the derivative modulo \(2\) before trying to lift.

The roots modulo \(4\) are \(1\) and \(3\). Their candidate lifts are \(1, 5\) and \(3, 7\), and all four square to \(1\) modulo \(8\) — so each root split into two.

That is allowed precisely because \(f'(x) = 2x\) vanishes modulo \(2\), putting us in the degenerate branch where a root has either \(0\) or \(p\) lifts. The other outcome occurs too: \(x^2 = 3\) has the root \(1\) modulo \(2\), but no root at all modulo \(4\).

2. Root counting through exponents — derivation

Find all solutions of \(x^4 = 4\) modulo \(7\), and explain why there are exactly two.

TipHint and solution

Hint: convert to exponents using a primitive root.

The primitive root \(3\) has powers \(3^1,\ldots,3^6 = 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1\), so \(4 = 3^4\). Writing \(x = 3^t\), the equation becomes \(4t \equiv 4 \pmod 6\), i.e. \(2t \equiv 2 \pmod 3\), i.e. \(t \equiv 1 \pmod 3\): so \(t \in \{1, 4\}\) and \(x \in \{3, 4\}\).

The count \(\gcd(4, 6) = 2\) matches the general rule. Zero is not a root since \(4 \ne 0\).

3. Synchronization with a deadline — modelling

A machine is ready at times \(t \equiv 1 \pmod 6\) and another at \(t \equiv 3 \pmod 8\). Find the first common ready time that is at least \(50\).

TipHint and solution

Hint: CRT gives a residue class; the deadline picks a member of it.

CRT gives \(t \equiv 19 \pmod{24}\), i.e. \(t = 19 + 24k\). Imposing \(19 + 24k \ge 50\) gives \(k \ge \lceil 31/24 \rceil = 2\), so the answer is \(t = 67\).

Reporting the normalized residue \(19\) would answer a different question. For a general window \([A,B]\), the feasible \(k\) range is \([\lceil (A-19)/24 \rceil, \lfloor (B-19)/24 \rfloor]\), which is also how you count solutions rather than just find one.

4. Count valid exponents without a logarithm — synthesis

For a prime \(p\) and a unit \(a\) of known order \(d\), count the integers \(0 \le x < X\) with \(a^{kx} = 1\), where \(k \ge 1\) and \(X \ge 0\).

TipHint and solution

Hint: the target is the identity, so only divisibility matters.

\(a^{kx} = 1\) holds exactly when \(d \mid kx\). Writing \(D = d/\gcd(d,k)\), this is equivalent to \(D \mid x\). For \(X = 0\) the count is \(0\); otherwise it is \(1 + \lfloor (X-1)/D \rfloor\).

No discrete logarithm is needed — that is the point. With \(a = 2\), \(p = 7\), \(d = 3\), \(k = 2\), \(X = 10\): \(D = 3\) and the valid exponents are \(0, 3, 6, 9\), four of them, matching \(1 + \lfloor 9/3 \rfloor\).

Summary and reference sheet

  • Division modulo \(m\) is multiplication by an inverse, and inverses exist exactly for units. Modulo a prime that means “nonzero”; modulo a composite it means “coprime to \(m\)”.
  • \(ax \equiv b \pmod m\) is solvable iff \(\gcd(a,m) \mid b\), and then has exactly \(\gcd(a,m)\) solutions modulo \(m\).
  • Two congruences merge iff \(\gcd(m,n)\) divides the difference of the residues; the merged constraint lives modulo \(\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\).
  • Modulo a prime, the nonzero residues form a cyclic group, which is why multiplicative equations turn into linear congruences on exponents.
  • Exponents may be reduced modulo an element’s order only when that element is a unit; otherwise use the prime-power valuation threshold and keep a saturating record of the exponent’s size.
  • Valuations convert “divisible by a big prime power” into arithmetic on exponents; Kummer reads \(v_p\binom nk\) off base-\(p\) carries.
  • Hensel picks the next base-\(p\) digit by solving a linear congruence; a vanishing derivative means \(0\) or \(p\) lifts, never “the” lift.
  • Tonelli–Shanks takes square roots modulo any odd prime by isolating the error inside the \(2\)-power subgroup and halving its order each round; for \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), one exponentiation suffices instead.
  • Always match the required output — existence, one root, all roots, the smallest exponent — against what your chosen algorithm actually guarantees.
