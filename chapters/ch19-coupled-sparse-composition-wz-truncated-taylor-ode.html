19.1 Non-Commutative Weyl & Shift Operator Algebras (\(K[x]\langle D_x \rangle\) and \(K[n]\langle E_n \rangle\))

In Chapter 18, we converted linear differential equations into coefficient recurrences by extracting \([x^n]\) term-by-term. When expressions grow more intricate—such as composed differential operators, iterated Euler operators, or multivariate creative telescoping—manual coefficient extraction becomes error-prone. The clean solution is to package differentiation, multiplication by \(x\), index multiplication by \(n\), and index shifting \(n \mapsto n + 1\) as elements of two non-commutative polynomial rings of operators.

1. The Weyl Algebra \(K[x]\langle D_x \rangle\) and the Euler Subalgebra \(K[x]\langle \vartheta \rangle\)

Consider a formal power series \(F(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} f_n x^n \in K[[x]]\). Two elementary linear operators act on \(K[[x]]\):

  • The Multiplication Operator \(x\): shifts powers up via \((x \cdot F)(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} f_n x^{n+1}\).
  • The Formal Derivative Operator \(D_x = \frac{d}{dx}\): shifts powers down via \((D_x F)(x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} n f_n x^{n-1}\).

Because differentiating a product \(x F(x)\) triggers the Leibniz product rule, \(D_x\) and \(x\) do not commute. Applying \(D_x x - x D_x\) to any test series \(F(x)\) gives: \[D_x(x F(x)) - x (D_x F(x)) = \big(F(x) + x F'(x)\big) - x F'(x) = F(x).\]

ImportantDefinition 19.1 (The Weyl Algebra \(K[x]\langle D_x \rangle\))

The Weyl algebra \(\mathcal{W}_x = K[x]\langle D_x \rangle\) is the associative \(K\)-algebra generated by \(x\) and \(D_x\) subject to the fundamental commutation relation: \[D_x x - x D_x = 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad D_x x = x D_x + 1.\] By induction on \(s \ge 1\) and \(k \ge 1\), moving \(D_x^k\) past \(x^s\) obeys the generalized Leibniz operator identity: \[D_x^k x^s = \sum_{j=0}^{\min(k, s)} \binom{k}{j} s^{\underline{j}} \, x^{s-j} D_x^{k-j}.\]

While \(D_x\) shifts degrees down by \(1\), the Euler (homogeneity) operator \(\vartheta = x D_x\) preserves every monomial’s degree: \(\vartheta(x^n) = n x^n\). How does \(\vartheta\) commute with \(x^s\)?

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Commuting \(\vartheta\) Past \(x^s\)
  • The Diagnostic Signal: You have an operator expression mixing powers of \(x\) and powers of \(\vartheta = x D_x\), such as \(\vartheta^m \big(x^s F(x)\big)\), and you want to move \(x^s\) to the far left so all \(\vartheta\)’s act directly on \(F(x)\).
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Apply \(\vartheta = x D_x\) to \(x^s F(x)\) by the product rule: \[\vartheta\big(x^s F(x)\big) = x \frac{d}{dx}\big(x^s F(x)\big) = s x^s F(x) + x^{s+1} F'(x) = x^s (\vartheta + s) F(x).\] Thus pulling \(x^s\) to the left of \(\vartheta\) simply shifts \(\vartheta \gets \vartheta + s\)! Repeating this \(m\) times gives for any polynomial \(P(\vartheta) \in K[\vartheta]\): \[P(\vartheta) \, x^s = x^s \, P(\vartheta + s).\]

2. The Ore Shift Algebra \(K[n]\langle E_n \rangle\)

Now view the coefficient sequence \((f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}\) (with \(f_n = 0\) for \(n < 0\)) as the object of study. Two basic operators act on sequences:

  • The Index Operator \(n\): multiplies the \(n\)-th term by \(n\), mapping \((f_n) \mapsto (n f_n)\).
  • The Forward Shift Operator \(E_n\): increments the index by \(1\), mapping \((f_n) \mapsto (f_{n+1})\). Its inverse \(E_n^{-1}\) is the backward shift \((f_n) \mapsto (f_{n-1})\).

Applying \(E_n\) to the sequence \((n f_n)\) evaluates the product at \(n + 1\), giving \((n + 1) f_{n+1} = ((n + 1) E_n f)_n\).

ImportantDefinition 19.2 (The Shift Algebra \(K[n]\langle E_n, E_n^{-1} \rangle\))

The Ore shift algebra \(\mathcal{S}_n = K[n]\langle E_n, E_n^{-1} \rangle\) is the non-commutative \(K\)-algebra generated by \(n\) and \(E_n^{\pm 1}\) subject to the commutation rules: \[E_n \, n = (n + 1) E_n, \qquad E_n^{-1} \, n = (n - 1) E_n^{-1}.\] More generally, for any polynomial \(P(n) \in K[n]\) and integer \(s \in \mathbb{Z}\): \[E_n^s \, P(n) = P(n + s) \, E_n^s.\]

3. The Fourier–Laplace Operator Dictionary (\(K[x]\langle \vartheta \rangle \longleftrightarrow K[n]\langle E_n^{-1} \rangle\))

Every linear differential equation on \(F(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} f_n x^n\) can be multiplied by \(x^m\) to clear negative shifts, placing it in \(K[x]\langle \vartheta \rangle\). Look at how the generators \(x\) and \(\vartheta\) act on the \(n\)-th coefficient \([x^n]\):

  1. Extracting \([x^n]\) after multiplying by \(x^s\) yields \([x^n]\big(x^s F(x)\big) = f_{n-s} = (E_n^{-s} f)_n\).
  2. Extracting \([x^n]\) after applying \(P(\vartheta)\) yields \([x^n]\big(P(\vartheta) F(x)\big) = P(n) f_n\).

Notice that in \(K[x]\langle \vartheta \rangle\), we have \(P(\vartheta) x^s = x^s P(\vartheta + s)\), while in \(K[n]\langle E_n^{-1} \rangle\), we have \(P(n) E_n^{-s} = E_n^{-s} P(n - s)\)! Thus the map sending \(x \mapsto E_n^{-1}\) and \(\vartheta \mapsto n\) (when operators are written in normal form with \(x^s\) on the left and \(P(\vartheta)\) on the right, or vice versa using the commutation law) provides an exact algebraic dictionary:

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 4\)): Operator Translation for Large Schröder Numbers

Recall that the Large Schröder OGF \(S(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} S_n x^n = \frac{1 - x - \sqrt{1 - 6x + x^2}}{2x}\) satisfies the linear ODE (obtained from \((1 - 6x + x^2) W'(x) = (-3 + x) W(x)\) on \(W(x) = \sqrt{1 - 6x + x^2} = 1 - x - 2x S(x)\)): \[x(1 - 6x + x^2) S'(x) + (1 - 3x) S(x) = 1 - 2x.\] Let us translate the left-hand side into \(K[x]\langle \vartheta \rangle\) and then into \(K[n]\langle E_n^{-1} \rangle\) in three single-step lines:

  1. Since \(x S'(x) = \vartheta S(x)\), rewrite the differential operator in normal form with powers of \(x\) on the left: \[L(x, \vartheta) = (1 - 6x + x^2)\vartheta + (1 - 3x) = (\vartheta + 1) - 3x(2\vartheta + 1) + x^2 \vartheta.\]
  2. Apply the dictionary \(x^s P(\vartheta) \mapsto P(n - s) E_n^{-s}\) at degree \(n \ge 2\) (where \([x^n](1 - 2x) = 0\)): \[(n + 1) S_n - 3\big(2(n - 1) + 1\big) S_{n-1} + (n - 2) S_{n-2} = 0.\]
  3. Rearrange into the P-recursive recurrence for \(n \ge 2\): \[(n + 1) S_n = 3(2n - 1) S_{n-1} - (n - 2) S_{n-2}.\]
  • Trace (\(n = 0 \dots 4\)): With \(S_0 = 1, S_1 = 2\):
    • \(n = 2\): \(3 S_2 = 3(3)(2) - 0 \cdot S_0 = 18 \implies S_2 = 6\).
    • \(n = 3\): \(4 S_3 = 3(5)(6) - 1(2) = 88 \implies S_3 = 22\).
    • \(n = 4\): \(5 S_4 = 3(7)(22) - 2(6) = 450 \implies S_4 = 90\).

19.2 Zeilberger’s Creative Telescoping, WZ Pairs & EI’s Differential-Residue Binomial Sum Engine (Engine 3)

In Chapter 6, we met two classic engines for evaluating a definite binomial sum \(S_n = \sum_{k} F(n, k)\):

  1. Engine 1 (Snake Oil): Multiply by \(z^n\), swap \(\sum_n\) and \(\sum_k\), and sum the inner series over \(n\).
  2. Engine 2 (Egorychev / Residue Extraction): Write \(F(n, k) = [x^{g(n, k)}] P(x, n, k)\) so the sum over \(k\) becomes a geometric or binomial series inside \([x^m]\).

What happens when neither engine collapses the sum into a closed form? Consider the \(c\)-Delannoy polynomial \[D_n(c) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} \binom{n+k}{k} c^k,\] or Apéry’s sum \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}^2 \binom{n+k}{k}^2\). Here \(n\) appears in three places at once, and \(D_n(c)\) has no hypergeometric closed form—yet \(D_0(c), \dots, D_N(c)\) can still be computed in \(O(N)\) time because \(D_n(c)\) is P-recursive! We now develop the two complementary engines that compile such sums directly into \(O(N)\) recurrences.

1. Zeilberger’s Creative Telescoping & WZ Certificate Pairs

Suppose \(F(n, k)\) is a bivariate hypergeometric term, meaning that both shift ratios \(\frac{F(n+1, k)}{F(n, k)}\) and \(\frac{F(n, k+1)}{F(n, k)}\) are rational functions of \((n, k)\) over \(K\). If we try to telescope \(F(n+1, k) - F(n, k) = G(n, k+1) - G(n, k)\) directly, no hypergeometric \(G(n, k)\) may exist.

Zeilberger’s insight (1990) is to take a polynomial linear combination of shifts \(F(n, k), F(n+1, k), \dots, F(n+r, k)\) along \(n\) until their combination becomes an exact first difference \(\Delta_k G(n, k) = G(n, k+1) - G(n, k)\) along \(k\)!

ImportantTheorem 19.1 (Zeilberger’s Creative Telescoping & WZ Certificate)

Let \(F(n, k)\) be a proper hypergeometric term with natural compact support (so \(F(n, k) = 0\) for \(k < 0\) and \(k > n + O(1)\)). Then there exist an order \(r \ge 1\), polynomials \(p_0(n), p_1(n), \dots, p_r(n) \in K[n]\) (independent of \(k\)!), and a rational certificate \(R(n, k) \in K(n, k)\) such that with \(G(n, k) = R(n, k) F(n, k)\), the WZ difference identity holds for all \((n, k)\): \[\sum_{j=0}^{r} p_j(n) \, F(n + j, k) = G(n, k + 1) - G(n, k).\] Summing both sides over all \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\), the right-hand side telescopes to \(0\) on natural support, proving that \(S_n = \sum_{k} F(n, k)\) satisfies the homogeneous P-recursive recurrence: \[\sum_{j=0}^{r} p_j(n) \, S_{n+j} = 0.\]

WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Non-Natural Summation Boundaries (\(\sum_{k=0}^m \binom{n}{k}\) with \(m < n\))

The Trap: Students sometimes apply a homogeneous recurrence derived from creative telescoping to a sum whose upper limit \(m\) cuts off the summand before \(F(n, k)\) naturally vanishes (for example, the partial row sum \(S(n, m) = \sum_{k=0}^m \binom{n}{k}\) for a fixed \(m < n\)). Why It Fails: Pascal’s identity gives the WZ pair \(F(n, k) = \binom{n}{k}\) and \(G(n, k) = -\binom{n}{k-1}\), satisfying: \[F(n + 1, k) - 2 F(n, k) = \binom{n}{k - 1} - \binom{n}{k} = G(n, k + 1) - G(n, k).\] When summed over natural support \(k \in [0, n+1]\), the boundary values \(G(n, n+2) = -\binom{n}{n+1} = 0\) and \(G(n, 0) = -\binom{n}{-1} = 0\) vanish, giving \(S_{n+1} - 2 S_n = 0 \implies S_n = 2^n\). However, if we truncate the sum at \(k = m < n\), summing from \(k = 0\) to \(m\) leaves a non-zero upper boundary term: \[\sum_{k=0}^{m} \big(F(n+1, k) - 2 F(n, k)\big) = G(n, m + 1) - G(n, 0) = -\binom{n}{m}.\] The Remedy: Always evaluate \(G(n, k_{\max} + 1) - G(n, k_{\min})\) explicitly! For truncated limits, creative telescoping produces an inhomogeneous P-recurrence \(S(n+1, m) = 2 S(n, m) - \binom{n}{m}\).

2. EI’s Differential-Residue Binomial Sum Engine (Engine 3)

Finding the rational certificate \(R(n, k)\) by hand can be algebraically tedious. In competitive programming, Elegia (EI, 载谭 Binomial Sums) popularized a far more direct algorithm—Engine 3—that replaces Gosper’s discrete certificate search with formal residue integration by parts in \(K((x))\).

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: EI’s Differential-Residue Method
  • The Diagnostic Signal: You have a definite binomial sum \(S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} \binom{n+k}{k} c^k\) (or any sum where \(n\) enters via binomial factors \(\binom{n}{k}, \binom{n+k}{k}, \binom{2n-k}{n}\)) and you want the P-recursive recurrence for \(S_n\) by hand in 3 lines of algebra.

Part A: Coupled \(r\)-State Sparse Composition \(H(x) = F(P(x)) \bmod x^N\) in \(O(rNK)\)

In Chapter 2, we learned how to compute \(P(x)^\alpha\), \(\exp(P(x))\), and \(\ln(P(x))\) in \(O(NK)\) time when \(P(x)\) is a \(K\)-sparse polynomial (having at most \(K\) non-zero coefficients) by differentiating once: for example, \(H(x) = \exp(P(x))\) satisfies the first-order ODE \(H'(x) = P'(x) H(x)\).

What if the outer series \(F(y)\) is a general D-finite series of order \(r \ge 2\), satisfying an order-\(r\) linear ODE \[F^{(r)}(y) = \sum_{i=0}^{r-1} a_i(y) \, F^{(i)}(y)\] with polynomial (or rational) coefficients \(a_i(y)\), and we want to compute the composition \(H(x) = F(P(x)) \bmod x^N\) for a \(K\)-sparse polynomial \(P(x)\) with \(P(0) = 0\)?

WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: The \(1/P'(x)\) Sparsity-Destruction Trap

The Trap: If you try to form a single order-\(r\) ODE for \(H(x) = F(P(x))\) using the chain-rule operator \(\mathcal{D}_P = \frac{1}{P'(x)} \frac{d}{dx}\) from Chapter 18.3, two disasters occur when \(r \ge 2\):

  1. Valuation Singularity at \(x = 0\): For \(r = 2\), clearing denominators in \(\mathcal{D}_P^2 H + a_1(P)\mathcal{D}_P H + a_0(P) H = 0\) produces: \[P'(x) H''(x) - \big(P''(x) + a_1(P(x)) P'(x)^2\big) H'(x) - a_0(P(x)) P'(x)^3 H(x) = 0.\] Whenever \([x^1]P(x) = 0\) (for example, \(P(x) = x^2 + x^5\), so \(\operatorname{val}(P') = 1\)), the leading coefficient \(P'(x)\) vanishes at \(x = 0\), creating an apparent singularity where the indicial polynomial vanishes!
  2. Sparsity Explosion: Even if \([x^1]P(x) \neq 0\), multiplying by \(P'(x)^r\) expands a \(K\)-sparse polynomial into \(O(K^r)\) terms (or \(O(N)\) terms if you invert \(P'(x)\)), destroying the \(O(NK)\) runtime!

The Remedy (Coupled \(r\)-State First-Order System): Never eliminate \(F'(P(x)), \dots, F^{(r-1)}(P(x))\)! Instead, maintain all \(r\) derivatives evaluated at \(P(x)\) simultaneously as a coupled first-order system.

Part B: EI 科技 Truncated Taylor ODEs (\(A(x) F_{<N}' + B(x) F_{<N} = C(x) - R_N(x)\))

Suppose an infinite formal power series \(F(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n x^n\) satisfies a simple first-order linear ODE \[A(x) F'(x) + B(x) F(x) = C(x)\] where \(A(x), B(x), C(x) \in K[x]\) are low-degree polynomials. In many combinatorics problems, however, we do not work with the infinite series \(F(x)\)—we work with its degree-\((N-1)\) truncation: \[F_{<N}(x) = F(x) \bmod x^N = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f_n x^n.\] For example:

  • We may need the Taylor shift \(F_{<N}(x + c)\) (or \(F_{<N}(x - 1)\)) to convert between binomial moments, falling factorials, or inclusion-exclusion states. Computing \(F_{<N}(x + c)\) via Chapter 6’s reversed convolution costs \(O(N \log N)\) time via NTT.
  • Or we may need to integrate products of truncated exponentials \(S_{<a}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{a-1} \frac{x^j}{j!} = e^x \bmod x^a\) in continuous-time Poissonization (UOJ 514).

Elegia’s Breakthrough (EI 科技): If \(F(x)\) satisfies a sparse polynomial ODE, then its truncation \(F_{<N}(x)\) satisfies the exact same left-hand differential operator with a tiny polynomial remainder \(R_N(x)\) on the right-hand side! And because a polynomial ODE is preserved under any affine shift \(x \gets u + c\), we can unroll the shifted polynomial \(G(u) = F_{<N}(u + c)\) directly in \(O(N)\) time with zero NTTs!

    1. Since \(\deg F_{<N} \le N - 1\) and \(\deg F_{<N}' \le N - 2\), the left-hand side is a polynomial of degree at most \(N - 1 + d\), where \(d = \max(\deg A - 1, \deg B)\).
    2. Therefore, \(R_N(x)\) is a sparse polynomial localized in degrees \([N - 1, N + d - 1]\) depending on at most \(d + 1\) boundary coefficients \(f_N, f_{N+1}, \dots, f_{N+d}\)!

19.4 Motivating Sub-Section: Singularity Analysis Intuition (\([x^n]F(x) \sim \frac{C}{\Gamma(\alpha)} R^{-n} n^{\alpha - 1}\)) & Holonomic BSGS

Throughout Chapters 18 and 19, we have treated generating functions purely as algebraic objects in \(K[[x]]\). Yet there is a striking correspondence between the leading polynomial \(q_m(x)\) of a D-finite ODE \[q_m(x) F^{(m)}(x) + q_{m-1}(x) F^{(m-1)}(x) + \dots + q_0(x) F(x) = 0\] and the asymptotic growth of the coefficients \(f_n = [x^n] F(x)\) over \(\mathbb{C}\).

1. Why the Dominant Singularity \(x = R\) Controls Both Asymptotics and the ODE Leading Term

Suppose \(F(x) \in \mathbb{C}[[x]]\) has a finite radius of convergence \(R > 0\), and near its dominant singularity \(x = R\) (the singularity closest to the origin in \(\mathbb{C}\)) it behaves algebraically like \[F(x) \sim C \left(1 - \frac{x}{R}\right)^{-\alpha} \qquad (x \to R^-)\] for some exponent \(\alpha \notin \{0, -1, -2, \dots\}\). Expanding \((1 - x/R)^{-\alpha}\) by the generalized binomial theorem and applying Stirling’s approximation \(\binom{n + \alpha - 1}{n} = \frac{\Gamma(n + \alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha) \Gamma(n + 1)} \sim \frac{n^{\alpha - 1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\) yields the Flajolet–Odlyzko Transfer Principle: \[[x^n] F(x) \sim \frac{C}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \, R^{-n} \, n^{\alpha - 1} \qquad (n \to \infty).\]

Now look at what happens when we differentiate \((1 - x/R)^{-\alpha}\): each differentiation multiplies by \((1 - x/R)^{-1}\), increasing the pole order at \(x = R\) by \(1\)! Therefore, in any linear ODE \(\sum_{j=0}^m q_j(x) F^{(j)}(x) = 0\) satisfied by \(F(x)\):

  1. Roots of \(q_m(x)\) Are the Singularities of \(F(x)\): For the highest derivative \(F^{(m)}(x) \sim C' (1 - x/R)^{-\alpha - m}\) to cancel against lower derivatives \(F^{(m-1)}(x) \sim C'' (1 - x/R)^{-\alpha - m + 1}\), the leading polynomial \(q_m(x)\) must vanish at \(x = R\) (i.e., \((1 - x/R) \mid q_m(x)\))! For example, in Catalan/central binomial ODEs where \(R = 1/4\), the leading polynomial always contains the factor \((1 - 4x)\); in Delannoy/Schröder ODEs where \(R = 3 - 2\sqrt{2}\), it always contains \((1 - 6x + x^2)\).
  2. Asymptotic Ratio of the P-Recurrence: Dividing the P-recursive relation \(\sum_{s} P_s(n) f_{n+s} = 0\) by \(n^{\deg P}\) and taking \(n \to \infty\) yields the characteristic polynomial \(q_m(x)\) evaluated at \(x = R\), confirming that \(\frac{f_{n+1}}{f_n} \to R^{-1}\left(1 + \frac{\alpha - 1}{n} + O(n^{-2})\right)\).

2. Outlook: \(O(\sqrt{N} \log N)\) Holonomic Baby-Step Giant-Step (Bostan–Chudnovsky–Chudnovsky)

When a sequence \((f_n)\) satisfies an order-\(r\) P-recursive recurrence \(p_r(n) f_{n+r} + \dots + p_0(n) f_n = 0\) and we need a single term \(f_N \bmod p\) for \(N \le 10^9 < p\), \(O(N)\) linear unrolling is too slow. Rewriting the step-\(n\) transition as a \(r \times r\) companion matrix \(M(n) \in \operatorname{Mat}_r(K[n])\) (after clearing denominators into a scalar factorial product \(\prod_{n=0}^{N-1} p_r(n)\)) reduces \(f_N\) to the matrix product: \[P(0, N) = M(N - 1) M(N - 2) \cdots M(1) M(0).\] By setting \(B = \lfloor \sqrt{N} \rfloor\), constructing the block matrix \(M_B(x) = M(x + B - 1) \cdots M(x + 1) M(x)\) of polynomial degree \(O(B)\) via a binary product tree in \(O(r^3 \sqrt{N} \log^2 N)\) time, and evaluating \(M_B(x)\) at the giant-step points \(x = 0, B, 2B, \dots, (B - 1)B\) using the subproduct-tree multipoint evaluation and sampling-point shift engines that we will build in Chapter 20, we can evaluate any P-recursive sequence (and \(N! \bmod p\)) at \(N \le 10^9\) in \(O(\sqrt{N} \log N)\) time!

Stepping Stone 1 (#242) — Numbered Colorful Balls

  • Source: yukicoder No. 1939 — Numbered Colorful Balls
  • Prerequisites: Lagrange Inversion Formula (Ch 11), \(O(NK)\) Sparse Power ODE (Ch 02 / §19.3A)

Problem Statement

You have balls of \(M\) distinct colors. In the nested colorful-ball bracket grammar of yukicoder 1939, each color \(i \in \{1, 2, \dots, M\}\) forms atomic blocks of length \(l_i\) where each of the \(l_i\) slots carries a non-empty valid sequence generated by \(x F(x)\). Constraints

  • \(1 \le N \le 10^5\), \(1 \le M \le 100\), \(1 \le l_i \le N\).
  • Modulus: \(p = 998244353\).
  • Target complexity: \(O(N M)\) time and \(O(N)\) space with zero NTTs (faster and simpler than \(O(N \log N)\) polynomial ln/exp).

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

  • \(n = 4\): \(f_4 = 38\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Notice that \(x\) and \(F(x)\) appear on the right-hand side only through the product \(U(x) = x F(x)\)! Multiply both sides of the equation by \(x\) to isolate \(U(x)\). How can you write the equation in the Lagrange inversion form \(\frac{U(x)}{\phi(U(x))} = x\)?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Multiplying by \(x\) gives \(U(x) = \frac{x}{1 - \sum_{i=1}^M U(x)^{l_i}}\), which rearranges to: \[U(x) \left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{M} U(x)^{l_i}\right) = x.\] Define the sparse polynomial \(Q(y) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^M y^{l_i}\), which has at most \(M + 1 \le 101\) non-zero terms. In terms of \(\phi(y) = Q(y)^{-1}\), we have \(U(x) = x \phi(U(x))\). Applying Lagrange Inversion to extract \([x^N] F(x) = [x^{N+1}] U(x)\) gives: \[[x^N] F(x) = \frac{1}{N + 1} [y^N] \phi(y)^{N+1} = \frac{1}{N + 1} [y^N] Q(y)^{-(N+1)}.\]

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

  4. Complexity: \(O(n)\) preprocessing time, \(O(1)\) time per query, and \(O(n)\) space.

Using \(\binom{n - i}{r} = \binom{n - i - 1}{r} + \binom{n - i - 1}{r - 1}\), split the sum for \(g_i\): \[\begin{aligned} g_i &= \sum_{r=0}^{m - i} \binom{n - i - 1}{r} (-x)^r - x \sum_{r=1}^{m - i} \binom{n - i - 1}{r - 1} (-x)^{r - 1} \\ &= \left( g_{i+1} + \binom{n - i - 1}{m - i} (-x)^{m - i} \right) - x \, g_{i+1} \\ &= (1 - x) g_{i+1} + \binom{n - i - 1}{m - i} (-x)^{m - i}, \end{aligned}\] with base case \(g_m = \binom{n - m}{0} (-x)^0 = 1\)!

  • Prerequisites: Connected-Component Inclusion-Exclusion (Ch 08), Divide-and-Conquer Product Tree (Ch 01), Polynomial Taylor Shift (Ch 06 / §19.3B), Logarithmic Derivative / Power Sum Rational Merge (Ch 02)

Problem Statement

There are \(N\) distinguishable balls numbered \(1, 2, \dots, N\), where ball \(i\) has color \(a_i\).   • Modulus: \(p = 998244353\).
  • Target complexity: \(O((N + M) \log^2(N + M))\) time and \(O(N + M)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N = 3, M = 3, a = (1, 1, 2)\))

Of the \(3! = 6\) permutations of \((1_a, 1_b, 2)\):

  • \(2\) permutations ((1_a, 2, 1_b) and (1_b, 2, 1_a)) have \(0\) equal-color adjacencies, so \(c(P) = 3\) runs.

  • \(4\) permutations ((1_a, 1_b, 2), (1_b, 1_a, 2), (2, 1_a, 1_b), (2, 1_b, 1_a)) have \(1\) equal-color adjacency, so \(c(P) = 2\) runs. Thus:

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Split the problem into two stages:

  1. First, for each \(v = 1, 2, \dots, N\), count the number of permutations \(w_{N-v}\) that have colorfulness \(c(P) = v\) (i.e., \(k = N - v\) adjacent equal-color pairs).

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Suppose color \(r\) appears \(k_r\) times (\(\sum_r k_r = N\)). If we choose \(j_r \in [0, k_r - 1]\) adjacent equal-color pairs of color \(r\) to “contract” into \(k_r - j_r\) directed chains, there are \(\binom{k_r - 1}{j_r}\) ways to choose where the \(j_r\) merges occur among the \(k_r - 1\) internal boundaries and \(\frac{k_r!}{(k_r - j_r)!}\) ways to order the \(k_r\) distinguishable balls of color \(r\) relative to the \(k_r - j_r\) chains. Thus, define the per-color polynomial of degree \(k_r - 1\): \[Q_r(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k_r - 1} \binom{k_r - 1}{j} \frac{k_r!}{(k_r - j)!} x^j.\] Multiplying \(Q(x) = \prod_r Q_r(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} q_j x^j\) via a divide-and-conquer product tree gives the number of ways \(a_j = (N - j)! q_j\) to form at least \(j\) forced equal-color adjacencies!

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Per-Color Chain-Contraction Polynomial \(Q_r(x)\): Group the \(N\) balls by color into frequencies \(k_1, k_2, \dots, k_C\) with \(\sum_{r=1}^C k_r = N\). Within color \(r\), contracting \(j\) adjacent pairs merges the \(k_r\) balls into \(b = k_r - j\) ordered chains. Since there are \(k_r!\) ways to order the \(k_r\) balls, \(\binom{k_r - 1}{b - 1} = \binom{k_r - 1}{j}\) ways to split them into \(b\) non-empty chains, and \(\frac{1}{b!} = \frac{1}{(k_r - j)!}\) EGF weight for interleaving the \(b\) chains of color \(r\) with chains of other colors, the polynomial \[Q_r(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k_r - 1} \binom{k_r - 1}{j} \frac{k_r!}{(k_r - j)!} x^j\] encodes the EGF weight of contracting \(j\) adjacencies of color \(r\).
  2. For \(N = 1\), there is \(m = 1\) sequence \(A_1 = (a, b, c)\) of length \(3\). The front element is always the only available choice, so every permutation of \((1, 2, 3)\) is generable: \(\text{Ans}_1 = 3! = 6\).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Imagine running the merge algorithm on \(A_1, \dots, A_N\). A prefix-max block of size \(L \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) has its maximum fixed at the front, so it has \((L - 1)!\) valid internal orderings and EGF weight \(\frac{(L - 1)!}{L!} x^L = \frac{x^L}{L}\). Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Bijection Between Mergeable Permutations and Constrained Prefix-Max Blocks: Break any permutation \(P\) of \((1, \dots, 3N)\) into blocks at its left-to-right maxima: a new block starts at \(P_j\) whenever \(P_j > \max_{i < j} P_i\). By definition, each block is led by its maximum element, and the leading elements of the blocks appear in strictly increasing order. Conversely, if we partition each of the \(N\) triplets \(A_i\) at its own left-to-right maxima, the resulting sub-blocks across all \(N\) triplets are merged by the greedy minimum-front algorithm in strictly increasing order of their leading elements! Since each triplet of length \(3\) partitions into block sizes \((3)\), \((2, 1)\), or \((1, 1, 1)\), a collection of prefix-max blocks of total size \(3N\) can be packed into \(N\) triplets if and only if:

    • Every block has size \(L \in \{1, 2, 3\}\), and
    • \(c_1 \ge c_2\), where \(c_L\) is the number of blocks of size \(L\) (note that \(c_1 + 2c_2 + 3c_3 = 3N\) automatically implies \(c_1 - c_2 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\)).

  2. Bivariate EGF & 3-Sparse Differential Equation: In a prefix-max block of size \(L\) on a chosen label set of size \(L\), the largest label must come first, leaving \((L - 1)!\) ways to arrange the remaining \(L - 1\) labels. Thus the EGF of a single block of size \(L\) is \(\frac{(L - 1)!}{L!} x^L = \frac{x^L}{L}\). Because a permutation is uniquely determined by an unordered set of prefix-max blocks (which are then sorted by their maximum labels), the bivariate EGF tracking total size \(x\) and balance \(b = c_1 - c_2\) via \(y^b\) is: \[F(x, y) = \exp\!\left( x y + \frac{x^2}{2} y^{-1} + \frac{x^3}{3} \right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \sum_{b=-n}^{n} f_{n, b} \, x^n y^b.\] Differentiating with respect to \(x\) gives the 3-sparse partial differential equation: \[\frac{\partial F(x, y)}{\partial x} = \big(y + x y^{-1} + x^2\big) F(x, y).\]

  3. Division-Free \(O(N^2)\) Coefficient Extraction: Extract \([x^{n-1} y^b]\) from both sides of \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = (y + x y^{-1} + x^2) F\): \[n f_{n, b} = f_{n-1, b-1} + f_{n-2, b+1} + f_{n-3, b}.\] To eliminate the division by \(n\) (and obtain the count of permutations \(dp_{n, b} = n! f_{n, b}\) directly), multiply both sides by \((n - 1)!\): \[dp_{n, b} = dp_{n-1, b-1} + (n - 1) dp_{n-2, b+1} + (n - 1)(n - 2) dp_{n-3, b},\] with base case \(dp_{0, 0} = 1\). Finally, sum \(dp_{3N, b}\) over \(0 \le b \le 3N\).

  4. Verification on the Sample Trace (\(N = 1, 3N = 3\)):

    • \(n = 0\): \(dp_{0, 0} = 1\).
    • \(n = 1\): \(dp_{1, 1} = dp_{0, 0} = 1\).
    • \(n = 2\): \(dp_{2, 2} = dp_{1, 1} = 1\); \(dp_{2, -1} = 1 \cdot dp_{0, 0} = 1\).
    • \(n = 3\):
      • \(b = 3\): \(dp_{3, 3} = dp_{2, 2} = 1\) (three blocks of size \(1\)).
      • \(b = 0\): \(dp_{3, 0} = dp_{2, -1} + 2 \cdot dp_{1, 1} + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot dp_{0, 0} = 1 + 2 + 2 = 5\) (\(3\) ways with one block of size \(2\) and one of size \(1\), plus \(2\) ways with one block of size \(3\)).

Define the power tower \(B_k(x) = S_{<b}(x)^k\) for \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n - 2\) (a polynomial of degree \(k(b - 1) < n b\)). Differentiate \(B_k(x) = S_{<b}(x)^k\) using \(S_{<b}'(x) = S_{<b}(x) - \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!}\): \[B_k'(x) = k S_{<b}(x)^{k-1} S_{<b}'(x) = k B_k(x) - k \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} B_{k-1}(x).\] Look at the right-hand side: \(B_{k-1}(x)\) is multiplied by the single monomial \(x^{b-1}\)! Extracting \([x^m / m!]\) (or \([x^m]\)) computes the entire polynomial \(B_k(x)\) from \(B_{k-1}(x)\) in \(O(1)\) time per coefficient, i.e., \(O(n^2 b)\) total time across all \(k = 0, \dots, n - 2\)!

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Continuous-Time Poissonization & Stopping-Time Integral: Embed the discrete uniform process into \(n\) independent Poisson processes of rate \(1\). Let \(T\) be the stopping time when all \(n\) tanks reach \(\ge b\) fuel. By linearity of expectation and symmetry among the \(n\) tanks, \(\mathbb{E}[\#\{i : c_i(T) \ge a\}] = n \big(1 - \Pr[c_1(T) < a]\big)\). At stopping time \(T\), how can tank \(1\) have \(< a\) units of fuel? There are two disjoint possibilities:

    • Case A (Tank \(1\) is the last tank to reach \(b\) fuel): By symmetry among the \(n\) tanks, this occurs with probability \(\frac{1}{n}\), and at time \(T\) tank \(1\) has exactly \(b < a\) fuel.
    • Case B (One of the other \(n - 1\) tanks is the last to reach \(b\), while tank \(1\) already has fuel in \([b, a - 1]\)): There are \(n - 1\) choices for which tank \(j \in \{2, \dots, n\}\) is the last to reach \(b\). Tank \(j\) hits its \(b\)-th unit in \([x, x + dx]\) with density \(e^{-x} \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} dx\), tank \(1\) has fuel in \([b, a - 1]\) at time \(x\) with probability \(e^{-x}\big(S_{<a}(x) - S_{<b}(x)\big)\), and the remaining \(n - 2\) tanks each have \(\ge b\) fuel at time \(x\) with probability \(1 - e^{-x} S_{<b}(x)\). Integrating over \(x \in (0, \infty)\) gives: \[I = (n - 1) \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-x}\big(S_{<a}(x) - S_{<b}(x)\big) \cdot e^{-x} \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} \cdot \big(1 - e^{-x} S_{<b}(x)\big)^{n-2} \, dx,\] and therefore \(\mathbb{E}[\#\{i : c_i(T) \ge a\}] = n\left(1 - \frac{1}{n} - I\right) = n - 1 - n I\).

  2. Binomial Expansion & EI 科技 Truncated Exponential Power Tower: Expand \(\big(1 - e^{-x} S_{<b}(x)\big)^{n-2} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} (-1)^k \binom{n-2}{k} e^{-kx} B_k(x)\), where \(B_k(x) = S_{<b}(x)^k\). How do we compute the coefficients of \(B_k(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{k(b-1)} b_{k, m} \frac{x^m}{m!}\) in \(O(n^2 b)\) time? Apply Theorem 19.2 (the Miracle Derivative \(S_{<b}'(x) = S_{<b}(x) - \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!}\)) to \(B_k(x) = S_{<b}(x)^k\): \[B_k'(x) = k \, S_{<b}(x)^{k-1} \left(S_{<b}(x) - \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!}\right) = k \, B_k(x) - k \, \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} B_{k-1}(x).\] Extracting \([x^m / m!]\) (remembering \([x^m / m!] B_k'(x) = b_{k, m+1}\) and \([x^m / m!] \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} B_{k-1}(x) = \binom{m}{b-1} b_{k-1, m-b+1}\)) gives the 2-term recurrence: \[b_{k, m+1} = k \, b_{k, m} - k \binom{m}{b - 1} b_{k - 1, m - b + 1}, \qquad b_{k, 0} = 1.\] This computes all \(b_{k, m}\) for \(0 \le k \le n - 2\) and \(0 \le m \le k(b - 1)\) in strictly \(O(n^2 b)\) arithmetic operations!

  3. Evaluating the Laplace Integral in \(O(n^2 a)\) Time: Aggregate the signed binomial combination \(W(x) = \sum_{m \ge 0} \left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-2} (-1)^k \binom{n-2}{k} b_{k, m} e^{-(k+2)x}\right) \frac{x^m}{m!}\). For each \(k \in [0, n - 2]\) and each term \(\frac{x^j}{j!}\) of \(S_{<a}(x) - S_{<b}(x) = \sum_{j=b}^{a-1} \frac{x^j}{j!}\), we use the Laplace identity \(\int_0^\infty e^{-(k+2)x} \frac{x^M}{M!} dx = \frac{1}{(k + 2)^{M + 1}}\) (or even faster, multiply \(\big(S_{<a}(x) - S_{<b}(x)\big) \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!}\) into \(B_k(x)\) via the same 1-monomial ODE for \(H_k(x) = \big(S_{<a}(x) - S_{<b}(x)\big) B_k(x)\), which satisfies \(H_k'(x) = (k + 1) H_k(x) - \big(\frac{x^{a-1}}{(a-1)!} - \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!}\big) B_k(x) - k \frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} H_{k-1}(x)\) in \(O(n^2 b + n a)\) time!).

  4. Verification on the Sample Trace (\(n = 2, a = 2, b = 1\)):

    • Here \(n - 2 = 0\), so only \(k = 0\) (\(B_0(x) = 1\)) appears, with \(S_{<2}(x) - S_{<1}(x) = x\) and \(\frac{x^{b-1}}{(b-1)!} = 1\).
