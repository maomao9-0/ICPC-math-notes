9.1 Causal Contractive Operators on \(K[[x]]\)

Concrete Combinatorial Motivation: When Offline Inversion Breaks

In Chapter 01, we saw that if a sequence \(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_N \in K\) counts sequentially decomposed structures via the shift-invariant linear recurrence \[ f_n = a_n + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f_i \, g_{n-i} \qquad (n \ge 0, \quad g_0 = 0), \] then translating into formal power series yields \(F(x) = A(x) + F(x) G(x)\), which we solve offline in a single pass as \(F(x) = A(x) (1 - G(x))^{-1} \bmod x^{N+1}\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time using Newton inversion.

Why does this offline algebraic shortcut work? Because every index \(n\) obeys the exact same linear rule: the convolution sum \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f_i g_{n-i}\) is added directly to \(a_n\) without any index-dependent rescaling, non-linear wrapper, or feedback into \(g\).

In competitive programming, four common structural twists immediately destroy this shift-invariant linearity while preserving strict causality (the property that \(f_n\) depends only on earlier terms \(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{n-1}\)):

  1. Pointwise Division by \(n\) (Formal Integration \(\int\)): In differential equations such as \(E'(x) = G'(x) E(x)\) (Chapter 02), extracting \([x^{n-1}]\) yields \[ e_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} e_i \cdot (n - i) g_{n-i} \qquad (n \ge 1, \quad e_0 = 1). \] Because of the outer \(\frac{1}{n}\) factor, we cannot write \(E(x)\) as \((1 - \widetilde{G}(x))^{-1}\); each coefficient \(e_n\) must be divided by \(n\) before it can participate in convolution products for \(e_{n+1}, \dots, e_N\).
  2. Non-Autonomous Index Multipliers: In problems with position-dependent constraints (ARC 138 E, ARC 115 E), the transition takes the form \[ f_n = w_n \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \alpha_i f_i \, g_{n-i}, \] where \(w_n\) and \(\alpha_i\) are arbitrary sequences depending on the input array at index \(n\) and \(i\). Multiplying the entire series by \(w_n\) pointwise is a Hadamard product \((W \odot ((A \odot F) * G))\), which does not commute with Cauchy multiplication.
  3. Pointwise \(\min / \max\) Envelopes Across Graph Edges: In time-expanded shortest-path or stochastic control problems (CF 553E — Kyoya and Train), a vertex \(u\) at time \(t\) chooses the best outgoing edge \(e = (u, v)\): \[ dp[u][t] = \min_{e = (u, v) \in \delta^+(u)} \left\{ c_e + \sum_{s=1}^{T - t} p_{e, s} \, dp[v][t + s] \right\}. \] The outer \(\min\) is non-linear: \(dp[u][t]\) is not known until all convolutions from later times \(t + s\) have been accumulated, and only after taking the minimum over all outgoing edges can \(dp[u][t]\) be fed back into incoming edges!
  4. Quadratic or Multi-Branch Self-Referential Convolutions: In binary tree and permutation decompositions (CF 438E, ABC 222 H, UOJ 50, AGC 060 D), both factors of the convolution depend on \(f\) itself: \[ f_n = \psi_n\!\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \alpha_i f_i \cdot \beta_{n-i} f_{n-i} \right). \] Here the convolution kernel is not known in advance—it is being constructed on the fly as \(f_1, f_2, \dots, f_{n-1}\) are discovered.

Formal Definition: Strictly Contractive Causal Operators

To unify all four regimes under a single mathematical framework, recall from Chapter 01 that the valuation \(\operatorname{val}(A)\) of a nonzero series \(A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n \in K[[x]]\) is the smallest index \(n\) such that \(a_n \neq 0\) (with \(\operatorname{val}(0) = +\infty\)), and the ultrametric valuation distance is \[ d_{\operatorname{val}}(F, G) = 2^{-\operatorname{val}(F - G)}. \] Two series \(F, G \in K[[x]]\) satisfy \(\operatorname{val}(F - G) \ge n\) if and only if they agree on their first \(n\) coefficients: \[ \operatorname{val}(F - G) \ge n \iff F(x) \equiv G(x) \pmod{x^n} \iff [x^k]F(x) = [x^k]G(x) \;\;\forall\, 0 \le k < n. \]

ImportantDefinition 9.1 (Strictly Contractive Causal Operator & Fixed-Point Theorem)

Let \(K\) be any commutative ring. A map \(\Phi : K[[x]] \to K[[x]]\) (not necessarily \(K\)-linear!) is called a strictly contractive causal operator if for all \(F, G \in K[[x]]\) with \(F \neq G\), \[ \operatorname{val}\!\big(\Phi(F) - \Phi(G)\big) > \operatorname{val}(F - G), \] or equivalently, in the valuation metric, \(d_{\operatorname{val}}(\Phi(F), \Phi(G)) \le \frac{1}{2} d_{\operatorname{val}}(F, G)\).

Coefficient Characterization: \(\Phi\) is strictly contractive and causal if and only if for every integer \(n \ge 0\), there exists a function \(\Phi_n : K^n \to K\) such that the \(n\)-th coefficient of \(\Phi(F)\) depends solely on the strictly earlier coefficients \((f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{n-1})\): \[ [x^n]\Phi(F) = \Phi_n(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{n-1}). \] (For \(n = 0\), \(\Phi_0()\) is a constant in \(K\) independent of \(F\).)

Theorem (Causal Fixed-Point Uniqueness): Every strictly contractive causal operator \(\Phi : K[[x]] \to K[[x]]\) possesses a unique fixed point \(F^*(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} f_n^* x^n \in K[[x]]\) satisfying \(F^* = \Phi(F^*)\). Its coefficients are uniquely determined in causal order \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) by \[ f_0^* = \Phi_0(), \qquad f_n^* = \Phi_n(f_0^*, f_1^*, \dots, f_{n-1}^*) \quad (n \ge 1). \]

Proof of Theorem 9.1: We verify existence and uniqueness by induction on \(n \ge 0\):

  1. For \(n = 0\), if \(F = \Phi(F)\), extracting \([x^0]\) yields \(f_0 = [x^0]\Phi(F) = \Phi_0()\), which uniquely fixes \(f_0^*\).
  2. Suppose \(f_0^*, f_1^*, \dots, f_{n-1}^*\) are uniquely determined by the condition \(F(x) \equiv \Phi(F)(x) \pmod{x^n}\). Then for any series \(F\) matching these first \(n\) coefficients, the \(n\)-th coefficient of \(\Phi(F)\) is \([x^n]\Phi(F) = \Phi_n(f_0^*, \dots, f_{n-1}^*)\). Thus \(f_n = [x^n]F\) equals \([x^n]\Phi(F)\) if and only if \(f_n = \Phi_n(f_0^*, \dots, f_{n-1}^*)\), completing the induction. \(\blacksquare\)

The Causal Convolution Landscape: Semi-Online vs. Full-Online

Theorem 9.1 guarantees that we can always compute \(f_0^*, f_1^*, \dots, f_N^*\) sequentially. However, if each evaluation of \(\Phi_n(f_0, \dots, f_{n-1})\) requires summing a convolution \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a_i b_{n-i}\) of length \(n\), then evaluating \(\Phi_0, \Phi_1, \dots, \Phi_N\) naively costs \(\sum_{n=1}^N O(n) = O(N^2)\) operations.

To accelerate this from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N \log^2 N)\), we classify causal convolution recurrences by what is known before the recursion begins:

9.2 Semi-Online CDQ Divide-and-Conquer NTT (solve(l, r))

The Divide-and-Conquer Strategy for Causal Transfers

Suppose we want to compute \(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{N-1}\) governed by the semi-online recurrence \[ f_n = \psi_n(S_n), \qquad S_n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \alpha_i(f_i) \, g_{n-i} \qquad (0 \le n < N), \] where the kernel \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{N-1} \in K\) is given offline, and for each index \(n\), once the accumulator \(S_n\) has received all contributions from \(0 \le i < n\), we can evaluate \(f_n = \psi_n(S_n)\) and \(\alpha_n(f_n)\) in \(O(1)\) time.

How can we batch the \(O(N^2)\) pairwise transfers \((i, n)\) with \(0 \le i < n < N\) into fast polynomial multiplications without violating causality? We use CDQ Divide-and-Conquer on the index interval \([l, r]\):

  1. Base Case (\(l = r\)): When the recursion reaches a single index \(l\), we require the invariant that the accumulator \(S_l\) has already received the contributions \(\alpha_i(f_i) g_{l - i}\) from every \(0 \le i < l\). We then finalize \(f_l \gets \psi_l(S_l)\) in \(O(1)\) time.
  2. Recursive Case (\(l < r\)): Let \(m = \lfloor (l + r) / 2 \rfloor\) split \([l, r]\) into a left half \([l, m]\) and a right half \([m + 1, r]\):
    • Step 1 (Solve Left Half): Recursively call solve(l, m). By causality, no index in \([m + 1, r]\) can ever influence \([l, m]\). Thus, when solve(l, m) returns, every \(f_i\) (and \(\alpha_i(f_i)\)) for \(l \le i \le m\) is 100% finalized.
    • Step 2 (Cross-Boundary NTT Transfer \([l, m] \to [m + 1, r]\)): We must now transfer the causal contribution of every finalized left index \(i \in [l, m]\) to every pending right index \(j \in [m + 1, r]\). Notice that for \(i \in [l, m]\) and \(j \in [m + 1, r]\), the lag \(k = j - i\) satisfies \[ 1 \le j - m \le k = j - i \le r - l. \] Define two polynomials of degrees \(m - l\) and \(r - l\) respectively: \[ A_{[l, m]}(x) = \sum_{i=l}^{m} \alpha_i(f_i) \, x^{i - l}, \qquad B_{[1, r - l]}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{r - l} g_k \, x^k. \] Multiply \(C(x) = A_{[l, m]}(x) \cdot B_{[1, r - l]}(x)\) using a single NTT of length \(L = 2^{\lceil \log_2(m - l + r - l + 1) \rceil} \le 4(r - l)\). For each \(j \in [m + 1, r]\), the coefficient of \(x^{j - l}\) in \(C(x)\) is \[ [x^{j - l}] C(x) = \sum_{\substack{l \le i \le m \\ 1 \le k \le r - l \\ (i - l) + k = j - l}} \alpha_i(f_i) \, g_k = \sum_{i=l}^{m} \alpha_i(f_i) \, g_{j - i}. \] Adding \([x^{j - l}] C(x)\) to \(S_j\) for each \(j = m + 1, \dots, r\) simultaneously delivers all transfers from \([l, m]\) to \([m + 1, r]\)!
    • Step 3 (Solve Right Half): Recursively call solve(m + 1, r).

Proof of Correctness: The LCA Partition Invariant

Why does this three-step recursion add every causal pair \((i, j)\) with \(0 \le i < j < N\) to \(S_j\) exactly once, and always before the base case solve(j, j) is executed?

NoteTheorem 9.2 (The LCA Partition Invariant of CDQ NTT)

Consider the binary recursion tree \(\mathcal{T}\) of intervals \([l, r]\) generated by solve(0, N - 1) with midpoint \(m = \lfloor (l + r) / 2 \rfloor\). For every ordered pair of indices \((i, j)\) with \(0 \le i < j < N\):

  1. Unique LCA Node: There exists a unique internal node \([l, r] \in \mathcal{T}\) with midpoint \(m = \lfloor (l + r) / 2 \rfloor\) such that \[ l \le i \le m < j \le r. \] Specifically, \([l, r] = \operatorname{LCA}_{\mathcal{T}}(\text{leaf } i, \text{leaf } j)\) is the unique node where leaf \(i\) branches into the left child \([l, m]\) and leaf \(j\) branches into the right child \([m + 1, r]\).
  2. Causal In-Order Sequencing: Because solve(l, r) executes in in-order traversal (Left Child \(\to\) Cross-Transfer \(\to\) Right Child), at the moment \([l, r]\) performs its cross-boundary NTT:
    • The entire subtree of \([l, m]\)—including leaf [i, i]—has already finished, so \(\alpha_i(f_i)\) holds its final value.
    • The entire subtree of \([m + 1, r]\)—including leaf [j, j]—has not yet begun, so the contribution \(\alpha_i(f_i) g_{j - i}\) arrives in \(S_j\) strictly before leaf [j, j] finalizes \(f_j = \psi_j(S_j)\).

Extending CDQ NTT to Self-Convolution \(C = F * F\) & Failure Mode #13

What if the recurrence is self-referential, such as \(f_n = \psi_n\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \alpha_i(f_i) \, \beta_{n-i}(f_{n-i})\right)\) where both factors \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_{n-i}\) are unknown until step \(i\) and \(n - i\)?

Suppose we pad \(N\) to a power of \(2\) so every CDQ node \([l, r]\) is a dyadic interval \([k \cdot 2^s, (k + 1) 2^s - 1]\) of length \(2^s\) with midpoint \(m = l + 2^{s-1} - 1\). Consider an ordered pair of positive indices \((i, j)\) with \(i \ge 1, j \ge 1\) contributing \(\alpha_i \beta_j\) to target index \(n = i + j \in [m + 1, r]\). Which index is finalized later—\(i\) or \(j\)?

  • Case 1: The Root Left-Aligned Node (\(l = 0\)). Here \([l, m] = [0, 2^{s-1} - 1]\) and \([m + 1, r] = [2^{s-1}, 2^s - 1]\). For any \(n \in [m + 1, r]\), if both \(i \le m\) and \(j \le m\), then both \(i\) and \(j\) belong to \([0, m]\)! Therefore, convolving \(\alpha[0 \dots m]\) with \(\beta[0 \dots m]\) once at node \([0, r]\) accounts for every pair \((i, j) \in [1, m] \times [1, m]\) summing to \(n \in [m + 1, r]\) (including both \((i, j)\) and \((j, i)\) when \(i \neq j\), and the diagonal \((i, i)\) once).

  • Case 2: Interior Dyadic Nodes (\(l > 0\)). Since \([l, r] = [k \cdot 2^s, (k + 1) 2^s - 1]\) with \(k \ge 1\), we have \(l = k \cdot 2^s \ge 2^s = r - l + 1\), so \[ r - l < l. \] Now suppose \(i + j = n \in [m + 1, r]\) where at least one of \(i, j\) lies in \([l, m]\). If \(i \in [l, m]\), then the partner index \(j = n - i\) satisfies \[ 1 \le (m + 1) - m \le j = n - i \le r - l < l. \] Thus \(j \in [1, r - l]\) lies strictly to the left of \(l\)! Because \([l, m]\) and \([1, r - l]\) are disjoint, an unordered pair \(\{i, j\}\) with \(i \in [l, m]\) and \(j \in [1, r - l]\) corresponds to two ordered terms in \(\sum_{a+b=n} \alpha_a \beta_b\):

    1. \((a, b) = (i, j)\) with \(a \in [l, m], b \in [1, r - l]\): contributed by \(\alpha[l \dots m] * \beta[1 \dots r - l]\).
    2. \((a, b) = (j, i)\) with \(a \in [1, r - l], b \in [l, m]\): contributed by \(\beta[l \dots m] * \alpha[1 \dots r - l]\).
WarningFailure Mode #13: The Self-Convolution CDQ Symmetry Bug (l == 0 vs. l > 0 Missing Factor of 2)

The Bug: When solving a symmetric self-convolution \(S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} f_i f_{n-i}\) (\(\alpha = \beta = f\), with \(f_0 = 0\)) via CDQ NTT, a common mistake is to write C = multiply(f[l..m], f[0..r-l]) and add C[j - l] to S[j] at every node \([l, r]\). This produces the correct values at \(l = 0\) (where f[0..m] * f[0..m] naturally includes both \((i, k)\) and \((k, i)\)), but under-counts every \(l > 0\) transfer by a factor of \(2\)!

Why It Happens: For \(l > 0\), f[l..m] contains indices \(a \in [l, m]\), whereas f[0..r-l] contains indices \(b \in [0, r-l]\). Because \(r - l < l\) on a dyadic grid, the two index ranges \([l, m]\) and \([0, r-l]\) are completely disjoint! Convolving f[l..m] with f[0..r-l] only counts ordered pairs \((a, b)\) where the first index is in \([l, m]\) and the second is in \([0, r-l]\), completely omitting the symmetric ordered pair \((b, a)\) where the first index is in \([0, r-l]\) and the second is in \([l, m]\).

The 1-Line Remedy:

  • Always pad the CDQ domain to \([0, 2^K - 1]\) so \(r - l < l\) holds at every node with \(l > 0\).
  • For symmetric self-convolution (\(\alpha = \beta = f\), \(f_0 = 0\)), at \(l = 0\) convolve f[0..m] * f[0..m] with weight 1, and at \(l > 0\) convolve f[l..m] * f[0..r-l] with weight 2:
Poly A(f.begin() + l, f.begin() + m + 1);
Poly B(f.begin(), f.begin() + (l == 0 ? m : r - l) + 1);
Poly C = multiply(A, B);
long long sym = (l == 0 ? 1 : 2);
for (int j = m + 1; j <= r; ++j)
    S[j] = (S[j] + sym * C[j - l]) % MOD;
  • For asymmetric bilinear products (\(\alpha \neq \beta\)), at \(l = 0\) compute \(\alpha[0 \dots m] * \beta[0 \dots m]\), and at \(l > 0\) compute \(\alpha[l \dots m] * \beta[0 \dots r - l] + \beta[l \dots m] * \alpha[0 \dots r - l]\).

9.3 Full-Online 0-Indexed Relaxed Convolution (van der Hoeven / Nyaan)

Why Stateless Online Engines Beat Tangled CDQ Trees

While self-convolution CDQ NTT (§9.2) works well for a single quadratic equation like \(F' = \frac{1}{2} A F^2 + 1\), what happens when a problem chains two or more causal products together—such as \(B(x) = W(x) E(x)\) coupled with \(E'(x) = G'(x) E(x)\) in ABC 230 Ex (§9.4), or \(F(x) = 1 + \frac{x}{6}\big(F(x) \cdot (F(x)^2 + 3F(x^2)) + 2F(x^3)\big)\) in LOJ 6538?

With recursive CDQ, chaining two products requires interleaving multiple solve(l, r) functions and manually tracking which cross-terms \(l = 0\) vs. \(l > 0\) have been doubled. In 1997, Joris van der Hoeven discovered Relaxed Convolution (popularized in competitive programming by Nyaan and maspy), which encapsulates the entire dyadic block schedule inside a stateful streaming object:

  • At each step \(q = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), you push the newly discovered pair \((a_q, b_q)\) into rc.next(a_q, b_q), and it immediately returns the exact \(q\)-th convolution coefficient \[ c_q = \sum_{i=0}^{q} a_i \, b_{q - i}. \] Because rc.next(a_q, b_q) is called inside a plain for (int q = 0; q <= N; ++q) loop, you can instantiate as many RelaxedConvolution objects as you want (rc1, rc2, rc3) and pipe the output of one directly into the input of the next!

The 2D Grid Block-Tiling Decomposition (d = lowbit(q))

How can rc.next(a_q, b_q) compute \(c_q = \sum_{i+j=q} a_i b_j\) on the fly in \(O(N \log^2 N)\) total time?

Consider the infinite quarter-plane grid \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) of index pairs \((i, j)\). At step \(q = 0\), \(c_0 = a_0 b_0\). For every step \(q \ge 1\), the anti-diagonal \(i + j = q\) splits cleanly into:

  • Two border cells \((q, 0)\) and \((0, q)\), which contribute \(a_q b_0 + a_0 b_q\) and are added directly in \(O(1)\) time at step \(q\); and
  • The positive interior grid \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\), which we partition into dyadic \(d \times d\) blocks triggered at step \(q \ge 1\) according to \(d = \operatorname{lowbit}(q) = q \ \& \ -q = 2^k\) (the largest power of \(2\) dividing \(q\)):
  1. Power-of-Two Steps (\(q = d = 2^k\), i.e., \(q = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots\)): Convolve the single \(d \times d\) square block on the main interior diagonal: \[ A[1 \dots d] \times B[1 \dots d], \] and accumulate all terms with index sum \(i + j > q\) into \(c[q + 1 \dots 2q]\).
  2. Off-Diagonal Interior Steps (\(d < q\), i.e., \(q = m \cdot d\) with \(m \ge 3\) odd): Convolve the two symmetric \(d \times d\) off-diagonal blocks: \[ \text{Block 1: } A[q - d + 1 \dots q] \times B[d \dots 2d - 1], \qquad \text{Block 2: } A[d \dots 2d - 1] \times B[q - d + 1 \dots q], \] and accumulate both products into \(c[q + 1 \dots q + 2d - 1]\).
ImportantCrucial Subtlety: Why Separating Border Cells (i = 0, j = 0) + Interior lowbit(q) Beats Pure 0-Indexed lowbit(q + 1)

Why do we separate the border terms \(a_q b_0 + a_0 b_q\) in \(O(1)\) and apply \(d = \operatorname{lowbit}(q)\) to the \(1\)-indexed interior \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\), rather than applying a single \(d = \operatorname{lowbit}(q + 1)\) block rule directly to the \(0\)-indexed grid \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\)?

  1. No Missing Corner Cells: On an unshifted \(0\)-indexed grid, triggering only a single block size \(d = \operatorname{lowbit}(q + 1)\) at step \(q = 5\) (\(q + 1 = 6, d = 2\)) would convolve \(A[4 \dots 5] \times B[1 \dots 2]\), missing the border cell \((5, 0)\) because \(5 + 1 = 6\) is divisible by \(2^1\) whereas \(0 + 1 = 1\) is only divisible by \(2^0\)! Separating the \(i = 0\) and \(j = 0\) borders in \(O(1)\) shifts the interior to \((i, j) \ge (1, 1)\), so every step \(q \ge 1\) needs only a single block size \(d = \operatorname{lowbit}(q)\) (\(1\) convolution if \(d = q\), or \(2\) convolutions if \(d < q\)).
  2. Built-In Self-Referential Causality: Because the interior blocks at step \(q\) convolve \(A[q - d + 1 \dots q]\) with \(B[d \dots 2d - 1]\), the minimum sum of indices in any interior block triggered at step \(q\) is \[ (q - d + 1) + d = q + 1 > q. \] Consequently, every interior pair \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) with \(i + j = q\) was ALREADY accumulated into \(c_q\) during steps \(1, 2, \dots, q - 1\)! At step \(q\), the only terms missing from \(c_q = \sum_{i=0}^q a_i b_{q-i}\) are the two border terms \(a_0 b_q + a_q b_0\)!

Let us prove why this interior grid partitions \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) with zero omissions and zero duplicates, and why \(\text{step}(i, j) < i + j\) for every \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\):

NoteTheorem 9.3 (Exact Partition & Strict Causality of lowbit(q) Relaxed Convolution)

For every integer \(q \ge 1\) with \(d = \operatorname{lowbit}(q)\):

  • If \(d = q\), define \(\mathcal{B}_q = [1, d] \times [1, d]\).
  • If \(d < q\), define \(\mathcal{B}_q = \big([q - d + 1, q] \times [d, 2d - 1]\big) \cup \big([d, 2d - 1] \times [q - d + 1, q]\big)\).

Then:

  1. Exact Partition: The sets \(\{\mathcal{B}_q\}_{q \ge 1}\) are pairwise disjoint and their union is all of \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\).
  2. Availability: Every \((i, j) \in \mathcal{B}_q\) satisfies \(\max(i, j) \le q\), so \(a_i\) and \(b_j\) are known at step \(q\).
  3. Strict Interior Causality: Every \((i, j) \in \mathcal{B}_q\) satisfies \(i + j \ge q + 1\). Consequently, for every \(n \ge 1\), the entire interior sum \[ c_n^{\text{int}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} a_i \, b_{n - i} \] is completely accumulated into \(c_n\) by the end of step \(q = n - 1\)!

Proof:

  1. Given any cell \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\), let \(2^k\) be the unique power of \(2\) such that \(\min(i, j) \in [2^k, 2^{k+1} - 1]\), and set \(d = 2^k\). Let \(M = \max(i, j) \ge d\), and let \(q = \lceil M / d \rceil \cdot d\) be the smallest multiple of \(d\) that is \(\ge M\).
    • If \(\operatorname{lowbit}(q) = d\), then \(M \in [q - d + 1, q]\) and \(\min(i, j) \in [d, 2d - 1]\), so \((i, j)\) belongs to the interior step \(\mathcal{B}_q\).
    • If \(\operatorname{lowbit}(q) = 2^r > d\) (which happens if and only if \(\lceil M / d \rceil\) is an even multiple of \(2^{r-k}\), meaning \(M \le q \le 2^r\)), then since \(M \ge d\), this can only happen when \(q = 2^r\) itself and \(1 \le i, j \le 2^r\), so \((i, j)\) belongs to the power-of-two square \(\mathcal{B}_{2^r}\) (specifically inside \([1, 2^r]^2 \setminus [1, 2^{r-1}]^2\) because \(\max(i, j) = M > q - d \ge 2^{r-1}\)). Thus every \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) belongs to a unique \(\mathcal{B}_q\).
  2. By definition of \(\mathcal{B}_q\), \(\max(i, j) \le q\).
  3. If \(d = q\), we only accumulate pairs with \(i + j \ge q + 1\) (since any pair in \([1, d]^2\) with \(i + j \le q = d\) has \(i, j \le d - 1\), and because \(d = 2^k \ge 2\) for \(i + j \le d\), both \(i, j \le 2^{k-1} - 1\) or \((i, j)\) was already covered at \(q' = d / 2\)!). And if \(d < q\), every \((i, j) \in \mathcal{B}_q\) has \(i + j \ge (q - d + 1) + d = q + 1\). Thus \(\mathcal{B}_q\) only contributes to indices \(n = i + j \ge q + 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

The 3-Step Self-Referential Protocol & Complete Engine

Because \(c_q^{\text{int}} = \sum_{i=1}^{q-1} a_i b_{q-i}\) is already sitting in c[q] before step \(q\) begins, we obtain the universal 3-Step Self-Referential Protocol at step \(q \ge 1\):

  1. Read Interior Sum: Read rc.c[q], which already equals \(\sum_{i=1}^{q-1} a_i b_{q-i}\).
  2. Resolve Step-\(q\) Equations: Use rc.c[q] (together with \(a_0, b_0\)) to solve for \(f_q\), \(a_q\), and \(b_q\).
  3. Commit Step \(q\) (rc.next(a_q, b_q)): Call rc.next(a_q, b_q), which adds the border terms \(a_q b_0 + a_0 b_q\) to c[q], runs the lowbit(q) block convolution(s) to push contributions into \(c[q + 1 \dots 2q]\), and returns the finalized \(c_q = \sum_{i=0}^q a_i b_{q-i}\)!
NoteAlgorithm Contract & Concise Core: RelaxedConvolution (van der Hoeven / Nyaan)
  • Input & Index Boundaries: Online stream of coefficient pairs \((a_q, b_q) \in K^2\) arriving sequentially at \(0\)-indexed steps \(q = 0, 1, \dots, N\).
  • Preconditions: Step \(q\) is invoked only after steps \(0, 1, \dots, q - 1\) have completed; \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) is an NTT-friendly prime field.
  • Postconditions: Calling next(a_q, b_q) at step \(q\) appends \(a_q, b_q\), finalizes and returns \(c_q = \sum_{i=0}^q a_i b_{q-i}\), and pre-accumulates \(\mathcal{B}_q\) into c[q + 1 .. q + 2d - 1] so that c[q + 1] holds the exact interior sum \(\sum_{i=1}^{q} a_i b_{q+1-i}\) prior to step \(q + 1\).
  • Complexity: Across \(N\) calls (\(q = 0 \dots N - 1\)), for each \(d = 2^k \le N\), there are \(\lfloor N / d \rfloor\) steps with \(\operatorname{lowbit}(q) = d\), each doing \(O(d \log d)\) work. Summing over \(k = 0 \dots \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor\) gives \[ \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor} \frac{N}{2^k} \cdot O(2^k \log(2^k)) = O(N \log^2 N) \text{ time}, \qquad O(N) \text{ space}. \]
  • 1-Sentence Loop Invariant: Prior to the \(q\)-th call next(a_q, b_q) (\(q \ge 1\)), c[q] already holds the complete interior convolution sum \(c_q^{\text{int}} = \sum_{i=1}^{q-1} a_i b_{q-i}\) from all positive index pairs \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) with \(i + j = q\).
struct RelaxedConvolution {
    vector<long long> a, b, c;
    long long next(long long aq, long long bq) {
        int q = a.size(), d = q & -q; a.push_back(aq); b.push_back(bq);
        if ((int)c.size() <= 2 * q + 2) c.resize(2 * q + 3, 0);
        if (q == 0) return c[0] = aq * bq % MOD;
        c[q] = (c[q] + aq * b[0] + a[0] * bq) % MOD;
        auto add = [&](int la, int lb) {
            Poly P = multiply({a.begin()+la, a.begin()+la+d}, {b.begin()+lb, b.begin()+lb+d});
            for (int i = 0; i < (int)P.size(); ++i) if (la + lb + i > q) c[la + lb + i] = (c[la + lb + i] + P[i]) % MOD;
        };
        if (d == q) add(1, 1); else { add(q - d + 1, d); add(d, q - d + 1); }
        return c[q];
    }
};

9.4 10-Line Online Replacements for inv, ln, exp, and Pólya Euler Transforms

One of the greatest practical payoffs of RelaxedConvolution is that every major power series primitive from Chapters 01–03—polynomial inversion, logarithm, exponential, square root, arbitrary power, and even coupled Pólya multiset transforms—can be written in 5 to 10 lines of loop code without a single Newton iteration!

The Online Differential-Equation Dictionary

Let us derive the exact 1-line RelaxedConvolution update for each primitive up to degree \(N\):

  1. Online Multiplicative Inverse \(F(x) = A(x)^{-1} \bmod x^{N+1}\) (where \(a_0 \in K^\times\)): Write \(A(x) F(x) = 1\). For \(q = 0\), \(f_0 = a_0^{-1}\). At step \(m = q - 1 \ge 0\), \([x^m] G'(x) = (m + 1) g_{m+1} = q g_q\) and \([x^m] E(x) = e_m = e_{q-1}\) are both already known!   3. Online Logarithm \(L(x) = \ln F(x) \bmod x^{N+1}\) (where \(f_0 = 1\) and \(p > N\)): From \(L'(x) F(x) = F'(x)\), write \(L'(x) = F'(x) - L'(x) (F(x) - 1)\). Extracting \([x^{q-1}]\) for \(q \ge 1\) gives \[ q \, \ell_q = q \, f_q - \sum_{i=1}^{q-1} (i \ell_i) \, f_{q-i} \implies \ell_q = f_q - \frac{1}{q} \cdot \texttt{rc.next}((q - 1)\ell_{q-1},\; f_q) \quad (q \ge 1, \text{ with } \ell_0 = 0). \]

  4. Online Square Root \(S(x) = \sqrt{F(x)} \bmod x^{N+1}\) (where \(f_0 = 1\) and \(\operatorname{char}(K) \neq 2\)): Write \(S(x) = 1 + T(x)\) with \(T(0) = 0\). Thus: \[ s_0 = 1, \qquad s_q = \frac{f_q - \texttt{rc.c}[q]}{2}, \qquad \texttt{rc.next}(s_q,\; s_q) \quad (q \ge 1, \text{ initialized with } \texttt{rc.next}(0, 0)). \]

  5. Online Power \(H(x) = F(x)^\alpha \bmod x^{N+1}\) (where \(f_0 = 1\) and \(p > N\)): From \(F(x) H'(x) = \alpha F'(x) H(x)\), rewrite as \(H'(x) = \alpha F'(x) H(x) - \frac{F(x) - 1}{x} \cdot x H'(x)\), or chain Online \(\ln\) into Online \(\exp\) in two lines!

Online Pólya / Euler Transform (MSET on the Fly)

In Chapter 03, when the generator series \(B(x) = \sum_{n=1}^N b_n x^n\) was completely known in advance, we computed the multiset Euler transform \[ E(x) = \operatorname{MSET}(B)(x) = \prod_{n \ge 1} \frac{1}{(1 - x^n)^{b_n}} = \exp\!\left( \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{B(x^k)}{k} \right) \] offline in \(O(N \log N)\) time by populating \(G(x) = \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{B(x^k)}{k}\) via a harmonic loop and calling \(\exp(G(x))\).

What if \(B(x)\) is not known in advance, because \(b_n\) itself depends on \(e_0, e_1, \dots, e_{n-1}\)—for example, in rooted unlabeled trees \(B(x) = x E(x)\) or nested multiset bags \(B(x) = W(x) E(x)\) (ABC 230 Ex — Bullion, Int-2 below)?

Let us trace the exact causal dependencies in \(E(x) = \exp(G(x))\) where \(G(x) = \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{B(x^k)}{k}\):

  • Differentiating \(G(x)\) and multiplying by \(x\) gives \[ x G'(x) = \sum_{k \ge 1} x^k B'(x^k) = \sum_{k \ge 1} \sum_{d \ge 1} d \, b_d \, x^{k d} = \sum_{n \ge 1} \left( \sum_{d \mid n} d \, b_d \right) x^n. \]

  • Therefore, the coefficient \(n g_n = [x^{n-1}] G'(x)\) needed by the Online Exponential at step \(n\) is simply the divisor sum \[ n g_n = \sum_{d \mid n} d \, b_d. \]

  • Notice that \(n g_n\) depends only on \(b_d\) for divisors \(d \mid n\), and every divisor \(d\) of \(n\) satisfies \(d \le n\)!

  • Consequently, the moment \(b_n\) becomes known at step \(n\), we immediately run a harmonic inner loop over multiples \(m = n, 2n, 3n, \dots \le N\), adding \(n \cdot b_n\) to an accumulator ng[m]:

    for (int m = n; m <= N; m += n)
    ng[m] = (ng[m] + 1LL * n * b[n]) % MOD;

    Across all \(n = 1 \dots N\), this harmonic inner loop executes \(\sum_{n=1}^N \lfloor N / n \rfloor = O(N \log N)\) additions. And by the time the outer loop reaches step \(n\) and finishes adding \(n \cdot b_n\) to ng[n], every divisor \(d \mid n\) (\(d \le n\)) has already contributed \(d \cdot b_d\) to ng[n], so ng[n] holds the exact value \(n g_n\) ready to be passed into rc_exp.next(ng[n], e[n - 1])!

  • Problem Statement: Given an integer \(N\), count the number of ordered pairs of permutations \((P, Q) \in S_N \times S_N\) of \((1, 2, \dots, N)\) that have the exact same descent set—that is, for every index \(1 \le i \le N - 1\), \[ (P_i < P_{i+1}) \iff (Q_i < Q_{i+1}). \] Output the number of such pairs modulo \(998244353\).
  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 2 \times 10^5\). Modulus \(p = 998244353\). Time limit: \(5.0\text{ s}\). Required complexity: \(O(N \log^2 N)\) via coupled CDQ NTT or \(O(N \log N)\) via Hadamard-square super-block EGF inversion.

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Let \(D(P) = \{i \in [1, N - 1] : P_i > P_{i+1}\}\) be the descent set of \(P\). For any fixed subset \(S \subseteq [1, N - 1]\), let \(f(S)\) be the number of permutations \(P \in S_N\) with \(D(P) = S\). Then we want to compute \[ \text{Ans}_N = \sum_{S \subseteq [1, N - 1]} f(S)^2. \] By Inclusion-Exclusion on a single permutation \(P\), if we instead fix a subset \(A \subseteq [1, N - 1]\) of positions forced to be ascents (\(P_i < P_{i+1}\)), then \(A\) partitions \([1, N]\) into contiguous increasing runs of lengths \(s_1, \dots, s_k\), and the number of such permutations is the multinomial coefficient \(\frac{N!}{s_1! \, s_2! \cdots s_k!}\). How does squaring \(f(S)\) couple the run lengths of \(P\) and \(Q\)?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Using the algebraic identity on the Boolean hypercube \(\{+1, -1\}^{N-1}\) (or expanding \(f(S)\) via Inclusion-Exclusion over forced ascent runs for both \(P\) and \(Q\)), at each boundary \(i \in \{1, \dots, N - 1\}\), there are four possibilities for the IE relaxation states of \((P, Q)\):

  1. Both \(P\) and \(Q\) are cut (start a new run) at \(i\);
  2. \(P\) continues its run across \(i\), while \(Q\) is cut at \(i\);
  3. \(Q\) continues its run across \(i\), while \(P\) is cut at \(i\);
  4. Both \(P\) and \(Q\) continue their runs across \(i\). Key observation: if we group the sequence \([1, N]\) at the positions where both \(P\) and \(Q\) are simultaneously cut (forming super-blocks of length \(M \ge 1\)), inside a single super-block of length \(M\), \(P\)’s cuts and \(Q\)’s cuts never need to synchronize until the end of the super-block.
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Let us compute the weight of a super-block of length \(M\) (noshi91’s Super-Block Decomposition):

  • Encode the ascent/descent pattern of \(P\) as \(\sigma_i(P) = 2[P_i < P_{i+1}] - 1 \in \{+1, -1\}\). Inside a super-block of length \(M\), \(P\) chooses a composition \(s_1 + \dots + s_r = M\) of increasing run lengths where each run of length \(s\) has EGF weight \(2^{s-1} \frac{x^s}{s!} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{(2x)^s}{s!}\) and each of the \(r - 1\) internal cuts has weight \(-1\), giving the single-permutation super-block EGF \[ U(x) = \sum_{r \ge 1} (-1)^{r - 1} \left(\frac{e^{2x} - 1}{2}\right)^r = \frac{e^{2x} - 1}{e^{2x} + 1} = \tanh(x) = \sum_{M=1}^{N} u_M x^M. \]

  • Summing over all compositions of \([1, N]\) into super-blocks via SEQ inversion gives: \[ \text{Ans}_N = \frac{(N!)^2}{2^{N - 1}} [x^N] \frac{1}{1 - W(x)}, \qquad W(x) = \sum_{M=1}^{N} u_M^2 x^M. \]
