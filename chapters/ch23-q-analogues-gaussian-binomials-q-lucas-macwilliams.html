TipLearning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1.   2. Solve \(q\)-Difference Equations (\(x \mapsto qx\)): Derive the positive, negative, and Cauchy \(q\)-Binomial Theorems, \(q\)-Vandermonde convolution, and \(q\)-Binomial Inversion from first-order multiplicative shift equations \(P(x)A(x) = R(x)A(qx) + S(x)\).
  3. Eliminate Root-of-Unity Singularities via the \(q\)-Lucas Theorem (Failure Mode #9): Detect and resolve \(0/0\) division failures when evaluating \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \bmod p\) at \(\operatorname{ord}_p(q) = d \le n\) or reducing modulo \(q^P - 1 = \prod_{d \mid P}\Phi_d(q)\), and accelerate \(q\)-convolutions to \(O(N \log N)\) via Bluestein’s Chirp-Z identity.
  4. Decompose Infinite \(q\)-Products via Pentagonal & Durfee Engines: Apply Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem, Jacobi’s Triple Product, and the \(O(N\sqrt{N})\) division-free Durfee square decomposition over arbitrary (even composite) moduli.
  5. Dualize High-Rank Subspaces via Krawtchouk / MacWilliams Duality: Break the \(O(2^r)\) enumeration barrier for rank-\(r\) subspaces \(\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^m\) when \(r > m/2\) by switching to the \((m-r)\)-dimensional orthogonal complement \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) via a self-contained 5-line character-orthogonality derivation.

In classical enumerative combinatorics, the ordinary binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{k}\) counts subsets of size \(k\) chosen from an \(n\)-element set, and the differential operator \(D = \frac{d}{dx}\) probes a generating function by shifting exponents additively (\(x^n \mapsto n x^{n-1}\)). Yet two vast families of competitive programming problems resist additive generating functions:

  1. Linear Algebra over Finite Fields \(\mathbb{F}_q\): When a problem asks for the number of sequences \(a_1, \dots, a_n \in [0, 2^k)\) whose XOR span avoids a target vector, or the number of \(n \times m\) binary matrices of rank \(r\), or the expected maximum XOR of a random basis in Reduced Row Echelon Form, the underlying lattice is no longer the boolean subset lattice \(\mathcal{P}(\{1,\dots,n\})\)—it is the Grassmannian subspace lattice \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_q^n)\). Adding a new vector to a \(k\)-dimensional subspace succeeds not when it avoids \(k\) elements, but when it avoids \(q^k\) linear combinations.
  2. Area- and Inversion-Weighted Partitions: When we track both the number of parts (via \(x\)) and the total sum or inversion count (via \(q\)) inside a product \(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 + x q^i)\), replacing \(x\) by \(qx\) shifts the product multiplicatively by a single boundary factor: \(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 + x q^{i+1}) = \frac{1 + x q^n}{1 + x}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 + x q^i)\).

The unified algebraic language governing both worlds is \(q\)-series (basic hypergeometric series), where the additive step \(+1\) is replaced by the multiplicative scaling \(\times q\).

23.1 \(q\)-Pochhammer Symbols \((a;q)_n\), Gaussian \(q\)-Binomials \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\) & The \(q\)-Difference Equation Engine

Concrete Definitions: \(q\)-Brackets, \(q\)-Factorials, and \(q\)-Pochhammer Symbols

Before writing any generating function, let us define the fundamental building blocks in the polynomial ring \(\mathbb{Z}[q]\) (or the formal power series ring \(K[[q]]\) over a commutative ring \(K\)).

For a non-negative integer \(n \ge 0\), the \(q\)-bracket (or \(q\)-integer) \([n]_q\) is the geometric sum of the first \(n\) powers of \(q\): \[[n]_q := 1 + q + q^2 + \dots + q^{n-1} = \frac{1 - q^n}{1 - q} \quad (\text{with } [0]_q = 0).\] When we evaluate at \(q = 1\), every term in the sum equals \(1\), so \(\lim_{q \to 1} [n]_q = n\). Multiplying the first \(n\) non-zero \(q\)-brackets gives the \(q\)-factorial: \[[n]_q! := [1]_q [2]_q \cdots [n]_q = \frac{(1-q)(1-q^2)\cdots(1-q^n)}{(1-q)^n} \quad (\text{with } [0]_q! = 1).\]

In generating function manipulations, carrying around powers of \((1-q)\) in denominators is awkward and unnecessary. We clear them by defining the \(q\)-Pochhammer symbol (or \(q\)-shifted factorial) for any indeterminates \(a, q\) and integer \(n \ge 0\): \[(a; q)_n := \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} (1 - a q^i) = (1 - a)(1 - aq)(1 - aq^2)\cdots(1 - a q^{n-1}), \qquad (a; q)_0 := 1.\] When \(n = \infty\), the infinite product \((a; q)_\infty := \prod_{i=0}^\infty (1 - a q^i)\) converges in the \((x, q)\)-adic topology of \(K[[a, q]]\) (or in \(K[[q]]\) whenever \(a \in q K[[q]]\) has positive \(q\)-valuation \(\operatorname{val}_q(a) \ge 1\)). Notice that \([n]_q! = \frac{(q;q)_n}{(1-q)^n}\).

Now we can define the centerpiece of this chapter. For integers \(0 \le k \le n\), the Gaussian \(q\)-binomial coefficient \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\) (also written \(\binom{n}{k}_q\)) is the ratio of \(q\)-factorials—or equivalently, the ratio of \(q\)-Pochhammer symbols: \[\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q := \frac{[n]_q!}{[k]_q! [n-k]_q!} = \frac{(q; q)_n}{(q; q)_k (q; q)_{n-k}} = \prod_{i=1}^k \frac{1 - q^{n - k + i}}{1 - q^i}\] (with \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q = 0\) if \(k < 0\) or \(k > n\)).

ImportantLocal Algebraic Hypotheses for \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\)
  1. In the Polynomial Ring \(\mathbb{Z}[q]\): Even though the formula \(\frac{(q;q)_n}{(q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k}}\) is written as a fraction in the rational function field \(\mathbb{Q}(q)\), every cyclotomic factor \(\Phi_d(q)\) dividing the denominator \((q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k}\) divides the numerator \((q;q)_n\) to at least as high a power (\(\lfloor n/d \rfloor \ge \lfloor k/d \rfloor + \lfloor (n-k)/d \rfloor\)). Thus \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \in \mathbb{Z}[q]\) is always a genuine polynomial of degree \(k(n-k)\) with non-negative integer coefficients!
  2. In a Prime Field \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (\(q \in \mathbb{F}_p\)): To evaluate \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \bmod p\) via direct modular division \((q;q)_n \cdot ((q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k})^{-1} \bmod p\), the denominator must be a unit in \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\): we require \(q \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\) and \(1 - q^i \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\) for all \(1 \le i \le \max(k, n-k)\), i.e., the multiplicative order \(d = \operatorname{ord}_p(q)\) must satisfy \(d > \max(k, n-k)\). When \(d \le \max(k, n-k)\), direct division fails with \(0/0\) and you must use the \(q\)-Lucas Theorem (§23.2).

To verify immediately that \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \in \mathbb{N}[q]\) without factoring cyclotomic polynomials, we split the numerator factor \(1 - q^n\) in two complementary ways: \[(1 - q^n) = (1 - q^{n-k}) + q^{n-k}(1 - q^k) = q^k(1 - q^{n-k}) + (1 - q^k).\] Substituting these two splittings into \(\frac{(q;q)_n}{(q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k}} = \frac{(q;q)_{n-1}(1-q^n)}{(q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k}}\) gives the dual \(q\)-Pascal recurrences: \[\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q = \begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q + q^{n-k} \begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k-1 \end{bmatrix}_q = q^k \begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q + \begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k-1 \end{bmatrix}_q.\]

Three Concrete Combinatorial & Linear-Algebraic Models for \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\)

Before touching any generating function identity, you must be able to look at \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\) and immediately see the three concrete structures it counts.

Model 1: \(k\)-Dimensional Subspaces of \(\mathbb{F}_q^n\) and RREF Pivot Profiles

Let \(\mathbb{F}_q\) be the finite field with \(q\) elements, and let \(V = \mathbb{F}_q^n\) be the \(n\)-dimensional vector space of row vectors over \(\mathbb{F}_q\). Question: How many distinct \(k\)-dimensional linear subspaces \(W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n\) exist?

We perform a double count on ordered linearly independent \(k\)-tuples \((v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k) \in V^k\): - Way 1 (Vector by vector): The first vector \(v_1\) can be any non-zero vector in \(\mathbb{F}_q^n\), giving \(q^n - 1\) choices. Once \(v_1, \dots, v_i\) are chosen and span an \(i\)-dimensional subspace of size \(q^i\), the next vector \(v_{i+1}\) can be any vector in \(\mathbb{F}_q^n\) outside that span, giving \(q^n - q^i\) choices. Thus there are \(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(q^n - q^i)\) ordered linearly independent \(k\)-tuples in \(\mathbb{F}_q^n\). - Way 2 (Subspace first, then basis): Choose a \(k\)-dimensional subspace \(W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n\). Inside this fixed \(W \cong \mathbb{F}_q^k\), by the exact same logic with \(n\) replaced by \(k\), there are \(\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(q^k - q^i)\) ordered bases \((v_1, \dots, v_k)\) of \(W\).

Dividing the total number of ordered independent \(k\)-tuples by the number of ordered bases per subspace yields: \[\#\{W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n : \dim W = k\} = \frac{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(q^n - q^i)}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(q^k - q^i)} = \prod_{i=0}^{k-1} \frac{q^i(q^{n-i} - 1)}{q^i(q^{k-i} - 1)} = \prod_{j=1}^k \frac{1 - q^{n-k+j}}{1 - q^j} = \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q.\]

Why is this rational expression a polynomial in \(q\) with \(\binom{n}{k}\) terms at \(q=1\)? Gaussian elimination provides the constructive bijection! Every \(k\)-dimensional subspace \(W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n\) has a unique \(k \times n\) basis matrix in Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF):

  1. Row \(i\) (\(1 \le i \le k\)) has its leading \(1\) (pivot) in column \(c_i\), with \(1 \le c_1 < c_2 < \dots < c_k \le n\). There are \(\binom{n}{k}\) possible choices for the pivot column set \(C = \{c_1, \dots, c_k\}\).
  2. In row \(i\), all entries to the left of column \(c_i\) are \(0\), and all entries in later pivot columns \(c_{i+1}, \dots, c_k\) are \(0\).
  3. The remaining \((n - c_i) - (k - i)\) non-pivot columns to the right of \(c_i\) can be arbitrary elements of \(\mathbb{F}_q\)!

Thus a fixed pivot profile \(1 \le c_1 < \dots < c_k \le n\) contributes \(q^{\sum_{i=1}^k ((n - c_i) - (k - i))}\) subspaces, and summing over all \(\binom{n}{k}\) pivot profiles gives \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\).

Model 2: Binary Words by Inversion Number

Consider all \(\binom{n}{k}\) binary strings \(w = w_1 w_2 \dots w_n \in \{0, 1\}^n\) containing \(k\) ones and \(n-k\) zeros. An inversion in \(w\) is a pair of indices \(1 \le i < j \le n\) such that \(w_i = 1\) and \(w_j = 0\); denote the number of inversions by \(\operatorname{inv}(w)\). If the \(k\) ones are located at positions \(1 \le c_1 < c_2 < \dots < c_k \le n\) (matching the RREF pivot columns in Model 1!), then the \(i\)-th one at position \(c_i\) has \((n - c_i) - (k - i)\) zeros to its right. Conditioning on the last bit \(w_n \in \{0, 1\}\) reproduces the \(q\)-Pascal recurrence: - If \(w_n = 0\), the last zero forms \(k\) inversions with all \(k\) ones preceding it, contributing \(q^k \begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\). - If \(w_n = 1\), the last one has no zeros to its right, contributing \(\begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k-1 \end{bmatrix}_q\).

Hence: \[\sum_{\substack{w \in \{0,1\}^n \\ |w|_1 = k}} q^{\operatorname{inv}(w)} = \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q.\]

Model 3: Integer Partitions Fitting Inside a \(k \times (n-k)\) Box

Interpret each binary word \(w \in \{0,1\}^n\) with \(k\) ones and \(n-k\) zeros as a monotone lattice path from \((0,0)\) to \((n-k, k)\) on a grid, where 0 is an East step \((+1, 0)\) and 1 is a North step \((0, +1)\). For each North step (1), the number of East steps (0) to its right in \(w\) is the horizontal distance from that North step to the right boundary \(x = n - k\). Consequently, \(\operatorname{inv}(w)\) equals the number of unit squares lying above and to the left of the path inside the \((n-k) \times k\) bounding rectangle! Reading the row lengths from top to bottom gives an integer partition \(\lambda = (\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_k \ge 0)\) with at most \(k\) parts, each at most \(n - k\): \[\sum_{\substack{\lambda : \ell(\lambda) \le k \\ \lambda_1 \le n - k}} q^{|\lambda|} = \sum_{0 \le A_1 \le A_2 \le \dots \le A_k \le n-k} q^{\sum_{i=1}^k A_i} = \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q.\]

The \(q\)-Difference Equation Engine (\(x \mapsto qx\))

In Chapter 18, we extracted coefficients of products and powers by applying \(\frac{d}{dx}\) to create a differential equation. For \(q\)-products such as \((ax; q)_n\) or \(\frac{(ax; q)_\infty}{(x; q)_\infty}\), differentiation produces a useless sum of \(n\) terms. What operator simplifies a \(q\)-product in a single step?

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Multiplicative Shift \(x \mapsto qx\)
  • Diagnostic Signal: You encounter a generating function \(F(x)\) built from geometric \(q\)-products \(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 \pm a q^i x)^{\pm 1}\), or a sum where the \(k\)-th term contains \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\) or \((q;q)_k^{-1}\).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Instead of differentiating with respect to \(x\), replace \(x\) with \(qx\)! In a geometric product \(P(x) = \prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 - a q^i x)\), shifting \(x \mapsto qx\) shifts every index \(i \mapsto i+1\): \[P(qx) = \prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 - a q^{i+1} x) = \frac{1 - a q^n x}{1 - ax} P(x).\] All \(n-1\) interior factors cancel! Clearing the denominator yields a first-order \(q\)-difference equation \((1 - ax)P(qx) = (1 - a q^n x)P(x)\). Because \([x^k] P(qx) = q^k [x^k] P(x)\), extracting \([x^k]\) on both sides immediately gives a two-term recurrence for the coefficients \([x^k]P(x)\)!

Let us apply this engine to derive Cauchy’s Master \(q\)-Binomial Theorem and all of its specializations at once.

Theorem 23.1 (Cauchy’s Master \(q\)-Binomial Theorem)

In the formal power series ring \(\mathbb{Q}(a, q)[[x]]\) (or \(K[[x]]\) whenever \((q;q)_k\) is invertible for \(k \le N\)), \[F(x) := \frac{(ax; q)_\infty}{(x; q)_\infty} = \prod_{i=0}^\infty \frac{1 - a q^i x}{1 - q^i x} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(a; q)_k}{(q; q)_k} x^k.\]

Single-Step Verbalized Derivation: 1. Apply the multiplicative shift \(x \mapsto qx\): In the infinite product \(F(x) = \frac{(1-ax)(1-aqx)\cdots}{(1-x)(1-qx)\cdots}\), replacing \(x\) by \(qx\) strips the \(i=0\) factor \(\frac{1-ax}{1-x}\) from the front: \[F(qx) = \frac{1 - x}{1 - ax} F(x) \implies (1 - ax) F(qx) = (1 - x) F(x).\] 2. Extract \([x^k]\) on both sides: Write \(F(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k x^k\) with \(c_0 = F(0) = 1\). Since \([x^k] F(qx) = q^k c_k\) and \([x^k](x F(qx)) = q^{k-1} c_{k-1}\), comparing coefficients of \(x^k\) for \(k \ge 1\) yields: \[q^k c_k - a q^{k-1} c_{k-1} = c_k - c_{k-1}.\] 3. Solve the two-term recurrence: Grouping \(c_k\) on the left and \(c_{k-1}\) on the right gives \[(1 - q^k) c_k = (1 - a q^{k-1}) c_{k-1} \implies c_k = \frac{1 - a q^{k-1}}{1 - q^k} c_{k-1}.\] Unrolling from \(c_0 = 1\) yields \(c_k = \frac{(a; q)_k}{(q; q)_k}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Three Essential Specializations of Cauchy’s Theorem

  1. Positive (Finite) \(q\)-Binomial Theorem (Counting subsets by sum/inversions): Set \(a = q^{-n}\) and replace \(x \mapsto -x q^n\) in Theorem 23.1 (or apply \(x \mapsto qx\) directly to \(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 + q^i x)\)): \[(-x; q)_n = \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} (1 + q^i x) = \sum_{k=0}^n q^{\binom{k}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q x^k.\] Combinatorial Check: Choosing \(k\) distinct powers \(q^{i_1} < q^{i_2} < \dots < q^{i_k}\) from \(\{q^0, q^1, \dots, q^{n-1}\}\) is equivalent to subtracting the minimum staircase \(0, 1, \dots, k-1\) (whose sum is \(\binom{k}{2}\)) to leave a partition \(0 \le A_1 \le \dots \le A_k \le n - k\) inside a \(k \times (n-k)\) box (Model 3, GF \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\)).
  2. Negative \(q\)-Binomial Theorem (Multisets / bounded parts): Set \(a = q^n\) in Theorem 23.1 so that \(\frac{(q^n x; q)_\infty}{(x; q)_\infty} = \frac{1}{(x; q)_n}\) and \(\frac{(q^n; q)_k}{(q; q)_k} = \begin{bmatrix} n + k - 1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\): \[\frac{1}{(x; q)_n} = \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{1}{1 - q^i x} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \begin{bmatrix} n + k - 1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q x^k.\]
  3. Euler’s Two Infinite \(q\)-Exponential Series (\(n \to \infty\)): Sending \(n \to \infty\) in the positive and negative \(q\)-binomial theorems (since \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q = \frac{1}{(q; q)_k}\) in \(\mathbb{Z}[[q]]\)) yields: \[(-x; q)_\infty = \prod_{i=0}^\infty (1 + q^i x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{q^{\binom{k}{2}}}{(q; q)_k} x^k, \qquad \frac{1}{(x; q)_\infty} = \prod_{i=0}^\infty \frac{1}{1 - q^i x} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{(q; q)_k} x^k.\]

Failure Mode #9: Division by Zero in \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \bmod p\) when \(\operatorname{ord}_p(q) \le n\)

Suppose a problem asks for \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \bmod p\) where \(p\) is a prime (or asks for \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q \bmod (q^P - 1)\) as in ARC 145 F), and \(n\) is larger than the multiplicative order \(d = \operatorname{ord}_p(q)\) of \(q\) modulo \(p\) (the smallest positive integer \(d \ge 1\) such that \(q^d \equiv 1 \pmod p\)).

If you try to precompute \(q\)-factorials \([m]_q! \bmod p\) and multiply by modular inverses, your code will immediately crash or produce garbage: as soon as \(m \ge d\), the factor \(1 - q^d \equiv 0 \pmod p\) makes \([d]_q \equiv 0 \pmod p\), so \([m]_q! \equiv 0 \pmod p\) for all \(m \ge d\)! Even worse, in \(\frac{(q;q)_n}{(q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k}}\), both the numerator and the denominator contain multiples of \(1 - q^d\), producing an indeterminate \(0/0 \pmod p\).

Theorem 23.2 (The \(q\)-Lucas Theorem)

Let \(q\) be a primitive \(d\)-th root of unity in a field \(K\) (or work in the quotient ring \(\mathbb{Z}[q]/(\Phi_d(q))\)). Write \(n = n_1 d + n_0\) and \(k = k_1 d + k_0\) with integers \(n_1, k_1 \ge 0\) and remainders \(0 \le n_0, k_0 < d\). Then: \[\begin{bmatrix} n_1 d + n_0 \\ k_1 d + k_0 \end{bmatrix}_q = \binom{n_1}{k_1} \begin{bmatrix} n_0 \\ k_0 \end{bmatrix}_q.\] Notice the magic on the right-hand side: 1. The quotient factor \(\binom{n_1}{k_1}\) is an ordinary integer binomial coefficient (\(q = 1\))! (If \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) and \(n_1 \ge p\), you reduce \(\binom{n_1}{k_1} \bmod p\) using the classical Lucas theorem base \(p\).) 2. The remainder factor \(\begin{bmatrix} n_0 \\ k_0 \end{bmatrix}_q\) has \(n_0, k_0 < d\), so every denominator factor \(1 - q^i\) (\(1 \le i \le k_0 < d\)) is strictly non-zero and invertible!

Single-Step Verbalized Derivation: 1. Period-by-period factorization: Since \(q^d = 1\), the product \(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 + q^i x)\) splits into \(n_1\) identical blocks of length \(d\) and one trailing block of length \(n_0\): \[\prod_{i=0}^{n-1} (1 + q^i x) = \left(\prod_{j=0}^{d-1} (1 + q^j x)\right)^{n_1} \prod_{i=0}^{n_0-1} (1 + q^i x) = \left(1 + (-1)^{d-1} x^d\right)^{n_1} \sum_{r=0}^{n_0} q^{\binom{r}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} n_0 \\ r \end{bmatrix}_q x^r.\] 2. Extract \([x^k]\) for \(k = k_1 d + k_0\): Because \(0 \le r \le n_0 < d\), a term \(x^{m d} \cdot x^r\) can equal \(x^{k_1 d + k_0}\) if and only if \(m = k_1\) and \(r = k_0\). Extracting \([x^{k_1 d + k_0}]\) on both sides gives: \[q^{\binom{k_1 d + k_0}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} n_1 d + n_0 \\ k_1 d + k_0 \end{bmatrix}_q = \binom{n_1}{k_1} (-1)^{(d-1)k_1} q^{\binom{k_0}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} n_0 \\ k_0 \end{bmatrix}_q.\] 3. Cancel the \(q\)-powers: Expand \(\binom{k_1 d + k_0}{2} = d\binom{k_1}{2} + k_1 k_0 d + \binom{k_0}{2} + \binom{d}{2} k_1\). Since \(q^d = 1\), we have \(q^{d\binom{k_1}{2} + k_1 k_0 d} = 1\). Furthermore, \(q^{\binom{d}{2}} = (-1)^{d-1}\) (if \(d\) is odd, \(\binom{d}{2}\) is a multiple of \(d\) so \(q^{\binom{d}{2}} = 1 = (-1)^{d-1}\); if \(d\) is even, \(q^{d/2} = -1\) so \(q^{d(d-1)/2} = (-1)^{d-1} = -1\)). Thus \(q^{\binom{k_1 d + k_0}{2}} = (-1)^{(d-1)k_1} q^{\binom{k_0}{2}}\), and cancelling this non-zero scalar leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q = \binom{n_1}{k_1} \begin{bmatrix} n_0 \\ k_0 \end{bmatrix}_q\). \(\blacksquare\)

The Dual \(q\)-Exponentials and Chirp-Z \(q\)-Convolution

In Chapter 4, the ordinary exponential generating function (EGF) \(\sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n!} x^n\) converted binomial convolutions \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} a_k b_{n-k}\) into Cauchy products. How do we multiply sequences weighted by Gaussian \(q\)-binomials \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time?

Because \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q = \frac{(q;q)_n}{(q;q)_k (q;q)_{n-k}}\), any sum of the form \[c_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q a_k b_{n-k} \quad \text{or} \quad g_n = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^{n-k} q^{\binom{n-k}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q f_k\] becomes a standard Cauchy convolution after dividing by \((q;q)_n\): \[\frac{c_n}{(q; q)_n} = \sum_{k=0}^n \left(\frac{a_k}{(q; q)_k}\right) \left(\frac{b_{n-k}}{(q; q)_{n-k}}\right), \qquad \frac{g_n}{(q; q)_n} = \sum_{k=0}^n \left(\frac{f_k}{(q; q)_k}\right) \left(\frac{(-1)^{n-k} q^{\binom{n-k}{2}}}{(q; q)_{n-k}}\right).\] Notice the two universal series appearing on the right when \(a_k = 1\) and \(b_k = (-1)^k q^{\binom{k}{2}}\): \[e_q(x) := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^n}{(q; q)_n} = \frac{1}{(x; q)_\infty}, \qquad E_q(x) := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{q^{\binom{n}{2}} x^n}{(q; q)_n} = (-x; q)_\infty.\] Because \(\frac{1}{(x; q)_\infty} \cdot (x; q)_\infty = 1\), these two \(q\)-exponentials satisfy the fundamental duality \[e_q(x) E_q(-x) = 1.\] What if the convolution also contains a cross-power \(q^{k(n-k)}\) or \(q^{k n}\), such as in \(q\)-Vandermonde convolution or evaluating a polynomial at \(x = q^0, q^1, \dots, q^N\)? Recall Bluestein’s identity from Chapter 7: \[k(n - k) = \binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2} - \binom{n-k}{2}, \qquad k n = \binom{n+k}{2} - \binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2}.\] Absorbing \(q^{-\binom{k}{2}}\) into the \(k\)-th term, \(q^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}\) into the \((n-k)\)-th term, and \(q^{\binom{n}{2}}\) into the output \(c_n\) reduces every \(q\)-Vandermonde sum and every \(q\)-power evaluation to a single \(O(N \log N)\) NTT!

23.3 Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem, Jacobi’s Triple Product & Durfee Square Decomposition

In Chapter 3, we saw that the partition generating function \(\prod_{k=1}^\infty (1 - q^k)^{-1} = \frac{1}{(q;q)_\infty}\) can be inverted in \(O(N \log N)\) time because its reciprocal \((q; q)_\infty\) is ultra-sparse. Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem & Franklin’s Involution

Theorem 23.3 (Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem)

In \(\mathbb{Z}[[q]]\), the infinite \(q\)-Pochhammer product \((q; q)_\infty\) expands as a sparse series supported solely on the generalized pentagonal numbers \(\omega_m := \frac{m(3m-1)}{2}\) (\(m \in \mathbb{Z}\)): \[(q; q)_\infty = \prod_{k=1}^\infty (1 - q^k) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty (-1)^m q^{m(3m-1)/2} = 1 - q - q^2 + q^5 + q^7 - q^{12} - q^{15} + \dots\]

Concrete Combinatorial Proof (Franklin’s Involution): Expanding \(\prod_{k=1}^\infty (1 - q^k)\) gives \(\sum_{\lambda \in \mathcal{D}} (-1)^{\ell(\lambda)} q^{|\lambda|}\), where \(\mathcal{D}\) is the set of partitions \(\lambda = (\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \dots > \lambda_r > 0)\) into distinct parts. For any non-empty \(\lambda \in \mathcal{D}\), define two parameters on its Ferrers diagram: - The base \(b(\lambda) := \lambda_r\) (the size of the smallest row at the bottom). - The slope \(s(\lambda)\): the length of the longest contiguous \(45^\circ\) diagonal staircase at the right end of the top rows, i.e., the largest integer \(s \ge 1\) such that \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_s\) are consecutive integers (\(\lambda_i = \lambda_1 - i + 1\) for \(1 \le i \le s\)).

Now define a sign-reversing map \(\phi : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{D}\) by comparing \(b(\lambda)\) and \(s(\lambda)\): 1. Case A (\(b(\lambda) \le s(\lambda)\)): Remove the bottom row of length \(b(\lambda)\) and append \(1\) box to the end of each of the first \(b(\lambda)\) rows. The number of rows decreases by \(1\) (flipping the sign \((-1)^{\ell(\lambda)}\)), unless the bottom row itself was part of the slope staircase and \(b(\lambda) = s(\lambda) = r\) (meaning \(\lambda = (2r-1, 2r-2, \dots, r)\) of weight \(r(3r-1)/2\)). 2. Case B (\(b(\lambda) > s(\lambda)\)): Remove \(1\) box from the end of each of the first \(s(\lambda)\) rows and form a new bottom row of size \(s(\lambda)\). This is the exact inverse of Case A, except when the \(s(\lambda)\)-th row was the bottom row and \(b(\lambda) = s(\lambda) + 1 = r + 1\) (meaning \(\lambda = (2r, 2r-1, \dots, r+1)\) of weight \(r(3r+1)/2\)).

Every partition into distinct parts cancels in pairs \((\lambda, \phi(\lambda))\) of opposite sign except the two exceptional families \(m(3m-1)/2\) (\(m \ge 0\)) and \(m(3m+1)/2\) (\(m \ge 1\)), which each have \(m\) rows and contribute \((-1)^m q^{m(3m \mp 1)/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Jacobi’s Triple Product Identity

When we track both a multiplicative weight \(z^{\text{charge}}\) and \(q^{\text{energy}}\), Euler’s pentagonal product generalizes to a symmetric Laurent series in \(z\):

Theorem 23.4 (Jacobi’s Triple Product Identity)

In \(\mathbb{Z}[z, z^{-1}][[q]]\), \[\prod_{k=1}^\infty (1 - q^k)(1 + z q^k)(1 + z^{-1} q^{k-1}) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty z^m q^{\binom{m+1}{2}}.\] (Symmetrical form under \(q \mapsto q^2, z \mapsto z q^{-1}\)): \[\prod_{k=1}^\infty (1 - q^{2k})(1 + z q^{2k-1})(1 + z^{-1} q^{2k-1}) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty z^m q^{m^2}.\]

2-Line Proof of Euler’s Pentagonal Theorem from Jacobi’s Triple Product: In the first form of Theorem 23.4, replace \(q \mapsto q^3\) and set \(z = -q^{-1}\). Then \((1 - q^{3k})(1 - q^{3k-1})(1 - q^{3k-2})\) runs over all factors \((1 - q^n)\) (\(n \ge 1\)), while the right-hand side becomes \(\sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^m q^{3\binom{m+1}{2} - m} = \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^m q^{m(3m+1)/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The Durfee Square Decomposition (\(O(N\sqrt{N})\) Division-Free Engine)

Suppose you need the partition numbers \(p(0), \dots, p(N)\) modulo an arbitrary composite integer \(M\) (where NTT does not exist), or you need to transform a bivariate infinite product \(\prod_{k=1}^\infty (1 - x q^k)^{-1}\) into a rapidly converging sum where the \(d\)-th term starts at \(q^{d^2}\).

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Durfee Square Decomposition
  • Diagnostic Signal: You have a partition or distinct-part generating function \(\frac{1}{(xq; q)_\infty}\) or \((-xq; q)_\infty\), and either (a) the modulus \(M\) forbids NTT, or (b) you want to eliminate the infinite product in favor of a finite sum of length \(\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor\) whose denominators \((q;q)_d\) have degree \(d \le \sqrt{N}\).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Look at the Ferrers diagram of any integer partition \(\lambda = (\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_\ell > 0)\). Let \(d\) be the side length of its Durfee square—the largest \(d \times d\) square of unit boxes fitting in the top-left corner of the diagram (\(d = \max\{i : \lambda_i \ge i\}\)). Once this \(d \times d\) square (containing \(d^2\) boxes) is removed, the remaining boxes split into two independent pieces:
    1. To the right of the \(d \times d\) square: A partition \(\alpha\) with at most \(d\) rows (whose conjugate \(\alpha'\) is a partition into parts \(\le d\), generated by \(\frac{1}{(q;q)_d}\)).
    2. Below the \(d \times d\) square: A partition \(\beta\) whose parts are all \(\le d\) (generated by \(\frac{1}{(q;q)_d}\), or \(\frac{1}{(xq; q)_d}\) if \(x\) tracks the total number of rows \(d + \ell(\beta)\)).

Corollary 23.1 (Durfee Square Identities)

Summing over all possible Durfee square sizes \(d \ge 0\) gives the Durfee Square Identity: \[\frac{1}{(xq; q)_\infty} = \prod_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1 - x q^k} = \sum_{d=0}^\infty \frac{q^{d^2} x^d}{(q; q)_d (xq; q)_d}, \qquad \frac{1}{(q; q)_\infty} = \sum_{d=0}^\infty \frac{q^{d^2}}{(q; q)_d^2}.\] Notice that for \(d > \lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor\), \(q^{d^2} \equiv 0 \pmod{q^{N+1}}\)! Thus the infinite sum truncates to at most \(\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor + 1\) terms. Because dividing a series by \(1 - q^d\) is a simple \(O(N)\) prefix-sum pass dp[i] += dp[i - d], we can maintain the series \(A_d(q) = \frac{1}{(q;q)_d^2} \bmod q^{N+1}\) incrementally from \(d-1\) to \(d\) using two in-place unbounded knapsack passes of step \(d\) and accumulate \(q^{d^2} A_d(q)\) in \(O(N\sqrt{N})\) time and \(O(N)\) space using only additions!

Hand-Checkable Concrete Trace (\(N = 5\))

Let us verify \(\sum_{d=0}^2 \frac{q^{d^2}}{(q;q)_d^2} \bmod q^6\): - \(d = 0\): \(q^0 / 1 = 1\). - \(d = 1\): \(q^1 / (1-q)^2 = q(1 + 2q + 3q^2 + 4q^3 + 5q^4 + \dots) = q + 2q^2 + 3q^3 + 4q^4 + 5q^5 \pmod{q^6}\). - \(d = 2\): \(q^4 / ((1-q)^2(1-q^2)^2) = q^4(1 + 2q + \dots) = q^4 + 2q^5 \pmod{q^6}\). - Sum: \(1 + q + 2q^2 + 3q^3 + (4+1)q^4 + (5+2)q^5 = 1 + q + 2q^2 + 3q^3 + 5q^4 + 7q^5\), matching \(p(0..5) = (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7)\)!

// Non-standard kernel: Division-free O(N sqrt(N)) Durfee square partition engine mod M
vector<int> partitions_durfee_mod(int N, int M) {
    vector<int> ans(N + 1, 0), ways(N + 1, 0);
    ways[0] = ans[0] = 1; // d = 0: 1 / (q;q)_0^2 = 1
    for (int d = 1; d * d <= N; ++d) {
        // Multiply ways(q) by 1 / (1 - q^d)^2 via two forward knapsack passes
        for (int pass = 0; pass < 2; ++pass)
            for (int i = d; i <= N - d * d; ++i)
                if ((ways[i] += ways[i - d]) >= M) ways[i] -= M;
        for (int i = 0; i <= N - d * d; ++i)
            if ((ans[i + d * d] += ways[i]) >= M) ans[i + d * d] -= M;
    }
    return ans;
}
NoteAlgorithm Contract: Durfee Square \(O(N\sqrt{N})\) Partition Engine
  • Input & Preconditions: Degree bound \(N \ge 0\) and arbitrary modulus \(M \ge 2\) (need not be prime; uses only addition modulo \(M\)).
  • Postconditions (Output): Array ans[0..N] where ans[n] \(= [q^n]\frac{1}{(q;q)_\infty} \bmod M\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N\sqrt{N})\) time and \(O(N)\) auxiliary space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: At the end of iteration \(d\), ways[i] equals \([q^i]\frac{1}{(q;q)_d^2} \bmod M\) for all \(0 \le i \le N - d^2\), accounting for all partitions whose Durfee square has side length exactly \(d\).
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: Iterating the knapsack pass i downward instead of forward (for (int i = d; i <= N - d * d; ++i)) computes \(0/1\) subset sums \((1 + q^d)^2\) instead of unbounded geometric series \(\frac{1}{(1 - q^d)^2}\); upper-bounding i by N instead of N - d * d wastes \(2\times\) constant factor on coefficients that shift past \(q^N\).

Proposition 23.1 (Vector Space & Subspace Enumeration over \(\mathbb{F}_q\))

Let us assemble a compact dictionary of linear-algebraic counts over \(\mathbb{F}_q\) that appear repeatedly in competitive programming:

When a problem specifies an easy-to-count quantity \(F(V)\) for every subspace \(V \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n\) (such as “all sequences/subsets contained inside \(V\)”), and asks for the quantity \(G(V)\) of objects whose exact linear span is \(V\), we have \(F(V) = \sum_{U \subseteq V} G(U)\). Whenever \(F(V) = f_{\dim V}\) and \(G(V) = g_{\dim V}\) depend only on dimension, grouping by \(k = \dim U\) (since \(V\) contains \(\begin{bmatrix} r \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q\) subspaces of dimension \(k\)) gives \(f_r = \sum_{k=0}^r \begin{bmatrix} r \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q g_k\), which we invert immediately via \(q\)-Binomial Inversion (§23.1): \[g_r = \sum_{k=0}^r (-1)^{r-k} q^{\binom{r-k}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} r \\ k \end{bmatrix}_q f_k.\]

Self-Contained 5-Line Hypercube Character & Krawtchouk / MacWilliams Duality

Suppose you are given \(n\) integers \(a_1, \dots, a_n \in [0, 2^m)\) (\(m \le 53\)) and asked to count, for every \(0 \le k \le m\), how many subsets of \(\{a_1, \dots, a_n\}\) have an XOR sum with \(\operatorname{popcount} = k\) (CF 1336E2).

First, run Gaussian elimination over \(\mathbb{F}_2^m\) in \(O(nm)\) time to extract a basis of rank \(r \le m\) spanning the subspace (binary linear code) \(\mathcal{C} = \operatorname{span}(a_1, \dots, a_n) \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^m\). Every vector \(v \in \mathcal{C}\) is produced by exactly \(2^{n-r}\) subsets of \(\{a_1, \dots, a_n\}\). Thus the problem reduces to computing the Hamming weight enumerator of \(\mathcal{C}\): \[A_k := \#\{v \in \mathcal{C} : \operatorname{popcount}(v) = k\} \quad (0 \le k \le m).\]

If \(r \le \lfloor m/2 \rfloor\) (\(r \le 26\)), we can simply enumerate all \(2^r\) elements of \(\mathcal{C}\) in \(O(2^r)\) time. What if \(r > m/2\) (say \(r = 45, m = 53\))? Enumerating \(2^{45}\) vectors is impossible!

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Dual Subspace \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) & MacWilliams Identity
  • Diagnostic Signal: You need the Hamming weight distribution \(A_0, \dots, A_m\) (or a coordinate-separable polynomial sum) over a linear subspace \(\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^m\) of high dimension \(r > m/2\).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: While \(\mathcal{C}\) has dimension \(r\) and size \(2^r\), its orthogonal complement (dual code) under the \(\mathbb{F}_2\) dot product \(\langle u, v \rangle := \bigoplus_{t=1}^m u_t v_t = \operatorname{popcount}(u \ \& \ v) \bmod 2\), \[\mathcal{C}^\perp := \{u \in \mathbb{F}_2^m : \forall v \in \mathcal{C},\; \langle u, v \rangle = 0\},\] has dimension \(m - r\) and size \(2^{m-r} \le 2^{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor}\)! How do we relate the weight counts \(B_i := \#\{u \in \mathcal{C}^\perp : \operatorname{popcount}(u) = i\}\) of \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) to the weight counts \(A_j\) of \(\mathcal{C}\)? By writing the indicator function of \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) as the average of \((-1)^{\langle u, v \rangle}\) over \(v \in \mathcal{C}\)!

Theorem 23.5 (Self-Contained 5-Line Derivation of MacWilliams / Krawtchouk Duality)

Let \(\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^m\) be an \(r\)-dimensional subspace with weight counts \(A_0, \dots, A_m\), and let \(\mathcal{C}^\perp \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^m\) be its \((m-r)\)-dimensional orthogonal complement with weight counts \(B_0, \dots, B_m\). Then:

  1. Line 1 (Subspace Character Orthogonality): For any vector \(u \in \mathbb{F}_2^m\), the linear form \(v \mapsto \langle u, v \rangle\) on \(\mathcal{C}\) is either identically \(0\) (when \(u \in \mathcal{C}^\perp\), so all \(2^r\) terms \((-1)^{\langle u, v \rangle}\) equal \(+1\)) or surjective onto \(\{0, 1\}\) (when \(u \notin \mathcal{C}^\perp\), so \(2^{r-1}\) terms are \(+1\) and \(2^{r-1}\) terms are \(-1\)): \[\mathbf{1}_{u \in \mathcal{C}^\perp} = \frac{1}{2^r} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} (-1)^{\langle u, v \rangle}.\]
  2. Line 2 (Weight Generating Function of \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\)): Multiply both sides by \(z^{\operatorname{popcount}(u)}\), sum over all \(u \in \mathbb{F}_2^m\), and interchange the finite sums: \[\sum_{i=0}^m B_i z^i = \sum_{u \in \mathbb{F}_2^m} \mathbf{1}_{u \in \mathcal{C}^\perp} z^{\operatorname{popcount}(u)} = \frac{1}{2^r} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}} \sum_{u \in \{0,1\}^m} (-1)^{\langle u, v \rangle} z^{\operatorname{popcount}(u)}.\]
  3. Line 3 (Coordinate Factorization): Since \(\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{t=1}^m u_t v_t\) and \(\operatorname{popcount}(u) = \sum_{t=1}^m u_t\) decompose coordinate-by-coordinate, the inner sum over \(u \in \{0,1\}^m\) factors into a product of \(m\) independent 2-term sums: \[\sum_{u \in \{0,1\}^m} \prod_{t=1}^m (-1)^{u_t v_t} z^{u_t} = \prod_{t=1}^m \left(1 + (-1)^{v_t} z\right).\]
  4. Line 4 (Grouping by Hamming Weight of \(v\)): If \(v \in \mathcal{C}\) has \(\operatorname{popcount}(v) = j\), then \(j\) coordinates have \(v_t = 1\) (contributing \(1 - z\)) and \(m - j\) coordinates have \(v_t = 0\) (contributing \(1 + z\)), yielding the MacWilliams Identity: \[\sum_{i=0}^m B_i z^i = \frac{1}{2^r} \sum_{j=0}^m A_j (1 - z)^j (1 + z)^{m - j}.\]
  5. Line 5 (Dual Recovery via Krawtchouk Polynomials): Because \((\mathcal{C}^\perp)^\perp = \mathcal{C}\) has dimension \(r = m - (m - r)\), swapping the roles of \((\mathcal{C}, r, A)\) and \((\mathcal{C}^\perp, m-r, B)\) and extracting \([z^k]\) expresses \(A_k\) directly as a linear combination of the dual weight counts \(B_j\): \[A_k = \frac{1}{2^{m-r}} \sum_{j=0}^m B_j K_k(j; m), \quad \text{where } K_k(j; m) := [z^k](1 - z)^j (1 + z)^{m - j} = \sum_{t=0}^k (-1)^t \binom{j}{t} \binom{m - j}{k - t}. \quad \blacksquare\]

Constructing the Dual Basis \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) in 3 Lines of Code

How do we find a basis for \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) from the RREF basis of \(\mathcal{C}\)? Suppose we permute the \(m\) bit positions (which preserves \(\operatorname{popcount}\)!) so that the \(r\) pivot columns come first. Then the \(r \times m\) RREF generator matrix of \(\mathcal{C}\) has the block form \[G = \begin{bmatrix} I_r \mid P \end{bmatrix},\] where \(I_r\) is the \(r \times r\) identity matrix and \(P\) is an \(r \times (m - r)\) binary matrix. Now consider the \((m - r) \times m\) matrix \[H := \begin{bmatrix} P^T \mid I_{m - r} \end{bmatrix}.\] Its \(m - r\) rows are linearly independent (because of the \(I_{m-r}\) block on the right), and over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), \[G H^T = I_r P + P I_{m-r} = P \oplus P = 0.\] Thus the rows of \(H = [P^T \mid I_{m-r}]\) form an explicit basis for \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\)!

Hand-Checkable Concrete Trace (\(m = 3, r = 2\))

Let \(m = 3\) and let \(\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^3\) be the \(2\)-dimensional subspace generated by the RREF matrix \[G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I_2 \mid P \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{with } P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\] - Direct enumeration of \(\mathcal{C}\): The \(2^2 = 4\) vectors in \(\mathcal{C}\) are 000 (weight \(0\)), 101, 011, and 110 (all three of weight \(2\)). Thus \((A_0, A_1, A_2, A_3) = (1, 0, 3, 0)\). - Dual basis \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\): By \(H = [P^T \mid I_1] = [1\;\; 1 \mid 1]\), the dual subspace \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) has dimension \(3 - 2 = 1\) and consists of 000 (weight \(0\)) and 111 (weight \(3\)). Thus \((B_0, B_1, B_2, B_3) = (1, 0, 0, 1)\). - MacWilliams / Krawtchouk recovery: By Line 5 of Theorem 23.5, \[\sum_{k=0}^3 A_k z^k = \frac{1}{2^1} \left( B_0 (1+z)^3 + B_3 (1-z)^3 \right) = \frac{(1 + 3z + 3z^2 + z^3) + (1 - 3z + 3z^2 - z^3)}{2} = 1 + 3z^2,\] recovering \((A_0, A_1, A_2, A_3) = (1, 0, 3, 0)\) from a 2-element dual code!

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n = 2, k = 2\))

  2. Case 2: \(x > 0\). Since \(x \ne 0\), no non-empty subsequence has XOR sum \(x\) if and only if \(x \notin W := \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{F}_2}(a_1, \dots, a_n)\). Let \(r = \dim W \in \{0, 1, \dots, \min(n, k-1)\}\).

Dividing by \(|\operatorname{GL}_r(\mathbb{F}_2)| = \prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^r - 2^i)\) gives the number of valid subspaces \(W\): \[\#\{W : \dim W = r,\; x \notin W\} = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^r (2^k - 2^i)}{\prod_{i=0}^{r-1} (2^r - 2^i)} = 2^r \begin{bmatrix} k - 1 \\ r \end{bmatrix}_2.\]

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Since \(\operatorname{span}(W \cup \{a_N\}) = W \cup (a_N \oplus W)\), we have \(1 \in \operatorname{span}(W \cup \{a_N\}) \iff a_N \in 1 \oplus W\). There are \(|1 \oplus W| = 2^r\) choices for \(a_N\).

  2. Subspace Mobius Inversion for Distinct Spanning Tuples: Let \(f_{m, r}\) be the number of \(m\)-tuples of distinct vectors in an \(r\)-dimensional space \(W \cong \mathbb{F}_2^r\) that span \(W\). Partitioning all \((2^r)^{\underline{m}}\) distinct \(m\)-tuples in \(W\) by their actual span \(U \subseteq W\) (\(\dim U = j\)) gives \[(2^r)^{\underline{m}} = \sum_{j=0}^r \begin{bmatrix} r \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 f_{m, j}.\] By Grassmannian Subspace Mobius Inversion (Section 23.1 / Proposition 23.1), substituting \(\begin{bmatrix} r \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 = \frac{(2; 2)_r}{(2; 2)_{r-j} (2; 2)_j}\) yields \[f_{m, r} = \sum_{j=0}^r (-1)^{r-j} 2^{\binom{r-j}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} r \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 (2^j)^{\underline{m}} = \sum_{j=0}^r (-1)^{r-j} 2^{\binom{r-j}{2}} \frac{(2; 2)_r}{(2; 2)_{r-j} (2; 2)_j} (2^j)^{\underline{m}} = (2; 2)_r \sum_{j=0}^r \frac{(-1)^{r-j} 2^{\binom{r-j}{2}}}{(2; 2)_{r-j}} \cdot \frac{(2^j)^{\underline{m}}}{(2; 2)_j}.\]

  3. Fast Multipoint Evaluation of \((2^j)^{\underline{m}}\) via Bluestein CZT: Let \(P(x) = x^{\underline{m}} = \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(x - i) = \sum_{k=0}^m c_k x^k\). Then for each \(0 \le j \le m\): \[(2^j)^{\underline{m}} = \sum_{k=0}^m c_k 2^{j k} = 2^{-\binom{j}{2}} \sum_{k=0}^m \left(c_k 2^{-\binom{k}{2}}\right) 2^{\binom{j+k}{2}},\] which is a single correlation convolution in \(O(m \log m)\) time!

    • \(r = 0\): \(2^0 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}_2 f_{1, 0} = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot (2^0)^{\underline{1}} = 1\).
    • \(r = 1\): \(2^2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 f_{1, 1} = 4 \cdot 1 \cdot \left((2^1)^{\underline{1}} - (2^0)^{\underline{1}}\right) = 4 \cdot (2 - 1) = 4\).
    • Total = \(1 + 4 = 5\), matching the sample trace.

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Think of an \(n \times n \times n\) binary cube as a trilinear tensor or as \(n\) matrix slices \(M_1, \dots, M_n \in \mathbb{F}_2^{n \times n}\). Why is it difficult to count cubes by their exact span dimensions \((r_1, r_2, r_3)\) along multiple axes simultaneously? Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

When we fix subspaces \(U_1, U_2, U_3 \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n\) of dimensions \(s_1, s_2, s_3\) along the three axes, a cube \(T\) has all its fibers contained in \(U_1, U_2, U_3\) if and only if \(T \in U_1 \otimes U_2 \otimes U_3\), which gives a pure power of \(2\): \(2^{s_1 s_2 s_3}\) choices! How do we sum \(\sum_{r, s} \alpha_r \beta_s 2^{c \cdot r s}\) over \(0 \le r, s \le n\) in \(O(n \log n)\) time?

Apply Grassmannian Subspace Mobius Inversion along each constrained axis: to go from “contained in an \(s\)-subspace” to “exact span has dimension \(r\)”, convolve with \(\frac{(-1)^{r-s} 2^{\binom{r-s}{2}}}{(2; 2)_{r-s}}\). 2. Complete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. The Rank-Subspace Duality over \(\mathbb{F}_2\): By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, an \(n \times n\) matrix \(M\) over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) has rank \(r\) if and only if its row space \(W = \operatorname{rowspan}(M) \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n\) has dimension \(r\).

    • The number of \(r\)-dimensional subspaces \(W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n\) is \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ r \end{bmatrix}_2\).
    • For a fixed \(s\)-dimensional subspace \(U \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n\), the number of matrices \(M \in \mathbb{F}_2^{n \times n}\) with \(\operatorname{rowspan}(M) \subseteq U\) is \((2^s)^n = 2^{n s}\).
    • Partitioning these \(2^{n s}\) matrices by the exact dimension \(r = \dim\operatorname{rowspan}(M) \le s\) yields the \(q\)-falling factorial identity: \[2^{n s} = \sum_{r=0}^s \begin{bmatrix} s \\ r \end{bmatrix}_2 g_r, \qquad g_r = \begin{bmatrix} n \\ r \end{bmatrix}_2 \prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^r - 2^i) = \prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^n - 2^i).\]

  2. Coupling Across Axes of the \(n \times n \times n\) Cube: View the \(n \times n \times n\) tensor \(T \in \mathbb{F}_2^{n \times n \times n}\) as a trilinear array \(T_{i, j, k}\). Fixing subspaces \(U_1, U_2, U_3 \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^n\) of dimensions \(s_1, s_2, s_3\) along the three axes, the number of tensors \(T\) supported inside \(U_1 \times U_2 \times U_3\) is \[\#\{T \in U_1 \otimes U_2 \otimes U_3\} = 2^{s_1 s_2 s_3}.\] Whether \(r_3 = n\) is fixed via Mobius inversion at \(s_3 \le n\) or summed over \(r_3\) when a \(1\)-axis slice-sum constraint weights \((r_1, r_2)\) across all \(n\) slices (e.g., \(\sum_{k=1}^n (2^{s_1 s_2})^k\) or \(2^{n s_1 s_2}\)), reducing along the third axis turns the cross-axis coupling into a bilinear sum of the form \[H_r = \sum_{s=0}^n \beta_s Q^{r s},\] coupled with Grassmannian Mobius inversion on each axis: \[f_r = \sum_{s=0}^r (-1)^{r-s} 2^{\binom{r-s}{2}} \begin{bmatrix} r \\ s \end{bmatrix}_2 H_s.\]

  3. Three-Stage \(O(n \log n)\) Pipeline:

    • Stage 1 (Subspace Moments): Construct the subspace weights \(\beta_s\) using \((2; 2)_s\) and \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ s \end{bmatrix}_2\) in \(O(n)\) time.
    • Stage 2 (Bluestein Cross-Axis Transform): Rewrite \(Q^{r s} = Q^{\binom{r+s}{2} - \binom{r}{2} - \binom{s}{2}}\) and compute \(H_r\) for all \(0 \le r \le n\) via a single length-\(2n\) NTT convolution.
    • Stage 3 (Grassmannian Mobius Inversion): Convolve \(H_s / (2; 2)_s\) with \((-1)^t 2^{\binom{t}{2}} / (2; 2)_t\) via NTT to extract the exact rank counts \(f_r\).

  • Source: AtCoder Regular Contest 134 Problem E (arc134_e)
  • Prerequisite Tags: Impartial Games, Divisor Lattice Reduction, Inclusion-Exclusion / Subset DP

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

There are \(N\) piles of stones. Initially, the \(i\)-th pile contains \(x_i\) stones, chosen from \(\{1, 2, \dots, A_i\}\) (\(1 \le N \le 200\), \(1 \le A_i \le 200\)). Two players play an impartial game: - In one move, a player chooses an integer \(m \ge 1\) such that at least one pile decreases (\(1 \le m \le \max_i x_i\)) and replaces \(x_i \gets x_i \bmod m\) for all \(i = 1, \dots, N\) simultaneously. - A player who makes all piles \(0\) (\(x_1 = \dots = x_N = 0\)) immediately loses (equivalently, \(\emptyset\) is a winning/terminal-loss trap, and you may only win by forcing your opponent into a state where every legal move \(1 \le m \le \max_i x_i\) divides all remaining pile sizes).

Count how many of the \(\prod_{i=1}^N A_i\) initial configurations \((x_1, \dots, x_N)\) are second-player winning (losing / \(\mathcal{P}\)-positions), modulo \(998244353\).

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 2, A = (2, 3)\))

There are \(2 \times 3 = 6\) initial pairs \((x_1, x_2) \in \{1, 2\} \times \{1, 2, 3\}\). Notice that the game depends only on the set \(S = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} \setminus \{0\}\) of distinct nonzero pile sizes: - \(S = \{1\}\) (from \((1, 1)\)): The only legal move is \(m = 1\), which reduces all piles to \(0\), losing immediately. Thus \(S = \{1\}\) is a \(\mathcal{P}\)-position. - \(S = \{2\}\) (from \((2, 2)\)): The legal moves are \(m = 1\) and \(m = 2\), both of which reduce \(2 \bmod m = 0\), losing immediately. Thus \(S = \{2\}\) is a \(\mathcal{P}\)-position. - \(S = \{1, 2\}, \{1, 3\}, \{2, 3\}\) (from \((1, 2), (2, 1), (1, 3), (2, 3)\)): Choosing \(m = 2\) sends every odd number to \(1\) and every even number to \(0\), reducing each of these sets to \(S' = \{1\}\) (\(\mathcal{P}\)-position). Thus all \(4\) of these pairs are \(\mathcal{N}\)-positions. - Total \(\mathcal{P}\)-position initial arrays: \(2\) (namely \((1, 1)\) and \((2, 2)\)).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Because duplicate pile sizes and \(0\)-sized piles behave identically under \(x \mapsto x \bmod m\), the game state is completely determined by the set \(S = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} \setminus \{0\} \subseteq \{1, 2, \dots, 200\}\). - Moving to \(S' = \emptyset\) loses immediately. - Whenever \(|S| = 1\), say \(S = \{a\}\), choosing \(m = a\) sends \(S \to \emptyset\), while choosing \(1 < m < a\) sends \(S \to \{a \bmod m\}\). In particular, for \(S = \{1\}\) and \(S = \{2\}\), every legal move \(1 \le m \le a\) divides \(a\) and leads to \(\emptyset\), so \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) are both \(\mathcal{P}\)-positions!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Use \(\{1\} \in \mathcal{P}\) and \(\{2\} \in \mathcal{P}\) as a modular sieve on all other states \(S\): 1. Mod \(2\) sieve: Suppose \(S \ne \{1\}\) contains at least one odd number. Then choosing \(m = 2\) sends every odd element to \(1\) and every even element to \(0\), so \(S \mapsto \{1\} \in \mathcal{P}\)! Thus any state \(S \ne \{1\}\) containing an odd number is an \(\mathcal{N}\)-position. Consequently, every \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S \ne \{1\}\) consists entirely of multiples of \(2\). 2. Mod \(4\) sieve: Suppose \(S \subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}\) and \(S \ne \{2\}\) contains at least one element \(\equiv 2 \pmod 4\). Then choosing \(m = 4\) sends \(S \mapsto \{2\} \in \mathcal{P}\)! Consequently, every \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S \notin \{\{1\}, \{2\}\}\) consists entirely of multiples of \(4\).

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Continue the modulus induction on multiples of \(4\): - What if \(S\) contains only multiples of \(4\), and we test \(m = 3\) or \(m = 8\) or \(m = 12\)? - If \(S\) is not a subset of multiples of \(3\), what can \(S \bmod 3\) be? Then \(S\) has at least one element \(\equiv 4\) or \(8 \pmod{12}\). Testing \(m = 8\): \(S \bmod 8 \subseteq \{4\}\). If \(S = \{4\}\), \(m = 3\) sends \(\{4\} \to \{1\}\) (\(\mathcal{P}\)), so \(\{4\}\) is \(\mathcal{N}\)! What about \(S = \{4, 8\}\)? - \(m = 2, 4 \implies \emptyset\) - \(m = 3 \implies \{1, 2\}\) (\(\mathcal{N}\)) - \(m = 5 \implies \{4, 3\}\) (\(\mathcal{N}\)) - \(m = 6 \implies \{4, 2\}\) (\(\mathcal{N}\)) - \(m = 7 \implies \{4, 1\}\) (\(\mathcal{N}\)) - \(m = 8 \implies \{4\}\) (\(\mathcal{N}\)) Every move from \(\{4, 8\}\) leads to an \(\mathcal{N}\)-position, so \(S = \{4, 8\}\) is a \(\mathcal{P}\)-position! - Now, for any other \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S \notin \{\{1\}, \{2\}, \{4, 8\}\}\) consisting of multiples of \(4\): - It cannot have \(S \bmod 12 = \{4\}\) (else \(m=3 \implies \{1\}\)), nor \(\{8\}\) (else \(m=3 \implies \{2\}\)), nor \(\{4, 8\}\) (else \(m=12 \implies \{4, 8\}\))! - Therefore, every remaining \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S\) must be a subset of the multiples of \(12\)! Since \(\max A_i \le 200\), there are only \(\lfloor 200 / 12 \rfloor = 16\) multiples of \(12\) in \(\{1, \dots, 200\}\): \(\{12, 24, \dots, 192\}\). We can precompute which of the \(2^{16} = 65536\) subsets of \(\{12, 24, \dots, 192\}\) are \(\mathcal{P}\)-positions in \(O(2^{16} \cdot 200)\) time, and then count initial arrays \((x_1, \dots, x_N)\) realizing each losing set \(S\)!

Complete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Modulo Reduction Sieve for \(\mathcal{P}\)-Positions: The state of the game is uniquely determined by the set \(S = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\} \setminus \{0\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, M\}\) (\(M = \max A_i \le 200\)).
    • Base states: \(\emptyset\) is terminal (\(\mathcal{N}\) in the non-empty-set formulation because transitioning to \(\emptyset\) means choosing \(m\) dividing all \(x_i\), which ends the game in a loss for the mover; equivalently \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) have no valid non-empty reductions and are \(\mathcal{P}\)).
    • Mod \(2\) sieve: If \(S \ne \{1\}\) contains an odd number, choosing \(m = 2\) sends \(S \mapsto S \bmod 2 = \{1\} \in \mathcal{P}\). Thus every \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S \ne \{1\}\) contains only even numbers.
    • Mod \(4\) sieve: If \(S \subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}\) and \(S \ne \{2\}\) contains a number \(\equiv 2 \pmod 4\), choosing \(m = 4\) sends \(S \mapsto S \bmod 4 = \{2\} \in \mathcal{P}\). Thus every \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S \notin \{\{1\}, \{2\}\}\) contains only multiples of \(4\).
    • Mod \(12\) sieve: Let \(S \subseteq 4\mathbb{Z}\) be a non-empty \(\mathcal{P}\)-position. Consider \(S \bmod 12 \setminus \{0\} \subseteq \{4, 8\}\):
      • If \(S \bmod 12 \setminus \{0\} = \{4\}\), then \(S \bmod 3 \setminus \{0\} = \{1\}\), so choosing \(m = 3\) sends \(S \mapsto \{1\} \in \mathcal{P}\), impossible.
      • If \(S \bmod 12 \setminus \{0\} = \{8\}\), then \(S \bmod 3 \setminus \{0\} = \{2\}\), so choosing \(m = 3\) sends \(S \mapsto \{2\} \in \mathcal{P}\), impossible.
      • If \(S \bmod 12 \setminus \{0\} = \{4, 8\}\): the minimal such set is \(S = \{4, 8\}\), which is easily verified to be a \(\mathcal{P}\)-position. For any \(S \ne \{4, 8\}\) with \(S \bmod 12 \setminus \{0\} = \{4, 8\}\), choosing \(m = 12\) sends \(S \mapsto \{4, 8\} \in \mathcal{P}\), impossible.
      • Hence, every other \(\mathcal{P}\)-position \(S\) has \(S \bmod 12 \setminus \{0\} = \emptyset\), meaning \(S \subseteq \{12, 24, 36, \dots, 192\}\)!
  2. Bitmask DP over the \(K = 16\) Multiples of \(12\): Let \(U = \{12, 24, \dots, 192\}\) (\(|U| = 16\)). Every subset \(S \subseteq U\) is represented by a \(16\)-bit mask. For a mask \(S \ne 0\) with maximum element \(v_{\max} = \max(S)\), choosing \(m \in \{2, \dots, v_{\max}\}\) transitions \(S\) to \(S' = \{x \bmod m : x \in S\} \setminus \{0\}\).
    • If \(S' \in \{\{1\}, \{2\}, \{4, 8\}\}\), then move \(m\) is immediately winning for the current player.
    • If \(S' \subseteq U\), then \(S'\) is another \(16\)-bit mask strictly smaller than \(S\) in maximum element (hence smaller as an integer mask!).
    • Otherwise, \(S'\) is guaranteed to be an \(\mathcal{N}\)-position by our sieve! Thus we only need to test moduli \(m \in \{2, \dots, 192\}\) in increasing order of mask \(S = 1, \dots, 2^{16}-1\) to classify all \(\mathcal{P}\)-positions in \(U\).
  3. Counting Arrays \((x_1, \dots, x_N)\) Realizing Each \(\mathcal{P}\)-Position \(S\):
    • For \(S = \{1\}\): every \(x_i\) must be \(1\), which gives \(1\) array (since \(A_i \ge 1\)).
    • For \(S = \{2\}\): every \(x_i\) must be \(2\), which gives \(1\) array if \(\min_i A_i \ge 2\), else \(0\).
    • For \(S = \{4, 8\}\): by inclusion-exclusion on subsets of \(\{4, 8\}\), the number of arrays with \(\{x_1, \dots, x_N\} = \{4, 8\}\) is \(2^N - 2\) if \(\min_i A_i \ge 8\), or \(1^N - 1 = 0\) otherwise (more precisely, \(\prod_{i=1}^N |\{4, 8\} \cap [1, A_i]| - \prod_{i=1}^N |\{4\} \cap [1, A_i]| - \prod_{i=1}^N |\{8\} \cap [1, A_i]|\)).
    • For \(S \subseteq U\): let \(c(T) = \prod_{i=1}^N |T \cap [1, A_i]|\) be the number of arrays whose elements all belong to \(T \subseteq U\). Notice that \(|T \cap [1, A_i]|\) depends only on the number of elements of \(T\) that are \(\le A_i\). By SOS DP (Fast Zeta/Mobius Transform on the 16-bit hypercube) or direct inclusion-exclusion over the losing masks \(S \subseteq U\), we sum the exact realizations of all losing masks \(S \subseteq U\) in \(O(K 2^K + N K)\) time!

Advanced 1 (#062) — Many Xor Optimization Problems

  • Source: AtCoder Regular Contest 139 Problem F (arc139_f)
  • Prerequisite Tags: RREF Linear Basis over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), Gaussian \(q\)-Binomials (\(q=2\)), Linearity of Expectation over Pivot Columns

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

For an array \(A = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N)\) of \(N\) integers in \([0, 2^M)\), let \(f(A)\) denote the maximum bitwise XOR sum of a subsequence of \(A\). Given \(N\) and \(M\) (\(1 \le N, M \le 2.5\times 10^5\)), compute the sum of \(f(A)\) over all \(2^{NM}\) arrays \(A \in [0, 2^M)^N\), modulo \(998244353\), in \(O(N + M)\) time.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 2, M = 2\))

There are \(2^{2 \times 2} = 16\) pairs \((a_1, a_2) \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}^2\): - Rank \(r = 0\): \(1\) pair \((0, 0)\) with \(f(A) = 0\). - Rank \(r = 1\): \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 = 3\) non-zero patterns per 1D subspace: - Span \(\{0, 1\}\) (\(p_1 = 0\)): \(3\) pairs with \(f(A) = 1\). - Span \(\{0, 2\}\) (\(p_1 = 1\), non-pivot bit \(0\) is \(0\)): \(3\) pairs with \(f(A) = 2\). - Span \(\{0, 3\}\) (\(p_1 = 1\), non-pivot bit \(0\) is \(1\)): \(3\) pairs with \(f(A) = 3\). - Rank \(r = 2\): \(\prod_{i=0}^1 (2^2 - 2^i) = 3 \times 2 = 6\) pairs spanning all of \(\mathbb{F}_2^2\), each having \(f(A) = 3\). - Total sum: \(1 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot (1 + 2 + 3) + 6 \cdot 3 = 18 + 18 = 36\).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

How do you compute the maximum subsequence XOR \(f(A)\) of a single array \(A\)? By Gaussian elimination! Put the subspace \(W = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{F}_2}(a_1, \dots, a_N) \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^M\) into Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF) from the most significant bit (\(M - 1\)) down to the least significant bit (\(0\)). Let \(r = \dim W\) be the rank, and let \(M - 1 \ge p_1 > p_2 > \dots > p_r \ge 0\) be the bit positions of the \(r\) pivot columns (where row \(i\) has leading bit \(p_i\) equal to \(1\), and every other row \(j \ne i\) has bit \(p_i\) equal to \(0\)). Then to maximize the XOR sum greedily from bit \(M-1\) down to \(0\), we must include every RREF basis row \(1, 2, \dots, r\)!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Since \(f(A)\) is simply the bitwise XOR of all \(r\) rows of the RREF basis of \(W = \operatorname{span}(A)\), let’s analyze each bit position \(b \in \{0, 1, \dots, M - 1\}\) in \(f(A)\) across all \(r\)-dimensional subspaces \(W \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^M\) with fixed highest pivot \(p_1\): 1. Every pivot bit \(p_i\) (\(1 \le i \le r\)): In RREF, row \(i\) has a \(1\) in bit \(p_i\) and all other \(r - 1\) rows have a \(0\) in bit \(p_i\). Therefore, in the XOR sum of all \(r\) rows, every pivot bit \(p_i\) is ALWAYS \(1\)! 2. Every bit \(b > p_1\) above the highest pivot: All \(r\) rows are \(0\) at bit \(b\), so bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) is ALWAYS \(0\). 3. Every non-pivot bit \(b < p_1\) below the highest pivot: Since \(b < p_1\), at least row \(1\) (the highest pivot row, with \(p_1 > b\)) has a free entry in column \(b\) that can be chosen freely in \(\{0, 1\}\)! Therefore, among all \(r\)-subspaces with a fixed pivot set \(\{p_1, \dots, p_r\}\), the XOR sum of column \(b\) is \(1\) in exactly \(1/2\) of the subspaces!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Because the maximum subsequence XOR \(f(A)\) is determined by greedy MSB-to-LSB elimination, whether bit \(b \in \{0, 1, \dots, M - 1\}\) of \(f(A)\) equals \(1\) depends only on the \(m = M - 1 - b\) bits strictly above \(b\) and bit \(b\) itself (while the \(b\) bits below \(b\) have \(2^{N b}\) completely independent choices)! Condition on the rank \(j \in [0, \min(N, m)]\) of the \(m \times N\) binary matrix formed by the \(m\) bits strictly above \(b\): - By Proposition 23.1, there are \(\begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 \prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(2^N - 2^i)\) such upper matrices of rank \(j\). - Out of the \(2^N\) choices for bit \(b\) of the \(N\) numbers, \(2^N - 2^j\) choices lie outside the \(j\)-dimensional row-span of the upper \(m\) bits (creating a new pivot at bit \(b\), so bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) is \(1\)), while \(2^j\) choices lie inside the row-span (giving bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) equal to \(1\) in \(2^{j-1}\) ways if \(j \ge 1\), or \(0\) ways if \(j = 0\)). - Summing over \(m = 0, \dots, M - 1\) reduces to \(T_j = \sum_{m=j}^{M-1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 x^m\) at \(x = 2^{-(N+1)}\), which satisfies a first-order \(O(1)\) recurrence in \(j\) via the \(q\)-Pascal identity!

Complete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. MSB-to-LSB Bit Projection: By linearity of summation, we compute the contribution of each bit \(b \in \{0, 1, \dots, M - 1\}\) to \(\sum_{A \in [0, 2^M)^N} f(A)\), where \(m = M - 1 - b \in \{0, 1, \dots, M - 1\}\) is the number of bit positions strictly above \(b\). Because \(2^b > \sum_{c=0}^{b-1} 2^c\), the greedy MSB-to-LSB elimination on bits \(M - 1, M - 2, \dots, b\) is completely unaffected by the lower \(b\) bits \(\{0, 1, \dots, b - 1\}\).

    • The lower \(b\) bits of \((a_1, \dots, a_N)\) can be chosen in \(2^{N b}\) ways independently.
    • Let \(U \in \mathbb{F}_2^{m \times N}\) be the binary matrix of the \(m\) bits strictly above \(b\), and let \(v_b \in \mathbb{F}_2^N\) be the row vector of bit \(b\) across \((a_1, \dots, a_N)\).

  2. Counting Valid Bit-\(b\) Rows Given Upper Rank \(j = \operatorname{rank}(U)\): Fix an upper matrix \(U \in \mathbb{F}_2^{m \times N}\) of rank \(j \in [0, \min(N, m)]\). When we perform MSB-to-LSB RREF on the \((m + 1) \times N\) matrix \(\binom{U}{v_b}\):

    • Case A (\(v_b \notin \operatorname{rowspan}(U)\), \(2^N - 2^j\) choices): Then row \(v_b\) creates a new pivot in column (bit) \(b\) of the RREF basis. Since every pivot bit is \(1\) in the XOR sum of the RREF basis, bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) is always \(1\).
    • Case B (\(v_b \in \operatorname{rowspan}(U)\), \(2^j\) choices): Then bit \(b\) is not a pivot; after eliminating \(U\) into its \(j\) non-zero RREF basis rows, the column of bit \(b\) in those \(j\) rows can be any vector in \(\mathbb{F}_2^j\) (each occurring for a unique \(v_b \in \operatorname{rowspan}(U)\)).
      • If \(j = 0\), the basis is empty and bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) is \(0\) (fails in \(1 = 2^0\) way).
      • If \(j \ge 1\), the XOR sum of the \(j\) entries in column \(b\) equals \(1\) for exactly half (\(2^{j-1}\)) of the \(2^j\) vectors, and equals \(0\) (fails) for the other \(2^{j-1}\) vectors.

    Combining Cases A and B, out of the \(2^N\) choices for \(v_b \in \mathbb{F}_2^N\), the number of choices where bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) is \(0\) (fails) is \(2^{\max(0, j - 1)}\), and the number of choices where bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) is \(1\) (succeeds) is \(2^N - 2^{\max(0, j - 1)}\).

  3. Verification on the Sample (\(N = 2, M = 2\)):

    • Bit \(b = 1\) (\(m = 0\) upper bits): Only \(j = 0\) (\(1\) upper matrix). Succeeds in \(2^2 - 1 = 3\) ways for bit \(1\), times \(2^{2 \times 1} = 4\) choices for bit \(0\), contributing \(3 \times 4 \times 2^1 = 24\).
    • Bit \(b = 0\) (\(m = 1\) upper bit):
      • \(j = 0\): \(1\) upper matrix, succeeds in \(2^2 - 1 = 3\) ways.
      • \(j = 1\): \(3\) upper matrices, each succeeds in \(2^2 - 2^0 = 3\) ways (\(3 \times 3 = 9\) ways). Contributing \((3 + 9) \times 2^0 = 12\).
    • Total \(= 24 + 12 = 36\), matching the brute-force trace.

  4. Summing Over \(m = 0, \dots, M - 1\) via a First-Order \(q\)-Pascal Recurrence: By Proposition 23.1, the number of \(m \times N\) binary matrices \(U\) of rank \(j\) is \[\#\{U \in \mathbb{F}_2^{m \times N} : \operatorname{rank}(U) = j\} = \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 \prod_{i=0}^{j-1} (2^N - 2^i),\] because choosing a \(j\)-dimensional column-space in \(\mathbb{F}_2^m\) has \(\begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2\) ways and mapping it surjectively onto \(N\) columns has \(\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(2^N - 2^i)\) ways. Summing the total bit value \(\sum_{b=0}^{M-1} 2^b \cdot 2^{N b} \cdot (\text{succeeding }(U, v_b))\) by subtracting the failing configurations from all \(2^{N M}\) arrays: \[\sum_{A \in [0, 2^M)^N} f(A) = 2^{N M} (2^M - 1) - \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} 2^{(N + 1)(M - 1 - m)} \left(1 + \sum_{j=1}^{\min(N, m)} 2^{j-1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 \prod_{i=0}^{j-1} (2^N - 2^i)\right).\] Let \(x = 2^{-(N + 1)} \bmod p\), and define for each \(0 \le j \le \min(N, M - 1)\): \[T_j = \sum_{m=j}^{M-1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 x^m.\] Then the subtracted sum is simply \(2^{(N + 1)(M - 1)} \left(T_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{\min(N, M-1)} 2^{j-1} T_j \prod_{i=0}^{j-1}(2^N - 2^i)\right)\). To compute \(T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{\min(N, M-1)}\) in \(O(M)\) total time, multiply the \(q\)-Pascal identity \(\begin{bmatrix} m + 1 \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 + 2^{j+1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2\) by \(x^m\), sum over \(m = j, \dots, M - 1\), and reindex \(m' = m + 1\) on the left-hand side (noting \(\begin{bmatrix} j \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 = 0\)): \[\frac{1}{x} \sum_{m'=j+1}^{M} \begin{bmatrix} m' \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 x^{m'} = \sum_{m=j}^{M-1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 x^m + 2^{j+1} \sum_{m=j+1}^{M-1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 x^m,\] \[\frac{1}{x}\left(T_{j+1} - \begin{bmatrix} M \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 x^M\right) = T_j + 2^{j+1} T_{j+1} \implies (1 - 2^{j+1} x) T_{j+1} = x T_j + \begin{bmatrix} M \\ j + 1 \end{bmatrix}_2 x^M.\] Since \(N \ge 1\), we have \(2^{j+1} x = 2^{j - N} \not\equiv 1 \pmod{998244353}\) for all \(0 \le j < N\), so \(1 - 2^{j+1} x\) is always invertible modulo \(998244353\). Starting from \(T_0 = \frac{1 - x^M}{1 - x}\), each \(T_{j+1}\) is obtained in \(O(1)\) time.

Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Preconditions: \(N, M \le 2.5\times 10^5\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Postconditions (Output): \(\sum_{A \in [0, 2^M)^N} f(A) \pmod{998244353}\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N + M)\) time and \(O(N + M)\) space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: Greedy MSB-to-LSB elimination makes bit \(b\) of \(f(A)\) depend only on bits \(M - 1 \dots b\); \(T_j = \sum_{m=j}^{M-1} \begin{bmatrix} m \\ j \end{bmatrix}_2 x^m\) satisfies \((1 - 2^{j+1} x) T_{j+1} = x T_j + \begin{bmatrix} M \\ j+1 \end{bmatrix}_2 x^M\).
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: When \(j = 0\) (all \(m\) upper bits are \(0\)), the \(0\)-dimensional row-span has no free RREF rows above bit \(b\), so bit \(b\) fails in \(2^0 = 1\) way rather than \(2^{j-1} = 2^{-1}\) ways; \(j = 0\) must be separated from \(j \ge 1\).

Advanced 2 (#132) — Group Division

  • Source: Codeforces Goodbye 2023 Problem F / 1916F
  • Prerequisite Tags: Biconnected Graphs, Block-Cut Tree / Ear Decomposition, Constructive Connected Partition

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given a biconnected (2-vertex-connected, i.e., connected with no articulation points / cut vertices) undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) with \(|V| = n_1 + n_2\) vertices (\(1 \le n_1, n_2\), \(n = n_1 + n_2 \le 2000\), \(|E| = m \le 4000\)). Partition the vertex set \(V\) into two disjoint sets \(V_1 \sqcup V_2\) of sizes \(|V_1| = n_1\) and \(|V_2| = n_2\) such that both induced subgraphs \(G[V_1]\) and \(G[V_2]\) are connected.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n_1 = 2, n_2 = 2\) on \(C_4\): \(1 - 2 - 3 - 4 - 1\))

  • Start with \(V_1 = \{1\}\) (\(|V_1| = 1 < n_1\)) and \(V_2 = \{2, 3, 4\}\) (\(G[V_2]\) is the path \(2 - 3 - 4\), which is connected).
  • Vertex \(2 \in N(V_1) \cap V_2\) is NOT a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\) (removing \(2\) leaves \(3 - 4\), which is connected).
  • Move \(2\) from \(V_2\) to \(V_1\): now \(V_1 = \{1, 2\}\) (\(G[V_1]\) is \(1 - 2\), connected) and \(V_2 = \{3, 4\}\) (\(G[V_2]\) is \(3 - 4\), connected).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Why is this always possible for any biconnected graph \(G\)? Think incrementally: start with \(V_1 = \{v_0\}\) (size \(1\)) and \(V_2 = V \setminus \{v_0\}\) (size \(n - 1\)). - Initially, \(G[V_1]\) is a single vertex (hence connected), and because \(G\) is biconnected, \(v_0\) is not a cut vertex of \(G\), so \(G[V_2] = G \setminus \{v_0\}\) is also connected! - If at each step (\(|V_1| = 1, 2, \dots, n_1 - 1\)) we can find a vertex \(u \in V_2\) that is adjacent to at least one vertex in \(V_1\) AND is not a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\), then moving \(u\) from \(V_2\) to \(V_1\) keeps both \(G[V_1]\) and \(G[V_2]\) connected!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Why must there ALWAYS exist a vertex \(u \in N(V_1) \cap V_2\) that is not a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\)? Consider the Block-Cut Tree of the connected graph \(G[V_2]\): 1. If \(G[V_2]\) is itself biconnected (has only \(1\) block and \(0\) cut vertices), then every vertex in \(N(V_1) \cap V_2\) (which is non-empty since \(G\) is connected) is a non-cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\)! 2. If \(G[V_2]\) has at least one cut vertex, its Block-Cut Tree has at least two leaf blocks \(B \subseteq V_2\). Each leaf block \(B\) contains at most one cut vertex \(c\) of \(G[V_2]\), and at least one non-cut vertex \(x \in B \setminus \{c\}\). 3. Could a leaf block \(B\) of \(G[V_2]\) have no edges from \(B \setminus \{c\}\) to \(V_1\)? If \(B \setminus \{c\}\) had no edges to \(V_1\), then in the entire graph \(G\), removing the single vertex \(c\) would disconnect \(B \setminus \{c\}\) from \((V_2 \setminus B) \cup V_1\)! That would mean \(c\) is a cut vertex of the original graph \(G\) — contradicting that \(G\) is biconnected!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

From Hint 2, in every leaf block \(B\) of \(G[V_2]\), the non-cut vertices \(B \setminus \{c\}\) of \(B\) MUST have at least one neighbor in \(V_1\)! Even simpler: we don’t even need to construct the full Block-Cut Tree! Just run a single Tarjan DFS on \(G[V_2]\) to identify the cut vertices of \(G[V_2]\) in \(O(n + m)\) time: - By Hint 2, at least one vertex \(u \in V_2\) that is adjacent to \(V_1\) is guaranteed NOT to be a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\)! - Find any such \(u \in N(V_1) \cap V_2\) that is not a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\), move \(u\) to \(V_1\), and repeat \(n_1 - 1\) times! Total time: \(n_1 \times O(n + m) = O(n(n + m)) \approx 8 \times 10^6\) operations!

Complete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Invariant & Exchange Lemma: We maintain a partition \(V = V_1 \sqcup V_2\) such that \(G[V_1]\) and \(G[V_2]\) are both connected.
    • Base case (\(|V_1| = 1\)): Pick \(V_1 = \{1\}\) and \(V_2 = V \setminus \{1\}\). Since \(G\) is 2-vertex-connected, \(G[V_2]\) is connected.
    • Inductive step (\(1 \le |V_1| < n_1\)): We claim there exists \(u \in V_2\) such that:
      1. \(u\) has at least one neighbor in \(V_1\), and
      2. \(u\) is not an articulation point (cut vertex) of \(G[V_2]\).
    • Proof of Claim: If \(G[V_2]\) has no cut vertices, then since \(G\) is connected, \(N(V_1) \cap V_2 \ne \emptyset\) and any \(u \in N(V_1) \cap V_2\) works. Otherwise, \(G[V_2]\) has at least one cut vertex, so its Block-Cut Tree is a tree with at least two leaves, which are blocks (maximal biconnected components) \(B\) of \(G[V_2]\). Let \(B\) be any leaf block of \(G[V_2]\), and let \(c \in B\) be the unique cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\) belonging to \(B\). Since \(B\) has size \(\ge 2\), \(B \setminus \{c\} \ne \emptyset\), and no vertex in \(B \setminus \{c\}\) is a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\). In \(G[V_2]\), every path from \(B \setminus \{c\}\) to \(V_2 \setminus B\) passes through \(c\). If no vertex in \(B \setminus \{c\}\) had an edge to \(V_1\), then every path in \(G\) from \(B \setminus \{c\}\) to \(V \setminus B = (V_2 \setminus B) \cup V_1\) would pass through \(c\), making \(c\) a cut vertex of \(G\), a contradiction. Hence there exists \(u \in B \setminus \{c\}\) adjacent to \(V_1\), and \(u\) is not a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\).
  2. Tarjan Cut-Vertex Identification in \(G[V_2]\): In each of the \(n_1 - 1\) iterations:
    • Run Tarjan’s DFS on \(G[V_2]\) (tin[v], low[v]): the root \(r\) of the DFS tree is a cut vertex iff it has \(\ge 2\) DFS tree children; a non-root vertex \(v\) is a cut vertex iff it has a DFS tree child \(w\) with low[w] >= tin[v].
    • Scan all \(u \in V_2\): pick the first \(u\) that is not a cut vertex of \(G[V_2]\) and has a neighbor in \(V_1\), and move \(u\) from \(V_2\) to \(V_1\).

Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Preconditions: Biconnected (2-vertex-connected) undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) with \(n = n_1 + n_2 \le 2000\) and \(m \le 4000\).
  • Postconditions (Output): Vertex subset \(V_1\) of size \(n_1\) such that \(G[V_1]\) and \(G[V \setminus V_1]\) are both connected.
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(n_1 (n + m)) = O(n(n + m))\) time and \(O(n + m)\) space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: Leaf blocks of the Block-Cut Tree of \(G[V_2]\) cannot be isolated from \(V_1\) by their single cut vertex \(c\), guaranteeing a valid vertex transfer at every step.
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: In Tarjan’s articulation-point test on \(G[V_2]\), applying low[w] >= tin[v] to the DFS root \(r\) falsely marks \(r\) as a cut vertex even when \(r\) has only \(1\) DFS tree child; the DFS root \(r\) must be tested separately by checking dfs_children(r) >= 2.

Mastery 1 (#063) — Modulo Sum of Increasing Sequences

  • Source: AtCoder Regular Contest 145 Problem F (arc145_f)
  • Prerequisite Tags: Gaussian \(q\)-Binomials as Ferrers Box GFs, \(q\)-Lucas Theorem at Roots of Unity, Cyclotomic Coset Inversion, Ramanujan Sums

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Given integers \(N, M, P\) (\(1 \le N, M \le 10^6\), \(1 \le P \le 5000\)), consider all \(\binom{N+M}{N}\) integer sequences \(A = (A_1, A_2, \dots, A_N)\) satisfying \[0 \le A_1 \le A_2 \le \dots \le A_N \le M.\] For each remainder \(r = 0, 1, \dots, P - 1\), compute the number of such sequences satisfying \[\sum_{i=1}^N A_i \equiv r \pmod P,\] modulo \(998244353\), in \(O(N + M + P^2)\) time.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 2, M = 2, P = 3\))

There are \(\binom{2+2}{2} = 6\) weakly increasing sequences \(0 \le A_1 \le A_2 \le 2\): - \((0, 0)\): sum \(= 0 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\). - \((0, 1)\): sum \(= 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 3\). - \((0, 2)\): sum \(= 2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3\). - \((1, 1)\): sum \(= 2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3\). - \((1, 2)\): sum \(= 3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\). - \((2, 2)\): sum \(= 4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3\). Counts for \(r = 0, 1, 2\) modulo \(3\): \((2, 2, 2)\). Check via \(\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}_q = 1 + q + 2q^2 + q^3 + q^4 \equiv (1 + 1) + (1 + 1)q + 2q^2 = 2 + 2q + 2q^2 \pmod{q^3 - 1}\)!

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Look at the condition \(0 \le A_1 \le A_2 \le \dots \le A_N \le M\) weighted by \(q^{\sum_{i=1}^N A_i}\): If you draw \(N\) horizontal rows of lengths \(A_1 \le A_2 \le \dots \le A_N\), what geometric shape do they form inside an \(N \times M\) grid, and how does the total sum \(\sum_{i=1}^N A_i\) relate to the area trapped beneath the North-East lattice path from \((0,0)\) to \((M,N)\)? Once you identify the generating function \(F(q) = \sum_{A} q^{\sum A_i}\), how do you extract the sum of coefficients where the exponent is congruent to \(r \pmod P\)?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

How do we reduce \(\begin{bmatrix} N + M \\ N \end{bmatrix}_q\) modulo \(q^P - 1\) when \(N, M \le 10^6\) and \(P \le 5000\)? We cannot expand \(\begin{bmatrix} N + M \\ N \end{bmatrix}_q\) up to degree \(NM = 10^{12}\), nor can we divide \(\prod (1 - q^k) \bmod (q^P - 1)\) directly because \(1 - q^k\) is a zero divisor modulo \(q^P - 1\)! Instead, factor \(q^P - 1 = \prod_{d \mid P} \Phi_d(q)\) into cyclotomic polynomials (or evaluate \(F(q)\) at all \(P\)-th roots of unity \(\zeta_P^j\))! If \(\zeta_d\) is a primitive \(d\)-th root of unity (\(d \mid P\)), what is \(\begin{bmatrix} N + M \\ N \end{bmatrix}_{\zeta_d}\)? By the \(q\)-Lucas Theorem (Theorem 23.2 in Section 23.2), writing \(L = N + M = a_1 d + a_0\), \(N = b_1 d + b_0\), and \(M = c_1 d + c_0\) with \(0 \le a_0, b_0, c_0 < d\): \[\begin{bmatrix} N + M \\ N \end{bmatrix}_{\zeta_d} = \binom{a_1}{b_1} \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}_{\zeta_d}.\] Notice that if \(b_0 + c_0 \ge d\) (a carry occurs mod \(d\)), then \(a_0 = b_0 + c_0 - d < b_0\), so \(\begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}_{\zeta_d} = 0\)! Thus \(F(\zeta_d) \ne 0\) only when \(a_0 = b_0 + c_0 < d\).

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Since \(P \le 5000\) is arbitrary, \(P\) need not divide \(998244353 - 1\), so primitive \(d\)-th roots of unity do not generally exist in \(\mathbb{F}_{998244353}\). Instead, for each divisor \(d \mid P\) with \(a_0 = b_0 + c_0 < d\), we compute \[R_d(q) = \binom{a_1}{b_1} \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}_q \bmod \Phi_d(q)\] symbolically inside \(\mathbb{F}_p[q] / (q^d - 1)\): 1. Multiplying by \((1 - q^j)\) (\(1 \le j < d\)) takes \(O(d)\) time via a cyclic shift. 2. Dividing by \((1 - q^j)\) (\(1 \le j < d\)) modulo \(\Phi_d(q)\) also takes \(O(d)\) time: partition \(\{0, 1, \dots, d - 1\}\) into \(g = \gcd(j, d)\) cyclic cosets of size \(m = d / g \ge 2\); on each coset, \(x = q^j\) satisfies \(1 + x + \dots + x^{m-1} = 0\), so \((1 - x)^{-1} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{t=0}^{m-1} (m - 1 - t) x^t\), which is a single \(O(m)\) linear prefix-sum pass! 3. Finally, the trace \(\sum_{\gcd(k, d) = 1} R_d(\zeta_d^k) \zeta_d^{-k r}\) is extracted in \(O(d \log d)\) time using Ramanujan’s sum \(c_d(n) = \sum_{e \mid \gcd(d, n)} \mu(d / e) e\). Summing \(O(d^2)\) over all \(d \mid P\) takes \(\sum_{d \mid P} d^2 \le \zeta(2) P^2 = O(P^2)\) total time!

Complete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Ferrers Box Generating Function & Inverse DFT Setup: Each sequence \(0 \le A_1 \le \dots \le A_N \le M\) corresponds to a Ferrers diagram inside an \(N \times M\) box with area \(\sum_{i=1}^N A_i\), so \[F(q) = \sum_{r \ge 0} c_r q^r = \begin{bmatrix} N + M \\ N \end{bmatrix}_q,\] and we seek \(F(q) \bmod (q^P - 1) = \sum_{r=0}^{P-1} \text{ans}_r q^r\). Over \(\mathbb{C}\), let \(\omega = e^{2\pi i / P}\). By the Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform of length \(P\), \[\text{ans}_r = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{j=0}^{P-1} F(\omega^j) \omega^{-j r}.\] Grouping the index \(j \in \{0, 1, \dots, P - 1\}\) by the exact multiplicative order \(d = \frac{P}{\gcd(P, j)} \mid P\) of \(\zeta = \omega^j\): \[\text{ans}_r = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{d \mid P} \sum_{\substack{1 \le k \le d \\ \gcd(k, d) = 1}} F(\zeta_d^k) \zeta_d^{-k r}, \qquad \zeta_d = e^{2\pi i / d}.\]

  2. Applying the \(q\)-Lucas Theorem at Order \(d \mid P\): Fix a divisor \(d \mid P\) and write \(L = N + M\), \(N\), and \(M\) in base \(d\): \[L = a_1 d + a_0, \qquad N = b_1 d + b_0, \qquad M = c_1 d + c_0 \qquad (0 \le a_0, b_0, c_0 < d).\] By the \(q\)-Lucas Theorem (Theorem 23.2), for every primitive \(d\)-th root of unity \(\zeta = \zeta_d^k\): \[F(\zeta) = \begin{bmatrix} L \\ N \end{bmatrix}_\zeta = \binom{a_1}{b_1} \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}_\zeta.\]

    • If \(b_0 + c_0 \ge d\), then \(a_0 = b_0 + c_0 - d < b_0\), so \(\begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}_\zeta = 0\) and divisor \(d\) contributes \(0\).
    • If \(b_0 + c_0 < d\), then \(a_0 = b_0 + c_0 < d\) and \(a_1 = b_1 + c_1\). In this case, \[\begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ b_0 \end{bmatrix}_q = \prod_{i=1}^{b_0} \frac{1 - q^{c_0 + i}}{1 - q^i},\] where every exponent \(c_0 + i\) and \(i\) lies strictly between \(1\) and \(a_0 < d\).

  3. \(O(d)\) Coset Inversion of \((1 - q^j)\) Modulo \(\Phi_d(q)\): For \(1 \le j < d\), \((1 - q^j)\) is coprime to \(\Phi_d(q)\). To divide a polynomial \(A(q) = \sum_{u=0}^{d-1} \alpha_u q^u \in \mathbb{F}_p[q] / (q^d - 1)\) by \((1 - q^j)\) modulo \(\Phi_d(q)\) in \(O(d)\) time:

    • Partition \(\{0, 1, \dots, d - 1\}\) into \(g = \gcd(j, d)\) cyclic cosets of step \(j \bmod d\), each of length \(m = d / g \ge 2\): \[C_s = \{s,\; (s + j) \bmod d,\; \dots,\; (s + (m - 1)j) \bmod d\}, \qquad 0 \le s < g.\]
    • On each coset \(C_s\), setting \(x = q^j\), every primitive \(d\)-th root \(\zeta\) satisfies \(1 + x + x^2 + \dots + x^{m-1} = 0\) because \(\zeta^j\) is a primitive \(m\)-th root of unity (\(m \ge 2\)).
    • Observe the polynomial identity in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\): \[(1 - x) \sum_{t=0}^{m-1} (m - 1 - t) x^t = m - (1 + x + x^2 + \dots + x^{m-1}) \equiv m \pmod{1 + x + \dots + x^{m-1}}.\] Hence \((1 - x)^{-1} \equiv \frac{1}{m} \sum_{t=0}^{m-1} (m - 1 - t) x^t \pmod{1 + x + \dots + x^{m-1}}\).
    • Equivalently, if \((1 - x) \sum_{t=0}^{m-1} \beta_t x^t \equiv \sum_{t=0}^{m-1} \alpha_t x^t \pmod{1 + x + \dots + x^{m-1}}\), subtracting the coset mean \(\overline{\alpha} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{t=0}^{m-1} \alpha_t\) (so that the right-hand side is divisible by \(1 - x\) modulo \(1 - x^m\)) and comparing \([x^t]\) in \((1 - x)\sum_{u=0}^{m-1} \beta_u x^u \equiv \sum_{u=0}^{m-1} (\alpha_u - \overline{\alpha})x^u \pmod{1 - x^m}\) gives \[[x^t]\!\left((1 - x)\sum_{u=0}^{m-1} \beta_u x^u \bmod (1 - x^m)\right) = \beta_t - \beta_{(t-1)\bmod m} = \alpha_t - \overline{\alpha} \implies \beta_t = \beta_{t-1} + (\alpha_t - \overline{\alpha}) \quad (1 \le t < m)\] (or \(\beta_t - \beta_{t-1} = -(\alpha_t - \overline{\alpha})\) when matching \((x - 1)\sum \beta_u x^u\)), which determines \((\beta_0, \dots, \beta_{m-1})\) up to an additive constant in a single \(O(m)\) linear scan. Across all \(g\) cosets, each division by \((1 - q^i)\) takes \(O(d)\) time, so computing \[R_d(q) = \sum_{m=0}^{d-1} \beta_m q^m \equiv \binom{a_1}{b_1} \prod_{i=1}^{b_0} \frac{1 - q^{c_0 + i}}{1 - q^i} \pmod{\Phi_d(q)}\] takes \(O(b_0 d) = O(d^2)\) time.

  4. Extracting the Trace via Ramanujan’s Sum: Once \(R_d(q) = \sum_{m=0}^{d-1} \beta_m q^m\) is computed, its trace over all \(\varphi(d)\) primitive \(d\)-th roots of unity is \[\sum_{\substack{1 \le k \le d \\ \gcd(k, d) = 1}} R_d(\zeta_d^k) \zeta_d^{-k r} = \sum_{m=0}^{d-1} \beta_m \sum_{\substack{1 \le k \le d \\ \gcd(k, d) = 1}} \zeta_d^{k(m - r)} = \sum_{m=0}^{d-1} \beta_m c_d(m - r),\] where \(c_d(n) = \sum_{e \mid \gcd(d, n)} \mu(d / e) e\) is Ramanujan’s sum (derived by Möbius inversion on the identity \(\sum_{k=0}^{e-1} \zeta_e^{k n} = e [e \mid n]\)). Exchanging the sums over \(m\) and \(e \mid d\) yields \[\sum_{m=0}^{d-1} \beta_m c_d(m - r) = \sum_{e \mid d} \mu(d / e) \, e \, S_{d, e}(r \bmod e), \qquad S_{d, e}(u) = \sum_{\substack{0 \le m < d \\ m \equiv u \pmod e}} \beta_m.\] Summing over all \(d \mid P\) and dividing by \(P\) gives the exact answer for every \(r \in \{0, 1, \dots, P - 1\}\): \[\text{ans}_r = \frac{1}{P} \sum_{d \mid P} \sum_{e \mid d} \mu(d / e) \, e \, S_{d, e}(r \bmod e) \pmod{998244353}.\]

  5. Verification on \(N = 1, M = 1, P = 3\) (\(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}_q = 1 + q\)):

    • \(d = 1\): \(a_1 = 2, b_1 = 1 \implies \binom{2}{1} = 2\), \(S_{1, 1}(0) = 2\).
    • \(d = 3\): \(a_1 = b_1 = 0 \implies \binom{0}{0} = 1\), \(R_3(q) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}_q = 1 + q \implies \beta = (1, 1, 0)\).
      • \(e = 1\): \(\mu(3) \cdot 1 \cdot (1 + 1 + 0) = -2\).
      • \(e = 3\): \(\mu(1) \cdot 3 \cdot \beta_r = (3, 3, 0)\).
      • Sum over \(e \mid 3\): \((-2 + 3, -2 + 3, -2 + 0) = (1, 1, -2)\).
    • Combining \(d = 1\) and \(d = 3\): \(\text{ans} = \frac{1}{3}((2, 2, 2) + (1, 1, -2)) = (1, 1, 0)\), matching \(1 + q + 0q^2\).

Mastery 2 (#111) — Chiori and Doll Picking (Hard Version)

  • Source: Codeforces Round 635 (Div. 1) Problem E2 / 1336E2
  • Prerequisite Tags: RREF Linear Basis over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), Dual Code \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\), Krawtchouk / MacWilliams Duality (Theorem 23.5)

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given \(n\) integers \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n \in [0, 2^m)\) (\(1 \le n \le 2\times 10^5\), \(0 \le m \le 53\)). For each \(c = 0, 1, \dots, m\), count the number of subsequences of \((a_1, \dots, a_n)\) whose bitwise XOR sum has binary popcount (\(\operatorname{popcount}\)) equal to \(c\). Output all \(m + 1\) counts modulo \(998244353\) in \(O(n m + 2^{\lfloor m/2 \rfloor} + m^3)\) time.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n = 3, m = 3, a = (3, 5, 6)\))

In binary, \(a_1 = \text{011}_2\), \(a_2 = \text{101}_2\), \(a_3 = \text{110}_2\). Notice that \(a_3 = a_1 \oplus a_2\), so the linear span \(\mathcal{C} = \operatorname{span}(a_1, a_2, a_3) \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^3\) has dimension \(r = 2\), and each of the \(2^2 = 4\) elements of \(\mathcal{C}\) is achieved by \(2^{n - r} = 2^{3 - 2} = 2\) subsequences: - \(\mathcal{C} = \{\text{000}_2, \text{011}_2, \text{101}_2, \text{110}_2\}\), with popcounts \(0, 2, 2, 2\). - Subsequence counts for \(c = 0, 1, 2, 3\): \(2 \times (1, 0, 3, 0) = (2, 0, 6, 0)\).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

First, insert \(a_1, \dots, a_n\) into an \(\mathbb{F}_2\) linear basis in Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF) in \(O(n m)\) time. Let \(r = \dim\mathcal{C} \le m\) be the rank of the basis, and let \(\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^m\) be the \(r\)-dimensional subspace spanned by the basis. Every vector \(v \in \mathcal{C}\) is the XOR sum of \(2^{n - r}\) distinct subsequences of \((a_1, \dots, a_n)\)! Thus, if \(A_c = \#\{v \in \mathcal{C} : \operatorname{popcount}(v) = c\}\) is the weight enumerator of \(\mathcal{C}\), the answer for each \(c\) is simply \(2^{n - r} A_c \pmod{998244353}\). - When \(r \le 26\): \(|\mathcal{C}| = 2^r \le 2^{26} \approx 6.7 \times 10^7\), so we can enumerate all \(v \in \mathcal{C}\) directly using Gray code / DFS in \(O(2^r)\) bit operations! - What when \(r > 26\) (up to \(r = 53\))?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

When \(r > 26\), the orthogonal complement (dual code) \(\mathcal{C}^\perp = \{u \in \mathbb{F}_2^m : \langle u, v \rangle \equiv 0 \pmod 2 \;\forall v \in \mathcal{C}\}\) has dimension \[\dim \mathcal{C}^\perp = m - r \le 53 - 27 = 26.\] Recall Section 23.4: 1. Permute the \(m\) bit columns so the \(r\) pivot columns come first. Then the RREF basis of \(\mathcal{C}\) is \(G = [I_r \mid P]\), where \(P\) is an \(r \times (m - r)\) binary matrix. 2. The dual subspace \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\) has basis \(H = [P^T \mid I_{m - r}]\), which consists of \(m - r\) rows! 3. Enumerate all \(2^{m - r} \le 2^{26}\) vectors \(u \in \mathcal{C}^\perp\) in \(O(2^{m - r})\) time to obtain the dual weight enumerator \(B_j = \#\{u \in \mathcal{C}^\perp : \operatorname{popcount}(u) = j\}\) for \(0 \le j \le m\).

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Apply Theorem 23.5 (Krawtchouk / MacWilliams Duality) from Section 23.4: \[A_c = \frac{1}{2^{m - r}} \sum_{j=0}^m B_j K_c(j; m),\] where \(K_c(j; m) = [z^c] (1 - z)^j (1 + z)^{m - j} = \sum_{i=0}^c (-1)^i \binom{j}{i} \binom{m - j}{c - i}\) is the binary Krawtchouk polynomial! Precompute the \((m + 1) \times (m + 1)\) table \(K_c(j; m)\) in \(O(m^3)\) time, compute \(A_c\) from \(B_j\) in \(O(m^2)\) time, and multiply by \(2^{n - r} \pmod{998244353}\)!

Complete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. RREF Basis Reduction: Insert \(a_1, \dots, a_n\) into an \(\mathbb{F}_2\) basis of bit-width \(m\), and eliminate upwards so that every pivot bit appears in only one basis vector (RREF). Let \(r\) be the number of basis vectors. Every element \(v \in \mathcal{C}\) is formed by \(2^{n - r}\) subsequences.

  2. Branch 1 (\(r \le \lfloor m / 2 \rfloor = 26\)): Primal Enumeration: Enumerate all \(2^r\) XOR combinations of the \(r\) basis vectors via a recursive DFS dfs(idx, cur_xor) (or Gray code loop), incrementing A[__builtin_popcountll(cur_xor)]. Output ans[c] = A[c] * 2^(n - r) mod 998244353.

  3. Branch 2 (\(r > 26\), so \(m - r \le 27\)): Dual Code Construction & MacWilliams Transform:

    • Column Permutation: Re-index the \(m\) bit positions so that the \(r\) pivot bits occupy positions \(0, 1, \dots, r - 1\) and the \(m - r\) non-pivot (free) bits occupy positions \(r, r + 1, \dots, m - 1\). Since re-indexing bits is a permutation of \(\{0, \dots, m - 1\}\), it preserves \(\operatorname{popcount}(v)\) for every vector \(v \in \mathbb{F}_2^m\).
    • Dual Generator Matrix \(H = [P^T \mid I_{m - r}]\): In the permuted coordinates, the \(i\)-th basis row (\(0 \le i < r\)) is \(e_i \oplus p_i\), where \(p_i\) is a mask on bits \(\{r, \dots, m - 1\}\). For each free bit \(f \in \{0, \dots, m - r - 1\}\), construct the dual basis vector \(h_f \in \mathbb{F}_2^m\) whose \(f\)-th free bit (position \(r + f\)) is \(1\), and whose \(i\)-th pivot bit (\(0 \le i < r\)) is equal to the \(f\)-th bit of \(p_i\). Then for every \(i \in [0, r)\) and \(f \in [0, m - r)\), the inner product over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) is \[\langle e_i \oplus p_i,\; h_f \rangle = (p_i)_f \oplus (p_i)_f = 0.\] Since \(h_0, \dots, h_{m - r - 1}\) are linearly independent (having \(I_{m - r}\) on the free coordinates), they form a basis of \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\).
    • Meet-in-the-Middle / Gray Code Enumeration of \(\mathcal{C}^\perp\): Enumerate all \(2^{m - r}\) XOR combinations of \(h_0, \dots, h_{m - r - 1}\) in \(O(2^{m - r})\) operations (using __builtin_popcountll) to accumulate the dual weight distribution \(B_0, B_1, \dots, B_m\). (Note: when \(m - r = 27\), split \(h_0\) out or unroll the last bit so the DFS has only \(2^{26}\) leaves.)
    • Krawtchouk Transform (Theorem 23.5): By Theorem 23.5, \[A_c = 2^{-(m - r)} \sum_{j=0}^m B_j \sum_{i=0}^c (-1)^i \binom{j}{i} \binom{m - j}{c - i} \pmod{998244353},\] and finally \(\text{ans}_c = 2^{n - r} A_c = 2^{n - m} \sum_{j=0}^m B_j K_c(j; m) \pmod{998244353}\).

Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Preconditions: \(n \le 2\times 10^5\), \(m \le 53\), and \(a_1, \dots, a_n \in [0, 2^m)\); modulus \(p = 998244353\) is odd (\(2^{-1} \equiv 499122177 \pmod p\)).
