09 — Formal Power Series

Infinitely many coefficients, finitely much work

Suppose a counting problem gives you the equation

\[B(x) = 1 + x\,B(x)^2\]

and asks for the coefficient of \(x^{n}\). There is no closed form to plug in, no DP transition to write down, and \(B\) has infinitely many coefficients. Yet the answer is completely determined, and computable in \(O(n\log n)\).

The reason is that coefficients of low degree never depend on coefficients of high degree. If you only want the first \(n\) coefficients, everything beyond \(x^{n}\) is irrelevant and can be discarded at every step. That single observation turns “solve an equation between infinite sequences” into a finite computation — and, better, it means a partially correct answer can be improved: if a candidate is right in the first \(m\) coefficients, one multiplication can make it right in the first \(2m\). That is Newton iteration, and it is the engine of this whole chapter.

A formal power series \(A(x) = \sum_{i\ge 0}a_ix^i\) is an element of \(R[[x]]\): algebra on an infinite coefficient sequence. It is not a function. We never substitute a real number for \(x\), never ask about convergence, and \(A \equiv B \pmod{x^n}\) means only that the first \(n\) coefficients agree.

Why this topic matters, prerequisites, and objectives

Recognition signals are equations between whole coefficient sequences: an inverse that undoes a convolution, an exponential that assembles labelled components, or an implicit equation like the one above whose low coefficients determine the higher ones.

Read polynomial operations and units in rings; the counting interpretations are developed in generating functions.

You should finish able to prove that precision doubles, state existence and uniqueness conditions, track leading zeros through an operation, and decide between a sparse recurrence and a dense operation.

Notation. \(R\) is commutative with identity, and \(R[[x]]\) is all sequences \((a_0,a_1,\ldots)\) under coefficientwise addition and convolution — each product coefficient is a finite sum, which is why no analysis is needed. The valuation \(v(A)\) is the index of the first nonzero coefficient, with \(v(0) = \infty\). Over a field \(v(AB) = v(A) + v(B)\); over a ring with zero divisors only \(v(AB) \ge v(A) + v(B)\) is automatic, which is still enough for the error-squaring arguments below.

The precision contract. \(A \equiv B \pmod {x^n}\) means \(A - B = x^nC\). Multiplying by anything preserves that agreement. Differentiation loses one coefficient of precision; integration with zero constant gains one, when its divisions are legal. Track this like you would track array bounds.

Finally, a series is a unit exactly when \(a_0\) is: solve \(b_0 = a_0^{-1}\) in \(AB = 1\) and each later \(b_j\) follows from earlier ones.

Let \(M(n)\) denote polynomial multiplication cost, \(O(n\log n)\) with NTT. Every operation below truncates immediately to the requested precision.

1. The differential toolkit and its contracts

\[A' = \sum_{i\ge1}ia_ix^{i-1}, \qquad \int A = \sum_{i\ge0}\frac{a_i}{i+1}x^{i+1}.\]

These are defined coefficientwise — no limits involved. For \(A = 1+2x+3x^2\), \(A' = 2 + 6x\). Note what integration needs: to produce coefficients below degree \(n\) over \(\mathbb F_p\) it divides by \(1, \ldots, n-1\), so it requires \(n \le p\). Over a general ring, read every “nonzero” below as “invertible”.

\(\exp(A)\) \(a_0 = 0\); integration denominators units \(\log(\exp A) = A\)

2. Newton inversion: squaring the error

Suppose \(B\) is a partial inverse, \(AB \equiv 1 \pmod{x^m}\). The error \(E = 1 - AB\) has valuation at least \(m\). Instead of solving for the next coefficients one by one, fix all \(m\) of them at once:

\[B_{\text{new}} = B(2 - AB) \pmod{x^{2m}}.\]

Why it doubles: \(AB_{\text{new}} = AB(2-AB) = (1-E)(1+E) = 1 - E^2\), and \(v(E^2) \ge 2m\). The error squares. Starting from \(B = a_0^{-1}\) at precision \(1\) and doubling, the total cost telescopes to \(O(M(n))\) — the last multiplication dominates everything before it.

NoteTwo doublings by hand

\(A = 1 - x - x^2\), start \(B = 1\).

Modulo \(x^2\): \(AB = 1 - x\), so \(2 - AB = 1 + x\) and \(B \leftarrow 1 + x\).

Modulo \(x^4\): \(AB = (1-x-x^2)(1+x) = 1 - 2x^2 - x^3\), so \(B \leftarrow (1+x)(1 + 2x^2 + x^3) = 1 + x + 2x^2 + 3x^3\).

Those are Fibonacci numbers, as they must be: \(1/(1-x-x^2)\) is the Fibonacci generating function.

The invariant is “\(AB \equiv 1\) at the current precision” at every step; nothing is ever guessed. On the final step use \(\min(2m, n)\) so that a non-power-of-two request has exact boundaries. Work on a truncated copy, or you will clobber a caller’s longer series.

3. Logarithm and exponential

There is no analytic content here; \(\log\) and \(\exp\) are defined by the identities that make them useful, and they exist because of the precision contract.

For a series with \(a_0 = 1\), define the logarithm as the unique series with constant term zero whose derivative is \(A'/A\):

\[\log A = \int A'A^{-1}\,dx,\]

computable in \(O(M(n))\). Two conditions are hiding here. The integral discards the scalar \(A[0]\), so this is a normalized logarithm and \(\exp(\log A) = A\) only when \(A[0] = 1\) (otherwise you get \(A/A[0]\)). And integration divides by \(1,\ldots,n-1\), which must be units.

For the exponential the defining identity is \(\log B = A\). If \(B\) is already correct below degree \(m\), then \(E = A - \log B\) has valuation at least \(m\), so

\[B_{\text{new}} = B\,(1 + A - \log B) \pmod{x^{2m}}.\]

Why this preserves what is known and fixes what is not: the bracket is \(1 \bmod x^m\), so known coefficients are untouched; and at precision \(2m\) every \(E^j\) with \(j \ge 2\) vanishes, so \(\log(1+E) \equiv E\) and

\[\log\bigl(B(1+E)\bigr) \equiv \log B + E = A \pmod{x^{2m}} .\]

(The product rule for \(\log\) follows by differentiating both sides and matching zero constant terms, again needing the integers through the working degree to be units.) Standard implementations are \(O(M(n))\), though a careless one that re-inverts from scratch each round adds a factor.

NoteWhere the characteristic bites

Over the rationals, \(\exp(x + x^2) = 1 + x + \tfrac32x^2 + \tfrac76x^3 + \cdots\), read off from \(B' = (1+2x)B\): \(b_1 = 1\), \(2b_2 = b_1 + 2b_0 = 3\), \(3b_3 = b_2 + 2b_1 = \tfrac72\).

Over \(\mathbb F_5\) the same prefix is \((1, 1, 4, 2)\). But at degree five the recurrence needs to divide by \(5 \equiv 0\): the operation simply does not exist at that precision. This is not an implementation detail — it is why problems with \(\exp\) come with \(n < p\).

4. Powers and square roots

When the \(\log/\exp\) contracts hold and \(A(0) = 1\),

\[A^k = \exp(k\log A),\]

because \(\log\) turns multiplication into addition. (\(k = 0\) is the separate identity case.)

For a general nonnegative \(k\) and a series with first nonzero term \(a_tx^t\) whose coefficient \(a_t\) is a unit (over a field, nonzero suffices; over a general ring it does not):

\[A^k = x^{tk}\,a_t^{\,k}\,\exp\!\left(k \log \frac{A}{a_tx^t}\right).\]

Two practical points. If \(tk \ge n\) the truncated answer is zero — check this before anything else, since \(k\) may be astronomically large. And the shift \(tk\) must be computed from the true \(k\), while only the scalar \(k\) multiplying \(\log A\) may be reduced modulo the characteristic. Negative powers need a unit series and are a different contract.

Square roots. Over a field of odd characteristic, solving \(B^2 - A = 0\) by Newton gives the familiar

\[B_{\text{new}} = \tfrac12\left(B + A/B\right),\]

started from a scalar square root of \(a_0\) — supplied by Tonelli–Shanks over an odd prime field. A unit series has a root exactly when \(a_0\) is a quadratic residue, and then exactly two, differing by sign. For a non-unit \(A\), strip an even valuation \(2t\) first and take the root of the remaining unit series; an odd finite valuation makes a root impossible.

Worked failure: how many roots does a truncated series have?

Over \(\mathbb F_5\), take \(A = x^2 \pmod{x^5}\) and look for \(B = b_1x + b_2x^2 + b_3x^3 + b_4x^4\). Comparing degrees two, three, and four:

\[b_1^2 = 1, \qquad 2b_1b_2 = 0, \qquad b_2^2 + 2b_1b_3 = 0 .\]

So \(b_1 = \pm1\) and \(b_2 = b_3 = 0\) — but \(b_4\) is completely free, because \(b_4x^4\) only affects \(B^2\) from degree five onward. There are ten roots, \(B = \pm x + cx^4\), even though the exact equation \(B^2 = x^2\) in \(R[[x]]\) has only two solutions.

The lesson generalizes: whenever \(v(A) = 2t > 0\), the top \(t\) coefficients of \(B\) are invisible modulo \(x^n\). A problem must either specify a canonical root or accept any valid one; for the zero series, take the all-zero root unless told otherwise.

5. Composition

Computing \(A(B(x))\) naively via the powers \(1, B, B^2, \ldots\) costs \(O(nM(n))\). Brent–Kung’s baby-step/giant-step decomposition takes \(k \approx \sqrt n\) and writes

\[A(B) = \sum_q B^{qk}\left(\sum_{r<k}a_{qk+r}B^r\right),\]

precomputing the baby powers \(B^r\), forming each inner block, then combining by Horner in \(B^k\).

Be honest about the cost: building every dense block as a separate linear combination is still \(O(n^2)\). The real algorithm batches those combinations into rectangular matrix multiplications, giving the classical \(O((n\log n)^{3/2})\) field-operation bound. The block decomposition is the idea, not a proof of a fast implementation — the practical cost depends on your polynomial and matrix routines.

Why \(B[0] = 0\) is required: it makes \(v(B^r) \ge r\), so a coefficient below degree \(n\) can depend only on \(a_0, \ldots, a_{n-1}\). With a nonzero constant term, arbitrarily high powers of \(B\) feed back into the constant coefficient, and no finite prefix of \(A\) determines the answer. (A finite polynomial \(A\) is fine either way — the restriction is about unknown infinite outer series.)

6. Compositional inverse (reversion)

If \(A(0) = 0\) and \(A'(0)\) is a unit, there is exactly one series \(B\) starting with \(x/A'(0)\) such that \(A(B(x)) = x\). Newton’s method on \(A(B) - x = 0\) gives the familiar tangent step

\[B_{\text{new}} = B - \frac{A(B) - x}{A'(B)} \pmod{x^{2m}},\]

whose denominator stays a unit because \(A'(B)\) has constant term \(A'(0)\). Cost is dominated by composition, so do not bill reversion at multiplication cost.

Small check: for \(A = x + x^2\), writing \(B = x + b_2x^2 + b_3x^3 + \cdots\) and imposing \(B + B^2 = x\) gives \(b_2 = -1\), then \(b_3 + 2b_2 = 0\) so \(b_3 = 2\). Hence \(B = x - x^2 + 2x^3 - \cdots\).

When you need only one coefficient — or a structured family of them — Lagrange inversion (Chapter 10) is far cheaper than a full reversion. For \(1 \le k \le n\),

\[[x^n]B(x)^k = \frac kn\,[t^{\,n-k}]\left(\frac{t}{A(t)}\right)^n .\]

The division by \(n\) means the coefficient field must make \(n\) invertible; in small characteristic use a division-free variant.

7. Sparse series: don’t pay for zeros

If the defining series has only \(K\) nonzero coefficients, a dense NTT is mostly multiplying zeros. Differentiating the defining identity gives a coefficient recurrence costing \(O(nK)\) instead.

For \(B = \exp(A)\), from \(B' = A'B\):

\[n\,b_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n}i\,a_i\,b_{n-i},\]

which touches only the nonzero \(a_i\). For \(B = A^{-1}\), after \(b_0 = a_0^{-1}\),

\[b_n = -a_0^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_{n-i} .\]

The exponential version divides by \(n\), so it is valid only while \(1, \ldots, n\) are units; the inverse version has no such restriction. Choose sparse versus dense by estimated work — \(nK\) against \(n\log n\) — not by degree alone.

8. Debugging invariants

Take \(A = 1 - x\). Then \(\operatorname{inv}(A) = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots\), \(\log A = -x - x^2/2 - x^3/3 - \cdots\), and \(\operatorname{pow}(A,k)\) reproduces \(\sum_i(-1)^i\binom kix^i\). This one example catches sign errors, integration mistakes, and truncation bugs.

More generally, every operation here is defined by an identity, so you can test it without knowing the implementation. On random small series check:

  • \(A\cdot\operatorname{inv}(A) \equiv 1 \pmod {x^n}\);
  • \(\log(\exp A) = A\) when \(A[0] = 0\);
  • \(\exp(\log A) = A\) when \(A[0] = 1\);
  • \(\bigl(\sqrt A\bigr)^2 = A\);
  • \(\operatorname{compose}(A, \operatorname{revert}(A)) = x\).

9. Contest checklist and reference

Before calling an operation, fix: the output indices \(0,\ldots,n-1\), the coefficient ring, the constant-term normalization, the valuation, and which integer divisions will occur. For powers, handle \(k = 0\) and the zero series before hunting for a leading coefficient. For logarithms, decide whether you mean \(A\) or \(A/A(0)\). For composition, distinguish a finite outer polynomial from an unknown infinite outer series — only the latter needs \(B[0] = 0\).

Operation to precision \(n\) Typical cost Invariant or restriction
Inverse \(O(M(n))\) \(AB = 1\); \(a_0\) a unit
Log / exp \(O(M(n))\) integers \(1,\ldots,n-1\) units
Nonnegative power via log \(O(M(n))\) + scalar power preserve the integer shift \(kv(A)\)
Unit square root \(O(M(n))\) + scalar root root of \(a_0\) must exist; \(2\) a unit
Sparse inverse / exp \(O(nK)\) \(K\) nonzero defining coefficients
Composition / reversion depends on the composition routine dense block assembly is a real cost

The classical Brent–Kung result gives \(O((n\log n)^{3/2})\) operations for general composition and reversion; the baby-step decomposition alone does not establish that bound.
