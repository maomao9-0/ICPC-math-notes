Checkpoint II — Bipartite Matching, König–Hall–Dilworth Duality, and Decomposition

Unassisted Diagnostic Triage, Parity & Poset Traps (FM-07–FM-08), and 4-Step Compilation Across Chapters 05–08 (#201–#208)

How to Use This Blind Recognition Checkpoint

In Chapters 05 through 08, you studied the four structural pillars of Part II:

  1. Chapter 05 — Bipartite Matching & König’s Duality: Converting 2D assignment, prefix-sequential matching, and Minimum Vertex Cover (\(\tau(G) = \nu(G)\)) / Maximum Independent Set (\(\alpha(G) = |V| - \nu(G)\)) problems into unit bipartite networks.
  2. Chapter 06 — Grid & Parity Matching, Dulmage–Mendelsohn Decomposition & Games: Discovering hidden bipartiteness via coordinate parity invariants, decomposing vertices into essential (\(E\)), optional (\(O\)), and forbidden (\(N\)) classes, and solving two-player bipartite walk games (博弈).
  3. Chapter 07 — Non-Graph Bipartite Reductions, 2D Line/Factor Incidence & \(b\)-Matching: Compiling non-graph arrays, prime factorizations, maximal H/V grid strips (Royal Guards), and degree-constrained 2-factors (Fox and Dinner) into bipartite incidence graphs.
  4. Chapter 08 — Hall’s Marriage Theorem, Dilworth’s Poset Duality & Submodular Deficiency: Exploiting Hall’s deficiency formula \(\nu(G) = |L| - \max_{S \subseteq L}(|S| - |N(S)|)\) to eliminate explicit flow networks on structured intervals, and compiling DAG path covers and poset antichains via Dilworth’s theorem.

When you solve problems inside a specific chapter, the chapter title gives away the paradigm. In a live contest, nobody tells you whether a problem is a König vertex cover, a Dulmage–Mendelsohn game, a poset antichain, a Hall deficiency DP, or a non-flow 0-1 BFS decoy.

The Three-Pass Blind Protocol

  • Pass 1 — Diagnostic Triage (15 minutes total for Section 1, 5 minutes per problem in Section 2): Read the raw, untagged problem statement and constraints. Without opening any callouts, write down:
    1. Target Paradigm: Is this Maximum Matching (Ch 05), König MVC/MIS (Ch 05–06), Dulmage–Mendelsohn Game (Ch 06), Bipartite \(b\)-Matching / Incidence (Ch 07), Dilworth Poset Antichain / DAG Path Cover (Ch 08), Hall’s Deficiency (Ch 08), or a Non-Flow Decoy?
    2. Left vs. Right Bipartition: What are the two disjoint vertex sets \(L\) and \(R\), and what invariant proves there are no odd cycles?
    3. Primal vs. Dual Objective: Are you maximizing packed edges \(\nu(G)\), minimizing covering lines \(\tau(G)\), maximizing mutually compatible elements \(\alpha(G) = |V| - \nu(G)\), or decomposing a poset into chains?
  • Pass 2 — Progressive Hints (Hint 1 \(\to\) Hint 2): If your diagnostic stalls after 5 minutes, open Hint 1 (which reveals the structural bipartition or duality lens without giving away the graph wiring). Only open Hint 2 if you need the explicit algebraic-to-graph translation.
  • Pass 3 — Full 4-Step Compilation Audit: Compare your scratchpad against the Full 4-Step Compilation & Solution callout. Pay special attention to Step 4 (Broken Gadget Counterexamples), which audits the two signature Part II failure modes: omitting transitive closure before applying Dilworth (FM-07) and using standard \((r+c) \bmod 2\) checkerboard coloring on parity-preserving jumps (FM-08).

1. Rapid-Fire Diagnostic Triage Drills

For each of the six miniature scenarios below, identify the exact Part II mechanism (Ch 05–Ch 08), the bipartition \((L, R)\), and any active failure mode (FM-07 or FM-08) in under 90 seconds before expanding the diagnostic key.

Drill 1.1 — Multi-Delegation Table Seating & Quota Assignment

Scenario (Luogu P3254 圆桌问题 / Luogu P2763 试题库问题): \(M \le 150\) delegations (delegation \(i\) has \(r_i\) members) must be seated across \(N \le 270\) dining tables (table \(j\) has capacity \(c_j\)) such that no two members of the same delegation sit at the same table. Determine if a valid seating exists and reconstruct the table assignment for every delegation.

TipDiagnostic Triage Key — Drill 1.1
  • Target Paradigm: Ch 07 (§7.3 Bipartite \(b\)-Matching / Gale–Ryser Multi-Allocation) & Ch 01 (§1.2).
  • Diagnostic Signal: Two disjoint entity classes (Delegations \(L\), Tables \(R\)) with vertex supply/demand caps \((r_i, c_j)\) and a pairwise uniqueness constraint (“at most one member of delegation \(i\) at table \(j\)”).
  • Algebraic & Graph Compilation: Let \(x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether delegation \(i\) sends a representative to table \(j\). We require \(\sum_{j=1}^N x_{i,j} = r_i\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^M x_{i,j} \le c_j\). Wire \(S \xrightarrow{r_i} \text{Del}_i\), \(\text{Del}_i \xrightarrow{1} \text{Tab}_j\), and \(\text{Tab}_j \xrightarrow{c_j} T\). A valid seating exists iff \(\text{MaxFlow}(S, T) = \sum_{i=1}^M r_i\).
  • Critical Failure Guard: Setting the cross-edge capacity \(\text{Del}_i \to \text{Tab}_j\) to \(\infty\) instead of \(1\) destroys the uniqueness constraint and allows all \(r_i\) members of delegation \(i\) to crowd onto a single table.

Drill 1.2 — Prime-Sum Circular Dining Tables

Scenario (Codeforces 510 E — Fox And Dinner): Given \(N \le 200\) foxes with integer ages \(a_i \ge 2\), seat all \(N\) foxes around circular tables of size at least \(3\) such that every pair of adjacent foxes around any table hasages summing to a prime number.

TipDiagnostic Triage Key — Drill 1.2
  • Target Paradigm: Ch 06 (§6.1 Parity Bipartition) + Ch 07 (§7.3 Bipartite \(2\)-Factor Cycle Decomposition).
  • Diagnostic Signal: Every vertex in a collection of disjoint simple cycles has degree exactly \(2\), and since \(a_i \ge 2\), any prime sum \(a_i + a_j\) must be an odd prime (\(a_i + a_j \ge 4 > 2\)).
  • Algebraic & Graph Compilation: An odd prime sum requires one odd age and one even age! Partition the foxes into \(L = \{i : a_i \text{ is odd}\}\) and \(R = \{j : a_j \text{ is even}\}\). If \(|L| \neq |R|\), output Impossible. Otherwise, wire \(S \xrightarrow{2} u\) for \(u \in L\), \(v \xrightarrow{2} T\) for \(v \in R\), and \(u \xrightarrow{1} v\) whenever \(a_u + a_v\) is prime.
  • Why Cycles Have Length \(\ge 4\) Automatically: Because each cross-edge \(u \to v\) has capacity \(1\), the two incident edges chosen for any fox \(u\) must go to two distinct neighbors \(v_1 \neq v_2\), eliminating \(2\)-cycles. Since the graph is bipartite, every simple cycle has even length \(\ge 4 > 3\). Check \(\text{MaxFlow}(S, T) = N\) and extract cycles via DFS on saturated cross-edges.

Drill 1.3 — Laser Attack Devices on a Chessboard (FM-08 Audit)

Scenario (Luogu P4304 [TJOI2013] 攻击装置 vs. (±2, ±2) Bishop Traps): On an \(N \times N\) grid (\(N \le 200\)) with obstacles, place the maximum number of mutually non-attacking devices. Compare two rules:

  • Rule A: Device attacks \((\pm 1, \pm 2)\) and \((\pm 2, \pm 1)\) (standard knight jumps).
  • Rule B: Device attacks \((\pm 1, \pm 3)\) and \((\pm 3, \pm 1)\) (camel/giraffe jumps).
TipDiagnostic Triage Key — Drill 1.3
  • Target Paradigm: Ch 06 (§6.2 Coordinate-Parity Bipartite MIS: \(\alpha(G) = |V_{\text{free}}| - \nu(G)\)) and FM-08 (Broken Parity Invariant).
  • Algebraic Parity Audit:
    • Under Rule A, \(\Delta r + \Delta c \in \{\pm 1, \pm 3\}\) is always odd, so \((r + c) \bmod 2\) flips on every attack edge. Coloring by \((r + c) \bmod 2\) yields a valid bipartition.
    • Under Rule B, \(\Delta r + \Delta c \in \{0, \pm 2, \pm 4\}\) is always even! If you color by \((r + c) \bmod 2\), every attack edge connects two cells of the same color (FM-08 trap!). However, \(\Delta r \in \{\pm 1, \pm 3\}\) is always odd, so row parity \(r \bmod 2\) (and likewise \(c \bmod 2\)) strictly alternates on every attack edge!
  • Lesson: Never hardcode \((r + c) \bmod 2\) by reflex. Always compute the generator increments \((\Delta r, \Delta c)\) and find a linear functional \(\phi(r, c)\) (or bit-shifted parity \(\lfloor r/2^k \rfloor \bmod 2\)) that evaluates to \(1 \pmod 2\) on all step vectors.

Drill 1.4 — Mutually Unrelated String Antigens (FM-07 Audit)

Scenario (Codeforces 590 E — Birthday): Given \(N \le 750\) distinct strings of total length \(\sum |s_i| \le 10^7\) over {a, b}, select the maximum number of strings such that no chosen string is a contiguous substring of another chosen string, and print the chosen indices.

TipDiagnostic Triage Key — Drill 1.4
  • Target Paradigm: Ch 08 (§8.2 Dilworth’s Poset Maximum Antichain & constructive Kőnig extraction via Aho–Corasick).
  • Diagnostic Signal: The substring relation \(s_u \subseteq_{\text{sub}} s_v\) is reflexive, antisymmetric (on distinct strings), and transitive — it defines a strict poset \((P, \prec)\). “No chosen string is a substring of another” asks for a Maximum Antichain.
  • Critical FM-07 Guard: In the Aho–Corasick automaton, a single fail-link jump from \(s_v\) only finds the longest proper suffix of prefixes of \(s_v\) that appears in the dictionary. If you only add direct Aho–Corasick transitions without computing the transitive closure of the reachability DAG, a 3-chain \(s_x \prec s_y \prec s_z\) where \(s_x \to s_z\) is omitted allows the non-transitive path cover to undercount the antichain (FM-07)! You must collapse fail-tree ancestors to find direct dictionary predecessors for each prefix of \(s_v\), then run Floyd–Warshall (\(N \le 750\)) before bipartite matching.

Drill 1.5 — 3D Cuboid Chemical Disinfection

Scenario (Luogu P3231 [HNOI2013] 消毒): You are given a 3D grid of dimensions \(A \times B \times C\) containing infected cells \((x, y, z)\), where \(A \cdot B \cdot C \le 5000\). Sterilizing a sub-box of dimensions \(p \times q \times r\) costs \(\min(p, q, r)\) units of chemical. Find the minimum total cost to sterilize all infected cells.

TipDiagnostic Triage Key — Drill 1.5
  • Target Paradigm: Ch 07 (§7.1 Dimensional Slice Reduction) + Ch 05 (§5.2 König 2D Line Cover).
  • Diagnostic Signal: A slice of thickness \(1\) along any axis (\(1 \times B \times C\), \(A \times 1 \times C\), or \(A \times B \times 1\)) costs \(\min(1, \dots) = 1\), and any box of cost \(k\) can be decomposed into \(k\) unit-thickness planes! Thus we are covering 3D points \((x, y, z)\) with axis-parallel planes at cost \(1\) per plane.
  • Why 3D Line Cover Is NP-Hard — And How Constraints Rescue It: Minimum Vertex Cover on a 3-uniform hypergraph is NP-hard, but \(A \cdot B \cdot C \le 5000 \implies \min(A, B, C) \le \lfloor 5000^{1/3} \rfloor = 17\)!
  • Compilation: Permute coordinates so \(A \le 17\). Enumerate the bitmask \(S \subseteq \{0, \dots, A-1\}\) of \(x\)-planes sterilised directly (\(2^{17}\) masks). All remaining infected cells \((x, y, z)\) with \(x \notin S\) project onto a 2D \((y, z)\) grid, whose minimum plane cover is \(\nu(G_{y,z})\) by König’s Theorem (Ch 05)!

Drill 1.6 — Two-Player Token Walk on a Grid with Obstacles

Scenario (Luogu P4055 [JSOI2009] 游戏): An \(N \times M\) grid (\(N, M \le 100\)) has passable cells . and obstacles #. Alice places a token on a starting cell \(v_0\); then Bob and Alice alternate moving the token to an adjacent unvisited passable cell. The first player unable to move loses. Find all starting cells \(v_0\) from which Bob (the first mover) has a winning strategy.

TipDiagnostic Triage Key — Drill 1.6
  • Target Paradigm: Ch 06 (§6.3 Dulmage–Mendelsohn Essential Vertex Theorem & Joyner–Edmonds Bipartite Walk Game).
  • Diagnostic Signal: Impartial normal-play path-extension game on a bipartite graph (\(2\)-colored by \((r+c) \bmod 2\)).
  • Theorem & Compilation: Bob (first mover from \(v_0\)) has a winning strategy if and only if \(v_0\) belongs to EVERY maximum matching of \(G\) (\(v_0 \in E_L \cup E_R\) in the Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition).
    1. Compute a maximum matching \(M^*\) on the \((r+c) \bmod 2\) bipartite graph.
    2. Any unmatched vertex \(u\) can never be entered by a matched edge, so flood-fill alternating paths (unmatched \(\xrightarrow{\text{non-}M^*}\) matched \(\xrightarrow{M^*}\) matched) from all unmatched vertices in \(L\) and \(R\).
    3. Every vertex not reached by this alternating BFS/DFS is essential (\(v_0 \in E\)) and is a winning start for Bob.

Problem #201 — Placing Rooks & Multi-Quota Incidence (Luogu P10937 / CH 6801 車的放置 & Luogu P1894 / P2763)

Problem Statement: You are given an \(N \times M\) chessboard (\(1 \le N, M \le 200\)) in which \(T\) specified cells \((r_k, c_k)\) are forbidden (muddy/damaged) and cannot hold a piece. Two rooks attack each other if they share the same row or the same column (forbidden cells do not block rook attacks).

  1. Part A (Luogu P10937): Find the maximum number of mutually non-attacking rooks that can be placed on the non-forbidden cells of the board.
  2. Part B (Multi-Quota Extension — Luogu P2763 试题库问题): Suppose instead that row \(r\) can hold at most \(1\) rook, column \(c\) requires exactly \(q_c \ge 1\) rooks, and you must reconstruct which rows are assigned to each column \(c\).
NoteHint 1 — Structural Recognition & Bipartition

Instead of viewing a cell \((r, c)\) as a vertex in a conflict graph of size \(N \times M = 40{,}000\), ask: what are the two orthogonal resources consumed when you place a rook at \((r, c)\)? Every rook consumes one row \(r \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) and one column \(c \in \{1, \dots, M\}\).

NoteHint 2 — Algebraic-to-Graph Compilation

Introduce a binary indicator variable \(x_{r,c} \in \{0, 1\}\) for each allowed cell \((r, c) \notin \mathcal{F}\). The non-attacking rules are linear degree bounds on the bipartite incidence graph between \(L = \{\text{Row}_1, \dots, \text{Row}_N\}\) and \(R = \{\text{Col}_1, \dots, \text{Col}_M\}\).

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — Problem #201

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

  • Surface Cues: Grid coordinates \((r, c)\) with \(N, M \le 200\), \(T\) forbidden cells, and global row/column mutual exclusion (“at most \(1\) rook per row and at most \(1\) rook per column”).
  • Why Naive DP / Conflict Graph Fails: Bitmask DP over columns takes \(O(N 2^M)\), which is impossible for \(M = 200\). Building a cell-conflict graph with \(V = NM \le 4 \times 10^4\) vertices and \(O(NM(N+M))\) edges loses the bipartite structure (a row of cells forms a clique!). The key shift is Rank-2 Coordinate-to-Edge Duality (Ch 05.1 / Ch 07.1): rows and columns are vertices; allowed cells are edges.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(\mathcal{A} = (\{1..N\} \times \{1..M\}) \setminus \mathcal{F}\) be the set of allowed cells, and let \(x_{r,c} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether a rook is placed at \((r, c) \in \mathcal{A}\).

\[\max \sum_{(r,c) \in \mathcal{A}} x_{r,c} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{c : (r,c) \in \mathcal{A}} x_{r,c} \le 1 \;\; (\forall r), \qquad \sum_{r : (r,c) \in \mathcal{A}} x_{r,c} \le q_c \;\; (\forall c),\]

where \(q_c = 1\) in Part A and \(q_c \ge 1\) in Part B.

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  • Vertices: Source \(S\), Sink \(T\), Left row nodes \(L = \{R_1, \dots, R_N\}\), Right column nodes \(R = \{C_1, \dots, C_M\}\).
  • Arcs:
    1. Row supply bound \(\sum_c x_{r,c} \le 1 \implies\) directed arc \(S \xrightarrow{1} R_r\) for each \(r \in \{1..N\}\).
    2. Allowed cell decision \(x_{r,c} \in \{0, 1\} \implies\) directed arc \(R_r \xrightarrow{1} C_c\) for each \((r, c) \in \mathcal{A}\).
    3. Column quota bound \(\sum_r x_{r,c} \le q_c \implies\) directed arc \(C_c \xrightarrow{q_c} T\) for each \(c \in \{1..M\}\).
  • Part A Output: \(\nu(G) = \text{MaxFlow}(S, T)\) in \(O(E \sqrt{V}) = O(NM \sqrt{N+M})\) via Hopcroft–Karp or Dinic.
  • Part B Output: Check \(\text{MaxFlow}(S, T) == \sum_{c=1}^M q_c\); if satisfied, for each column \(C_c\), collect all \(R_r\) whose arc \(R_r \to C_c\) carries flow \(1\) (residual capacity == 0).

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Broken Gadget Counterexample

  • Toy Trace (\(N = M = 3\), forbidden \(\mathcal{F} = \{(1,1), (1,2), (2,3), (3,3)\}\)):
    • Allowed edges: \(R_1 \to C_3\), \(R_2 \to \{C_1, C_2\}\), \(R_3 \to \{C_1, C_2\}\).
    • Augmenting paths saturate \(R_1 \to C_3\), \(R_2 \to C_1\), and \(R_3 \to C_2\), giving \(\nu(G) = 3\) rooks at \((1,3), (2,1), (3,2)\).
  • Broken Gadget Counterexample: Suppose a solver forgets to deduplicate input forbidden cells \((r_k, c_k)\) and instead subtracts \(1\) from a count matrix without clamping, adding duplicate parallel edges \(R_r \to C_c\) in Part B. Then a single cell \((r, c)\) could contribute \(2\) units of flow to column \(C_c\)’s quota \(q_c\), placing two rooks on the exact same cell! Always store a boolean grid forbidden[r][c] and emit at most one unit-capacity arc per \((r, c)\).

Problem #202 — Sequential Stage Clearance (Luogu P2319 [HNOI2006] 超级英雄)

Problem Statement: A game show consists of \(M\) sequential stages numbered \(1, 2, \dots, M\) (\(1 \le M \le 1000\)) and a pool of \(N\) distinct “Hint Keys” numbered \(0, 1, \dots, N-1\) (\(1 \le N \le 1000\)).

  • To clear stage \(i\), the contestant must use one of two specified Hint Keys \((u_i, v_i) \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}^2\) associated with stage \(i\).
  • Each Hint Key in \(\{0, \dots, N-1\}\) can be used at most once across the entire game.
  • The contestant plays stages strictly in order \(i = 1, 2, \dots, M\) and stops immediately at the first stage \(i\) that cannot be cleared (even if later stages \(j > i\) could still be cleared).

Compute the maximum number of consecutive stages \(K\) (\(0 \le K \le M\)) the contestant can clear starting from stage \(1\), and print the sequence of \(K\) Hint Keys used at stages \(1, 2, \dots, K\).

NoteHint 1 — Prefix Monotonicity vs. Offline Binary Search

Because the contestant must clear a contiguous prefix \(1, 2, \dots, K\) of stages, stages \(K+1, \dots, M\) must not steal keys from earlier stages. You could binary search \(K\) and run bipartite matching from scratch, but how does Kuhn’s augmenting-path algorithm behave when you insert left vertices \(i = 1, 2, \dots, M\) one by one?

NoteHint 2 — Incremental Kuhn Invariant & Reconstruction Trap

In Kuhn’s algorithm (find_augmenting_path(i)), augmenting from stage \(i\) can rearrange the key assignments among stages \(\{1, \dots, i-1\}\), but it never unmatches an already-matched stage \(j < i\)! Be careful at the end: if you store match_stage[key] = stage, how do you print the key chosen for each stage \(1 \dots K\) in order?

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — Problem #202

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

  • Surface Cues: Sequential stages \(i = 1, \dots, M\) (\(M \le 1000\)), each offering \(2\) candidate keys \((u_i, v_i) \in \{0..N-1\}^2\), each key usable at most once, stopping at the first unmatchable stage.
  • Why Greedy Fails: Choosing \(u_1\) at stage \(1\) might block stage \(2\) (which only accepts \((u_1, u_1)\)), whereas choosing \(v_1\) at stage \(1\) lets both stages pass. An augmenting path automatically “regrets” stage \(1\)’s choice from \(u_1\) to \(v_1\).

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

For a prefix length \(K \in \{0, \dots, M\}\), we seek an injective map \(\pi : \{1, \dots, K\} \to \{0, \dots, N-1\}\) such that \(\pi(i) \in \{u_i, v_i\}\) for every \(1 \le i \le K\). By Berge’s Augmenting Path Theorem (Ch 05.1), the set of matchable left vertices in a bipartite graph is a matroid (transversal matroid): if \(\{1, \dots, i-1\}\) is matchable, then \(\{1, \dots, i\}\) is matchable iff there exists an \(M\)-augmenting path starting at stage \(i\) in the subgraph induced by \(\{1, \dots, i\} \cup \{0, \dots, N-1\}\).

  • Bipartite Graph: Left vertices \(L = \{1, \dots, M\}\) (stages, added online), Right vertices \(R = \{0, \dots, N-1\}\) (Hint Keys). Each stage \(i \in L\) has at most \(2\) outgoing edges \(i \to u_i\) and \(i \to v_i\).

    3. Print \(K = i - 1\) followed by ans_key[1] through ans_key[K].

    • \(i = 2\): dfs(2) inspects Key \(0\) (held by Stage \(1\)), recurses dfs(1) which shifts Stage \(1 \to\) Key \(1\) (ans_key[1] = 1), and matches Stage \(2 \to\) Key \(0\) (ans_key[2] = 0).

Problem #206 — Making a Divisibility Poset Bipartite (Codeforces 1630 F & Luogu P1263 Royal Guards)

Problem Statement: You are given \(N\) distinct positive integers \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N\) (\(1 \le N \le 5 \times 10^4\), \(1 \le a_i \le 5 \times 10^4\)). Define an undirected graph \(G_0 = (V, E)\) on the \(N\) integers where an edge connects \(u\) and \(v\) (\(u \neq v\)) if and only if \(a_u \mid a_v\) or \(a_v \mid a_u\).

Find the minimum number of vertices to delete from \(G_0\) so that the induced subgraph on the remaining vertices is bipartite.

NoteHint 1 — When Is a Poset Comparability Graph Bipartite?

Divisibility \(a_u \mid a_v\) (\(a_u < a_v\)) is a strict partial order (poset)! If three remaining vertices form a 3-chain \(a_x \mid a_y \mid a_z\), then \((x, y)\), \((y, z)\), and \((x, z)\) are all edges in \(G_0\), forming a triangle \(K_3\) (not bipartite!). Conversely, if there is no 3-chain \(a_x \mid a_y \mid a_z\), can the comparability graph ever have an odd cycle?

NoteHint 2 — Role-Splitting Each Integer into Top (Source) vs. Bottom (Sink) (Ch 08.2)

A poset comparability graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no 3-chain \(x \prec y \prec z\) — which means every kept vertex \(u\) is either a minimal element (source, out-only) or a maximal element (sink, in-only) in the kept sub-poset, never both! Split each integer \(u\) into two candidate roles \((u_{\text{source}}, u_{\text{sink}})\) and formulate a Dilworth Maximum Antichain!

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — Problem #206

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

  • Surface Cues: Distinct integers \(a_i \le 5 \times 10^4\), edges defined by \(a_u \mid a_v\), and “delete minimum vertices to make the graph bipartite.”
  • Why General Odd-Cycle Transversal Fails: Deleting minimum vertices to make a general graph bipartite (Odd Cycle Transversal) is NP-hard! The rescue is that \((V, \mid)\) is a transitive DAG (poset): any directed 3-path \(x \to y \to z\) automatically has the transitive shortcut \(x \to z\), creating a \(K_3\). Conversely, if no vertex has both an incoming edge and an outgoing edge, the kept vertices partition into sources (only outgoing edges) and sinks (only incoming edges), which is manifestly bipartite!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Each kept vertex \(u\) must be assigned a role \(r_u \in \{\text{Top (source)}, \text{Bot (sink)}\}\):

  1. Vertex \(u\) can be assigned at most one role: \((u, \text{Top})\) and \((u, \text{Bot})\) conflict.
  2. If \(a_u \mid a_v\) (\(a_u < a_v\)), then we cannot keep both \(u\) and \(v\) unless \(u\) is a \(\text{Top}\) (source) and \(v\) is a \(\text{Bot}\) (sink). In particular:
    • \((u, \text{Bot})\) conflicts with \((v, \text{Top})\) and \((v, \text{Bot})\) (since \(u\) would have an outgoing edge \(u \to v\), violating \(u\)’s sink role);
    • \((v, \text{Top})\) conflicts with \((u, \text{Top})\) and \((u, \text{Bot})\) (since \(v\) would have an incoming edge \(u \to v\), violating \(v\)’s source role).

Define a new poset \(\mathcal{P}^*\) on \(2N\) elements \(\{(u, 0), (u, 1) : u \in V\}\) (where \((u, 0)\) means keeping \(u\) as a source and \((u, 1)\) means keeping \(u\) as a sink) with strict partial order \(\prec^*\):

\[(u, 0) \prec^* (u, 1) \quad (\forall u \in V), \qquad\text{and for all } a_u \mid a_v \;(a_u < a_v): \quad (u, 0) \prec^* (v, 0),\;\; (u, 1) \prec^* (v, 1),\;\; (u, 0) \prec^* (v, 1).\]

Check transitivity of \(\prec^*\):

  • \((u, 0) \prec^* (u, 1) \prec^* (v, 1) \implies (u, 0) \prec^* (v, 1)\) (present!);
  • \((u, 0) \prec^* (v, 0) \prec^* (w, 0) \implies (u, 0) \prec^* (w, 0)\) since \(a_u \mid a_v \mid a_w \implies a_u \mid a_w\) (present!).

Wait: in an antichain of \((\mathcal{P}^*, \prec^*)\):

  • \((u, 0) \prec^* (u, 1)\) forbids picking both roles for the same vertex \(u\);
  • If \(a_u \mid a_v\), then \((u, 0) \prec^* (v, 0)\), \((u, 1) \prec^* (v, 1)\), and \((u, 0) \prec^* (v, 1)\) forbid all role pairs except \(\{(u, 1), (v, 0)\}\) (keeping \(u\) as sink and \(v\) as source, so the directed edge \(u \to v\) has its tail in sinks and head in sources!).

Thus, maximum kept vertices in a bipartite subgraph \(=\) Maximum Antichain of \((\mathcal{P}^*, \prec^*)\)!

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

By Dilworth’s Theorem (Ch 08.1–08.2), since \(\prec^*\) is already transitively closed, the maximum antichain of the \(2N\)-element poset \(\mathcal{P}^*\) has size:

\[\alpha(\mathcal{P}^*) = 2N - \nu(G_{\text{bip}}), \qquad \text{MinDeletedVertices} = N - \alpha(\mathcal{P}^*) = \nu(G_{\text{bip}}) - N.\]

  • Bipartite Split Graph \(G_{\text{bip}}\):
    • Left vertices: \(L = \{(u, 0)_{\text{out}}, (u, 1)_{\text{out}} : u \in V\}\) (\(2N\) nodes);
    • Right vertices: \(R = \{(u, 0)_{\text{in}}, (u, 1)_{\text{in}} : u \in V\}\) (\(2N\) nodes).
    • For each \(u \in V\), add \((u, 0)_{\text{out}} \to (u, 1)_{\text{in}}\).
    • Enumerate multiples of each \(a_u\) via harmonic sieve (\(O(A \log A)\) where \(A = 5 \times 10^4\)). For each \(a_v = k \cdot a_u\) (\(k \ge 2\)) present in the input:
      • Add \((u, 0)_{\text{out}} \to (v, 0)_{\text{in}}\), \((u, 1)_{\text{out}} \to (v, 1)_{\text{in}}\), and \((u, 0)_{\text{out}} \to (v, 1)_{\text{in}}\) (this third edge is even redundant if we include \((u,0)\to(v,0)\to(v,1)\) in transitive closure, so including all 3 guarantees exact transitivity!).
  • Output: \(\text{MaxMatching}(G_{\text{bip}}) - N\).
  • Complexity: \(V_{\text{bip}} = 4N \le 2 \times 10^5\), \(E_{\text{bip}} \le 3 A \ln A + N \approx 1.7 \times 10^6\). Hopcroft–Karp runs in \(O(E \sqrt{V}) \approx 0.35\text{ s}\)! (Companion Note: Compare this role-splitting with Luogu P1263 [CEOI2002] Royal Guards, where obstacles split rows/columns into maximal contiguous segments \(H_{\text{seg}} \times V_{\text{seg}}\) before König matching.)

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Broken Gadget Counterexample

  • Toy Trace (\(A = \{2, 4, 8\}\), a 3-chain \(2 \mid 4 \mid 8\)):
    • Here \(N = 3\), so \(\mathcal{P}^*\) has \(2N = 6\) elements.
    • Maximum matching on \(G_{\text{bip}}\) finds \(\nu(G_{\text{bip}}) = 4\) (leaving an antichain of size \(6 - 4 = 2\), e.g., \(\{(8, 0), (4, 1)\}\) corresponding to keeping \(\{4, 8\}\)).
    • Minimum deletions \(= \nu(G_{\text{bip}}) - N = 4 - 3 = 1\) (deleting \(2\), \(4\), or \(8\) leaves a single edge \(K_2\), which is bipartite).
  • Broken Gadget Counterexample (FM-07): Suppose a solver forgets the self-role link \((u, 0) \prec^* (u, 1)\) and only adds \((u, 0) \to (v, 1)\) for \(a_u \mid a_v\). Then for an isolated integer \(a_u\) (or the middle of a chain), the antichain picks both \((u, 0)\) and \((u, 1)\), counting vertex \(u\) twice in the kept subgraph! The intra-vertex chain \((u, 0) \prec^* (u, 1)\) is essential.

Problem #207 — Lexicographically Smallest Distance Permutation (Luogu P1963 [NOI2009] 变换序列)

Problem Statement: For two indices \(i, j \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\) on a circle of length \(N\) (\(1 \le N \le 10^4\)), define their circular distance as:

\[d(i, j) = \min\bigl(|i - j|,\; N - |i - j|\bigr).\]

Given \(N\) required circular distances \(D_0, D_1, \dots, D_{N-1}\), find a permutation \(T = (T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{N-1})\) of \(\{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\) such that \(d(i, T_i) = D_i\) for every \(0 \le i < N\). If multiple valid permutations exist, output the lexicographically smallest sequence \((T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{N-1})\); if none exists, output No Answer.

NoteHint 1 — Degree-2 Candidate Graph & Lexicographic Order

For each \(i \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}\), the equation \(d(i, T_i) = D_i\) yields at most two candidate targets in \(\{0, \dots, N-1\}\):

\[y_{i,1} = (i - D_i + N) \bmod N, \qquad y_{i,2} = (i + D_i) \bmod N.\]

Sort each vertex \(i\)’s adjacency list so that \(\min(y_{i,1}, y_{i,2})\) comes first and \(\max(y_{i,1}, y_{i,2})\) comes second. Now, in what order should you run Kuhn’s augmenting-path DFS over \(i\) so that \(T_0\) is minimized first, then \(T_1\), and so on?

NoteHint 2 — Why Forward Kuhn Fails & Reverse Kuhn (\(i = N-1 \downarrow 0\)) Works

When Kuhn’s algorithm runs dfs(i), it assigns \(i\) to its first valid neighbor \(y_{\min}\) and recursively pushes any previously matched vertex \(j\) along an augmenting path to \(j\)’s second choice! Therefore, the vertex processed last gets first priority on its preferred neighbor, while vertices processed earlier get displaced! To give \(i = 0\) highest priority, then \(i = 1\), etc., run the outer loop backwards from \(i = N-1\) down to \(0\)!

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — Problem #207

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

  • Surface Cues: Construct a permutation \(T\) of \(\{0..N-1\}\) where each \(T_i\) belongs to a small candidate set \(\{y_{i,1}, y_{i,2}\}\) of size \(\le 2\), and minimize \((T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{N-1})\) lexicographically (\(N \le 10^4\)).
  • Why Standard Max-Flow + Edge Re-Testing Is Too Slow: Testing edges one by one with rollback takes \(O(N)\) flow runs. Here every left vertex has degree \(\le 2\), so the entire graph is a union of cycles and paths (\(E \le 2N\)), and Reverse-Order Kuhn DFS (Ch 05.1 / Ch 08.1) produces the lexicographically smallest perfect matching in a single pass!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

For each \(i \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}\), define its ordered candidate tuple:

\[E(i) = \bigl(\min((i - D_i + N) \bmod N,\; (i + D_i) \bmod N),\;\; \max((i - D_i + N) \bmod N,\; (i + D_i) \bmod N)\bigr)\]

(deduplicated if \(D_i = 0\) or \(2D_i = N\)). We seek a bijection \(T : \{0..N-1\} \to \{0..N-1\}\) with \(T_i \in E(i)\) minimizing the vector \((T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{N-1})\) in dictionary order.

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  • Invariant of Reverse-Order Kuhn (\(i = N-1 \downarrow 0\)): Suppose we iterate \(i = N-1, N-2, \dots, 0\), and inside dfs(u) we always inspect \(u\)’s neighbors in increasing order \(y_{\text{small}}, y_{\text{large}}\):

    1. When \(i\) is processed, dfs(i) tries \(y_{\text{small}}\) first.
    2. Any augmenting path triggered by dfs(i) only visits vertices \(u > i\) (since vertices \(< i\) have not been matched yet!).
    3. Thus, when we reach \(i = 0\), \(0\) claims its smallest feasible target in any perfect matching; inductively, each \(i\) claims its smallest target compatible with the already-optimal choices of \(\{0, \dots, i-1\}\) without ever disturbing a smaller index!

  • Algorithm:

    1. For each \(i \in \{0..N-1\}\), build adj[i] sorted in increasing order.
    2. Initialize match_left[0..N-1] = -1, match_right[0..N-1] = -1.
    3. For \(i = N-1\) down to \(0\):
      • Run dfs(i) with visitation tag vis[y] == i.
      • If dfs(i) returns false, output No Answer and terminate.
    4. Print match_right[0], match_right[1], ..., match_right[N-1].

  • Complexity: Since every left vertex has degree \(\le 2\), each connected component is a cycle or path; Kuhn’s algorithm takes \(O(N^2)\) worst-case time (and \(O(N)\) on average) with \(< 10\text{ ms}\) runtime for \(N = 10^4\).

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Broken Gadget Counterexample

  • Toy Trace (\(N = 4\), \(D = [1, 1, 1, 1]\)):
    • Sorted adjacency lists:
      • \(i = 0\): \([1, 3]\)
      • \(i = 1\): \([0, 2]\)
      • \(i = 2\): \([1, 3]\)
      • \(i = 3\): \([0, 2]\)
    • Reverse loop \(i = 3 \downarrow 0\):
      • \(i = 3\): matches \(3 \to 0\).
      • \(i = 2\): matches \(2 \to 1\).
      • \(i = 1\): tries \(0\) (held by \(3\)), pushes \(3 \to 2\), matches \(1 \to 0\).
      • \(i = 0\): tries \(1\) (held by \(2\)), pushes \(2 \to 3\), matches \(0 \to 1\).
    • Resulting permutation: \(T = [1, 0, 3, 2]\) (as opposed to \([3, 2, 1, 0]\)), which is the unique lexicographically smallest valid permutation!
  • Broken Gadget Counterexample: Running the outer loop forwards from \(i = 0\) to \(N-1\) on the exact same input:
    • \(i = 0\) matches \(1\); \(i = 1\) matches \(0\); \(i = 2\) tries \(1\), pushes \(i = 0\) from \(1\) to \(3\), and matches \(2 \to 1\); \(i = 3\) tries \(0\), pushes \(i = 1\) from \(0\) to \(2\), and matches \(3 \to 0\).
    • Forward Kuhn outputs \(T = [3, 2, 1, 0]\) — the lexicographically largest permutation instead of the smallest!

Problem #208 — Balanced Intervals on a Binary String (AtCoder AGC 056 C — 01 Balanced)

Problem Statement: We need to construct a 01-string \(S = s_1 s_2 \dots s_N\) of length \(N\) (\(2 \le N \le 10^6\)) satisfying \(M\) balance conditions (\(1 \le M \le 2 \times 10^5\)). The \(j\)-th condition specifies an interval \([L_j, R_j]\) (\(1 \le L_j < R_j \le N\), where \(R_j - L_j + 1\) is even) and requires that the substring \(s_{L_j} s_{L_j+1} \dots s_{R_j}\) contains an equal number of 0s and 1s.

It is guaranteed that at least one valid 01-string exists. Find the lexicographically smallest valid 01-string \(S\).

NoteHint 1 — Diagnostic Boundary Check (\(N = 10^6\) Rules Out Bipartite Matching!)

Can you match 0s and 1s inside each interval via bipartite flow? With \(N = 10^6\) and \(M = 2 \times 10^5\) overlapping intervals, any explicit matching graph TLEs. Instead, replace each 0 with \(-1\) and each 1 with \(+1\), and consider the prefix sum array \(P_0, P_1, \dots, P_N\)!

NoteHint 2 — Prefix-Sum Difference Constraints & Parity Coupling

Let \(P_i = \sum_{k=1}^i (2 s_k - 1) \in \mathbb{Z}\), where \(s_k \in \{0, 1\}\).

  1. Step constraint: \(P_i - P_{i-1} \in \{-1, +1\}\) for each \(1 \le i \le N\).
  2. Interval balance: \(P_{R_j} - P_{L_j - 1} = 0\) for each \(1 \le j \le M\).

Wait: \(P_i - P_{i-1} \in \{-1, +1\}\) looks non-convex because \(P_i - P_{i-1} \neq 0\), until you notice that \(P_i \equiv i \pmod 2\) is fixed! Since \(R_j - (L_j - 1)\) is even, every zero-weight edge preserves parity, so any integer solution to \(|P_i - P_{i-1}| \le 1\) automatically has \(|P_i - P_{i-1}| = 1\)!

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — Problem #208

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

  • Surface Cues: Construct the lexicographically smallest 01-string of length \(N \le 10^6\) subject to \(M \le 2 \times 10^5\) even-length interval balance equations.
  • Why It Is the Canonical Part II Diagnostic Boundary Decoy (Ch 08 \(\to\) Ch 21 Bridge): On the surface, “equal number of 0s and 1s in \([L_j, R_j]\)” sounds like pairing 0s and 1s (Ch 05–08). However, interval sum constraints on a 1D array always telescope under prefix sums \(P_i\), turning the problem into a System of Difference Constraints (Potential LP Dual, Ch 21) solvable in \(O(N + M)\) via 0-1 BFS! (Contrast this with Codeforces 653 D — Delivery Bears, where \(N \le 50\) and “equal weight \(w\) per bear” requires binary searching \(w\) and running integer Max-Flow with capacities \(\lfloor c_e / w \rfloor\).)

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Map '0' \(\mapsto -1\) and '1' \(\mapsto +1\), and let \(P_i = (\#\text{'1' in } 1..i) - (\#\text{'0' in } 1..i)\) with \(P_0 = 0\).

  • Parity Invariant: At every index \(i\), \(P_i \equiv i \pmod 2\). Since each interval \([L_j, R_j]\) has even length, \(R_j \equiv L_j - 1 \pmod 2\).

  • Relaxing \(\{-1, +1\}\) to \([-1, +1]\): Because every adjacent pair \((i-1, i)\) has opposite parity (\(i \not\equiv i-1 \pmod 2\)) and every zero-cost equality \(P_{R_j} = P_{L_j-1}\) connects vertices of the same parity, any integer assignment satisfying:

    \[-1 \le P_i - P_{i-1} \le +1 \quad (\forall 1 \le i \le N) \qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{R_j} = P_{L_j - 1} \quad (\forall 1 \le j \le M)\]

    automatically satisfies \(P_i \equiv i \pmod 2\), which forces \(P_i - P_{i-1} \neq 0 \implies P_i - P_{i-1} \in \{-1, +1\}\)!

  • Lexicographic Optimality: Choosing \(s_1 = \text{'0'}\) (\(P_1 = P_0 - 1\)), then \(s_2 = \text{'0'}\) (\(P_2 = P_1 - 1\)), etc., means making every prefix sum \(P_i\) as small as possible! In a difference-constraint system \(P_v - P_u \le w(u \to v)\) rooted at \(P_0 = 0\), the pointwise minimum valid potential vector (Wait: for \(P_1 \ge P_0 - 1\), minimizing \(P_i\) is symmetric to maximizing \(-P_i\)!). Let \(Q_i = \#\text{'0' in } 1..i - \#\text{'1' in } 1..i = -P_i\). To make \(S\) lexicographically smallest (as many '0's as early as possible), we want to maximize \(Q_1\), then \(Q_2\), …, i.e., compute the pointwise maximum valid potential \(Q_i\), which is the shortest path distance \(d(0, i)\) in the difference-constraint graph!

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

Let \(Q_i = -P_i\) with \(Q_0 = 0\). The constraints on \(Q\) are:

  1. \(Q_i - Q_{i-1} \le 1\) and \(Q_{i-1} - Q_i \le 1 \implies\) undirected edge \((i-1) \leftrightarrow i\) of weight 1 for all \(1 \le i \le N\).
  2. \(Q_{R_j} - Q_{L_j - 1} \le 0\) and \(Q_{L_j - 1} - Q_{R_j} \le 0 \implies\) undirected edge \((L_j - 1) \leftrightarrow R_j\) of weight 0 for all \(1 \le j \le M\).

By the triangle inequality of shortest paths, setting \(Q_i = \text{dist}(0, i)\) gives the maximum possible value of \(Q_i\) (and hence puts '0' at position \(i\) whenever \(Q_i = Q_{i-1} + 1\) is feasible!).

  • Algorithm:
    1. Build the graph on vertices \(\{0, 1, \dots, N\}\) with \(N\) weight-\(1\) edges and \(M\) weight-\(0\) edges.
    2. Run 0-1 BFS using a std::deque from source \(0\) to compute dist[0..N] in \(O(N + M)\) time.
    3. For each \(i = 1, \dots, N\): if dist[i] == dist[i-1] + 1, output '0'; else (necessarily dist[i] == dist[i-1] - 1) output '1'.

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Broken Gadget Counterexample

  • Toy Trace (\(N = 4, M = 1\), interval \([1, 2]\) balanced):
    • Edges of weight \(1\): \(0 \leftrightarrow 1 \leftrightarrow 2 \leftrightarrow 3 \leftrightarrow 4\).
    • Edge of weight \(0\): \(0 \leftrightarrow 2\) (since \(L_1 - 1 = 0, R_1 = 2\)).
    • 0-1 BFS distances from \(0\):
      • dist[0] = 0, dist[2] = 0 (via \(0 \xrightarrow{0} 2\));
      • dist[1] = 1 (via \(0 \xrightarrow{1} 1\)), dist[3] = 1 (via \(2 \xrightarrow{1} 3\));
      • dist[4] = 2 (via \(3 \xrightarrow{1} 4\)).
    • Differences \(\Delta Q_i = \text{dist}[i] - \text{dist}[i-1]\):
      • \(i = 1\): \(1 - 0 = +1 \implies \text{'0'}\)
      • \(i = 2\): \(0 - 1 = -1 \implies \text{'1'}\)
      • \(i = 3\): \(1 - 0 = +1 \implies \text{'0'}\)
      • \(i = 4\): \(2 - 1 = +1 \implies \text{'0'}\)
    • Output: "0100" (interval \([1, 2]\) is "01", balanced, and positions \(3, 4\) greedily take '0').
  • Broken Gadget Counterexample: Defining \(P_i = \#\text{'1' in } 1..i\) (only counting '1's instead of \(\#\text{'0'} - \#\text{'1'}\)): then the step constraints are \(0 \le P_i - P_{i-1} \le 1\), which loses the parity invariant! A shortest-path solver can then set \(P_i - P_{i-1}\) to fractional or inconsistent values when zero-edges and negative-edges mix, requiring \(O(NM)\) Bellman–Ford instead of \(O(N + M)\) 0-1 BFS with non-negative weights \(\{0, 1\}\)!

3.2 Part II Failure Mode Self-Audit Checklist (FM-07, FM-08 & Constructive Readout Traps)

Before advancing to Part III, verify that you can state from memory both the algebraic cause and the mechanical fix for each of the following six Part II bugs:

  1. FM-07 — Confusing Non-Transitive DAG Path Cover with Poset Antichain (Ch 08.1 vs. Ch 08.2):
    • Symptom: Your minimum path cover / maximum antichain size is strictly smaller (or larger) than the sample answer on a DAG where paths are allowed to “skip” already-visited vertices (Luogu P4298 祭祀, CF 590E Birthday).
    • Root Cause: Dilworth’s Theorem (\(\text{Max Antichain} = \text{Min Chain Cover} = |V| - \nu(G_{\text{bip}})\)) holds only on a transitive poset (\(u \prec v \land v \prec w \implies u \prec w\)). Without transitive closure, the bipartite graph \(G_{\text{bip}}\) only counts vertex-disjoint paths and cannot route two chains through a shared intermediate vertex.
    • Fix: If chains may share intermediate vertices (or if you need a Maximum Antichain), always compute the transitive closure (Floyd–Warshall / bitset DAG reachability) before running bipartite matching.
  2. FM-08 — Reflexive \((r + c) \bmod 2\) Coloring on Parity-Preserving Jumps (Ch 06.2):
    • Symptom: Bipartite MIS outputs \(|V_{\text{free}}|\) (zero flow) or misses obvious conflicts (Luogu P5030 长脖子鹿放置, \((\pm 1, \pm 3)\) jumps, or \((\pm 2, \pm 2)\) jumps).
    • Root Cause: When \(\Delta r + \Delta c\) is even, \((r + c) \bmod 2\) does not change along an attack edge.
    • Fix: Test the step generators \((\Delta r, \Delta c)\) algebraically:
      • If \(\Delta r + \Delta c\) is odd: use \((r + c) \bmod 2\).
      • If \(\Delta r\) is odd and \(\Delta c\) is odd: use \(r \bmod 2\) (or \(c \bmod 2\)).
      • If \(\Delta r, \Delta c\) are multiples of \(2^k\): divide coordinates by \(2^k\) inside each \(\bmod 2^k\) residue class first (Ch 06.2).
  3. Constructive Kőnig Vertex Cover / Independent Set Extraction Trap (Ch 05.2):

    • Symptom: You know the size \(\tau(G) = \nu(G)\), but taking {all matched vertices in L} gives a set that fails to cover some edges!

    • Fix: Start an alternating BFS/DFS from all unmatched vertices in \(L\) (traversing unmatched edges \(L \to R\) and matched edges \(R \to L\)). Then:

      \[\text{MVC} = (L \setminus Z_L) \cup (R \cap Z_R), \qquad \text{MIS} = (L \cap Z_L) \cup (R \setminus Z_R).\]

  4. Forward vs. Reverse Kuhn Order in Lexicographic Matching (Ch 05.1 / CP-II #207):
    • Symptom: You sorted each adjacency list in increasing order, but your matching is lexicographically largest or scrambled at early indices.
    • Fix: Because Kuhn’s augmenting DFS displaces previously matched vertices to make room for the currently queried vertex, you must process the highest-priority vertices last (iterate \(i = N-1\) down to \(0\)).
  5. Dulmage–Mendelsohn Game Start Trap (Ch 06.3):
    • Symptom: Treating any vertex matched in one arbitrary maximum matching \(M^*\) as a winning start for the first player (Luogu P4055, Luogu P1971).
    • Fix: The first player wins from \(v_0\) iff \(v_0\) is matched in every maximum matching (\(v_0 \in E_L \cup E_R\)), which requires checking that no alternating path from an unmatched vertex can reach \(v_0\).
  6. Duplicate Obstacle Coordinates in Grid MIS (Ch 06.2 / CP-II #204):
    • Symptom: Computing \(|V_{\text{free}}| = N \cdot M - K\) when the input lists the same forbidden cell \((r, c)\) multiple times.
    • Fix: Always deduplicate forbidden cells in a boolean array bad[r][c] and count |V_free| by iterating over the grid.

4. Readiness Bridge to Part III — From Unweighted König Duality to the Minimum Cut Compiler (燃やす埋める)

Throughout Part II (Ch 05–Ch 08), every decision was unweighted (or uniform-cost) and every conflict was a hard binary exclusion on a bipartite or poset structure:

\[\tau(G) = \nu(G), \qquad \alpha(G) = |V| - \nu(G).\]

What happens when the problem statement breaks out of the unweighted bipartite world along any of the following three axes?

  1. Weighted Vertices & Implicational Dependencies (Ch 06 \(\to\) Ch 09 & Ch 11):
    • In Luogu P3355 骑士共存问题 (#042) and Luogu P5030 长脖子鹿放置 (#204), every cell had weight \(1\).
    • In Luogu P2774 方格取数问题 (#081, Ch 11.1), cell \((r, c)\) has profit \(w_{r,c} > 0\): replacing the unit capacities \(S \xrightarrow{1} u\) and \(v \xrightarrow{1} T\) by \(S \xrightarrow{w_u} u\) and \(v \xrightarrow{w_v} T\) (while locking conflict edges at \(u \xrightarrow{\infty} v\)) turns König’s Theorem into Weighted Bipartite Minimum Vertex Cover / Maximum Weight Independent Set via \(s\)-\(t\) Minimum Cut!
    • Even better: when implications \(x_u = 1 \implies x_v = 1\) (u \xrightarrow{\infty} v) are placed on an arbitrary directed graph (not necessarily bipartite!), the exact same \(s\)-\(t\) Min-Cut gadget solves Picard’s Maximum-Weight Closure / Project Selection (最大权闭合子图, Ch 09) in polynomial time!
  2. Soft Penalties & \(2 \times 2\) Payoff Matrices (Ch 07 \(\to\) Ch 10–Ch 11 燃やす埋める):
    • In Codeforces 1404 E — Bricks (#082 / #205 Track B), two adjacent borders sharing an L-corner were strictly forbidden from both being selected (\(\infty\) penalty).
    • What if choosing two adjacent items \((u, v)\) in the same class or different classes incurs a finite submodular cost table \(\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}\) with \(B + C \ge A + D\) (Ch 10), or a supermodular reward table \(A + D > B + C\) on a bipartite graph rescued by Bipartite Variable Inversion \(y_j = 1 - x_j\) (Ch 11, AtCoder AGC 038 F — Two Permutations)?
  3. Multi-State Variables \(x_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}\) (Ch 08 \(\to\) Ch 12–Ch 13):
    • In Part III (Ch 12–Ch 13), we upgrade binary indicators \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\) to Unary Threshold Chains \(z_{i,1} \ge z_{i,2} \ge \dots \ge z_{i,K-1}\) (切糕 / K値燃やす埋める) and Coarea Level-Set Integrals (Codeforces 1427 G — One Billion Shades of Grey), compiling multi-valued convex optimization directly into \(s\)-\(t\) minimum cuts.
