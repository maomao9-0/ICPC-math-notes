Checkpoint III — Part III Blind Recognition & Pseudo-Boolean Cut Compiler Audit

Part III Blind Diagnostic Assessment (Chapters 09–13 · Untagged Contest Problems, 燃やす埋める Compilation & Failure-Mode Triage)

When you solve an exercise at the end of Chapter 9 (“Maximum-Weight Closure and Project Selection”) or Chapter 11 (“Bipartite Variable Inversion and Supermodular Rescue”), the chapter title itself tells you which algebraic transformation to apply. Before reading the problem statement, you already know whether to orient implication arcs \(u \xrightarrow{\infty} v\), introduce a subset-bonus hub node, flip the indicator variables \(y_j = 1 - x_j\) on one side of a bipartite graph (二部グラフの片側反転), or expand discrete heights into unary threshold chains \(z_{i,t} = [h_i \ge t]\) (切糕).

In a live ICPC, NOI, Codeforces, or AtCoder contest, problems arrive completely untagged. Worse, Part III problems rarely mention graphs, flows, or cuts at all: they are phrased as discrete optimization over binary choices \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\), ternary sign assignments \(s_i \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\), or integer height fields \(h_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}\). Your decisive competitive skill is blind algebraic-to-cut compilation (燃やす埋める / Pseudo-Boolean Min-Cut Compilation): translating English profit/penalty clauses into a pseudo-Boolean polynomial, testing submodularity (\(B + C \ge A + D\)) or convexity (\(\Delta^2 \phi \ge 0\)), and compiling that polynomial into an exact directed \(s\)-\(t\) cut network without falling into any of the Part III failure modes (FM-09–FM-13)—while also recognizing non-flow decoys where greedy exchange or 2D prefix/monotone-stack enumeration outperforms network flow.

ImportantHow to Use This Blind Recognition Checkpoint
  1. Zero Visible Chapter or Technique Spoilers: None of the eight problem headers or statements in Section 2 reveal which chapter (Ch 09–Ch 13) applies, or whether the problem is a non-flow diagnostic decoy.
  2. Execute the 3-Pass Blind Protocol Before Peeking: For each problem (#209–#216), spend 5–10 minutes on scratch paper completing three passes before opening any dropdown:
    • Pass 1 (Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal & Decoy Triage): What are the decision variables (binary \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\), 4-layer chain states, or \(K\)-level integer heights \(h_i\)), what are the scale bounds (\(N, M, K\)), and does a greedy exchange or monotone-stack sweep solve it faster than Dinic?
    • Pass 2 (Intermediate Pseudo-Boolean / Level-Set Polynomial): Write the objective as \(\text{BaseProfit} - \text{Penalty}(x)\) in terms of cut indicators \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\) (where \(x_i = 0 \iff i \in S\) and \(x_i = 1 \iff i \in T\)) or unary threshold indicators \(z_{i,t} = [h_i \ge t]\) (\(1 = z_{i,0} \ge z_{i,1} \ge \dots \ge z_{i,K} \ge z_{i,K+1} = 0\)).
    • Pass 3 (Submodularity & Monotonicity Audit): Check every pairwise interaction \(c_{00}(1-x_u)(1-x_v) + c_{01}(1-x_u)x_v + c_{10}x_u(1-x_v) + c_{11}x_u x_v\). Is \(c_{01} + c_{10} \ge c_{00} + c_{11}\) directly (Ch 10), or is \(c_{00} + c_{11} > c_{01} + c_{10}\) on a bipartite conflict graph requiring variable inversion \(y_v = 1 - x_v\) (Ch 11)? On multi-state chains, have you included the reverse monotonicity locks \((i, t+1) \xrightarrow{\infty} (i, t)\) (Ch 12) and verified convexity \(\Delta^2 \phi \ge 0\) (Ch 13)?
  3. 3-Tier Progressive Unmasking:
    • Open Hint 1 (Diagnostic Signal & Chapter Reveal) only if you cannot determine which Part III paradigm (Ch 09–Ch 13 or non-flow decoy) governs the problem.
    • Open Hint 2 (Intermediate Algebraic / Pseudo-Boolean Model) to verify your cut polynomial and variable orientation before drawing vertices and edges.
    • Open Full 4-Step Compilation & Solution to check your mechanical graph compilation, hand-trace a small example (\(N \le 4\)), and audit your code against the broken-gadget counterexamples (FM-09–FM-13).

Drill 1.1 — The Unreachable Positive-Cycle Trap in Max-Weight Closure

In a grid defense game (Luogu P2805 [NOI2009] 植物大战僵尸), eating plant \(u\) yields net score \(w_u \in \mathbb{Z}\), and plant \(u\) can only be eaten after every plant \(v\) that protects \(u\) (or sits to the right of \(u\) in the same row) has already been eaten. A contestant wires \(S \xrightarrow{w_u} u\) for \(w_u > 0\), \(u \xrightarrow{-w_u} T\) for \(w_u < 0\), and \(u \xrightarrow{\infty} v\) whenever eating \(u\) requires eating \(v\). They run Dinic directly and output \(\sum_{w_u > 0} w_u - \text{MinCut}(S, T)\), getting Wrong Answer on every test case containing a mutually protecting pair \(u \leftrightarrows v\).

Why does the raw Picard closure graph fail here, and what exact pre-processing pass is required before building the flow network?

TipAnswer & Diagnosis — Drill 1.1
  • Failure Mode: FM-09 (Unpruned Cycles & Downstream Descendants in Project Selection, Chapter 9).
  • Mathematical Root Cause: In the physical game, zombies eat plants one by one in a valid topological order. If plant \(u\) protects \(v\) and \(v\) protects \(u\) (\(u \leftrightarrows v\)), neither plant can ever be eaten first! Furthermore, any third plant \(w\) that depends on \(u\) (\(w \to u \leftrightarrows v\)) is also permanently locked.
    • However, in Picard’s simultaneous set-closure formulation (Ch 09.1), the closed set \(C = \{u, v, w\}\) satisfies \((x \in C \land x \to y) \implies y \in C\) trivially! If \(w_u + w_v + w_w > 0\), the \(s\)-\(t\) min-cut happily leaves \(\{u, v, w\}\) on the \(S\)-side and collects their positive weight even though no valid sequential eating order exists.
  • Mandatory Defensive Fix (Ch 09.3): Before constructing the flow network, run Kahn’s topological sort (indegree queue) on the prerequisite graph (where edges point from prerequisite \(v \to \text{dependent } u\)). Any vertex \(u\) that is not visited by Kahn’s algorithm belongs either to a directed cycle or to the downstream shadow of a directed cycle; all such vertices must be deleted before wiring \(S\) and \(T\).

Drill 1.2 — The Reversed \(\infty\)-Arc Disaster on \(T\)-Side Subset Bonuses

In a binary partitioning problem (Codeforces 311 E Biologist / Luogu P1361 小M的作物), choosing \(x_i = 0\) (\(i \in S\)) or \(x_i = 1\) (\(i \in T\)) grants individual payoffs, and a bonus clause \(j\) grants an extra reward \(W_j > 0\) if every variable in subset \(A_j\) is assigned \(x_u = 1\) (\(u \in T\)). A contestant creates an auxiliary hub vertex \(B_j\), connects \(B_j \xrightarrow{W_j} T\), and connects \(B_j \xrightarrow{\infty} u\) for every \(u \in A_j\).

What happens to the minimum cut of this network, and how must the \(\infty\)-arcs be oriented?

TipAnswer & Diagnosis — Drill 1.2
  • Failure Mode: FM-10 (Inverted \(\infty\)-Arc Orientation on \(T\)-Side Clause Hubs, Chapter 10).
  • Why \(B_j \xrightarrow{\infty} u\) Fails Completely: In an \(s\)-\(t\) cut \((S, T)\), an arc pays its capacity only when its tail is in \(S\) and its head is in \(T\).
    • If you wire \(B_j \xrightarrow{\infty} u\) and \(B_j \xrightarrow{W_j} T\), there is zero incoming capacity into \(B_j\) from \(S\)!
    • Placing \(B_j \in T\) produces cut cost \(0\) on \(B_j \xrightarrow{\infty} u\) (since tail \(B_j \in T\)) and cut cost \(0\) on \(B_j \xrightarrow{W_j} T\) (both endpoints in \(T\)), regardless of whether \(u \in S\) or \(u \in T\)! Thus the penalty \(W_j\) is never paid, and the solver collects the bonus \(W_j\) for free even when every \(u \in A_j\) is in \(S\).
  • Exact Rule (Ch 10.3):
    • All-\(S\) (\(x_u = 0\)) Bonus Hub \(B_A\): Flow must go \(S \xrightarrow{W_j} B_A \xrightarrow{\infty} u\) (\(\forall u \in A_j\)). If any \(u \in T\), placing \(B_A \in S\) crosses \(B_A \xrightarrow{\infty} u\) (cost \(\infty\)), forcing \(B_A \in T\) and cutting \(S \xrightarrow{W_j} B_A\) at cost \(W_j\).
    • All-\(T\) (\(x_u = 1\)) Bonus Hub \(B_B\): Flow must go \(u \xrightarrow{\infty} B_B \xrightarrow{W_j} T\) (\(\forall u \in A_j\)). If any \(u \in S\), placing \(B_B \in T\) crosses \(u \xrightarrow{\infty} B_B\) (cost \(\infty\)), forcing \(B_B \in S\) and cutting \(B_B \xrightarrow{W_j} T\) at cost \(W_j\).

Drill 1.3 — Submodular vs. Supermodular \(2 \times 2\) Tables & The Odd-Cycle Barrier

Consider two binary decision problems over an undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) with variables \(x_u \in \{0, 1\}\):

  • Scenario A: For each edge \(\{u, v\} \in E\), you pay a penalty \(w_{uv} > 0\) whenever \(x_u = x_v\) (both \(0\) or both \(1\)).
  • Scenario B: You are given a triangle \(V = \{1, 2, 3\}\) with edges \(\{1, 2\}, \{2, 3\}, \{3, 1\}\), and for each edge you pay \(w_{uv} > 0\) whenever \(x_u = x_v = 1\).

Check the submodularity condition \(c_{01} + c_{10} \ge c_{00} + c_{11}\) for both scenarios. Under what structural condition on \(G\) can Scenario A be compiled into an \(s\)-\(t\) min-cut, and why does Scenario B fail Bipartite Variable Inversion?

TipAnswer & Diagnosis — Drill 1.3
  • Failure Mode: FM-11 (Supermodular Sign Violation & Odd-Cycle Bipartite Inversion Failure, Chapter 11).
  • Submodularity Check:
    • In Scenario A, \(c_{00} = c_{11} = w_{uv} > 0\) and \(c_{01} = c_{10} = 0\), so \(c_{01} + c_{10} = 0 < 2w_{uv} = c_{00} + c_{11}\). In Scenario B, \(c_{11} = w_{uv} > 0\) and \(c_{00} = c_{01} = c_{10} = 0\), so \(0 < w_{uv}\). Both cost tables are strictly supermodular (cannot be compiled directly via Ch 10).
  • When Bipartite Variable Inversion (二部グラフの片側反転, Ch 11.1) Rescues Scenario A:
    • If and only if \(G = (L \sqcup R, E)\) is bipartite, we can substitute \(y_v = 1 - x_v\) for all \(v \in R\) (while keeping \(y_u = x_u\) for \(u \in L\)).
    • Then for every edge \((u, v) \in L \times R\), the condition \(x_u = x_v\) transforms into \(y_u \neq y_v\), flipping the supermodular penalty \(w_{uv}[x_u = x_v]\) into the canonical submodular cut penalty \(w_{uv}[y_u \neq y_v] = w_{uv} y_u(1 - y_v) + w_{uv}(1 - y_u)y_v\)!
  • Why Scenario B Fails: A triangle \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) is an odd cycle (\(C_3\)). No 2-coloring of \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) exists: flipping any subset of variables leaves at least one edge with both endpoints unflipped (or both flipped), keeping its penalty supermodular (indeed, minimizing \(\sum_{\{u,v\} \in E} w_{uv}[x_u = x_v]\) on general graphs with odd cycles is equivalent to MAX-CUT, which is NP-hard).

Drill 1.4 — The Double-Cut Catastrophe on Multi-State Unary Chains

In a multi-state grid smoothing problem (Luogu P3227 [HNOI2013] 切糕), each column \(u\) must be assigned a height \(h_u \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) at cost \(w(u, h_u) > 0\), subject to \(|h_u - h_v| \le 1\) for adjacent columns \((u, v)\). A contestant builds a 3-edge chain \(S = (u, 1) \xrightarrow{w(u,1)} (u, 2) \xrightarrow{w(u,2)} (u, 3) \xrightarrow{w(u,3)} (u, 4) = T\) for each column \(u\), adds the diagonal smoothness arcs \((u, k) \xrightarrow{\infty} (v, k-1)\), and omits the reverse arcs \((u, k+1) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, k)\) because “Dinic only pushes flow forward from \(S\) to \(T\) anyway.”

Construct a 2-column numerical trace showing that omitting \((u, k+1) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, k)\) causes the min-cut to cut a single column twice and return an impossible cost.

TipAnswer & Diagnosis — Drill 1.4
  • Failure Mode: FM-12 (Omitting Reverse \(\infty\)-Monotonicity Arcs on Unary Chains, Chapter 12).
  • 2-Column Numerical Counterexample: Let \(u\) and \(v\) be adjacent with \(D = 1\) (\(|h_u - h_v| \le 1\)) and costs: \[w(u, \cdot) = (1, 100, 1), \qquad w(v, \cdot) = (100, 1, 100).\]
    • True Optimal Assignment: Since \(h_v = 2\) costs \(1\) and \(|h_u - h_v| \le 1\), we can pick either \(h_u = 1\) or \(h_u = 3\) (cost \(1\)), giving true minimum cost \(1 + 1 = 2\). Wait—what if the cross-constraint instead forces both \((u, 3) \xrightarrow{\infty} (v, 2)\) and \((v, 3) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, 2)\), or suppose \(w(u, \cdot) = (1, 100, 1)\) has \((v, 2) \in S\) and \((v, 3) \in T\) via diagonal edges \((v, 2) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, 3)\) and \((u, 2) \xrightarrow{\infty} (v, 3)\)?
    • Specifically, suppose external constraints force \((u, 2) \in T\) (so edge \((u, 1) \to (u, 2)\) is cut, cost \(1\)) AND force \((u, 3) \in S\) (via a diagonal \(\infty\)-arc from some neighbor \(v\)’s node in \(S\) into \((u, 3)\))!
    • Without the reverse lock \((u, 3) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, 2)\): The partition places \((u, 1) \in S\), \((u, 2) \in T\), \((u, 3) \in S\), \((u, 4) \in T\). The cut severs two edges on column \(u\)’s chain—\((u, 1) \to (u, 2)\) (cost \(1\)) and \((u, 3) \to (u, 4)\) (cost \(1\))—paying \(1 + 1 = 2\) to bypass the expensive middle edge \(w(u, 2) = 100\), which corresponds to the non-monotonic garbage state \(z_{u,1} = 1, z_{u,2} = 0, z_{u,3} = 1\)!
  • Why Reverse \(\infty\)-Arcs Are Mandatory (Ch 12.1): When \((u, 2) \in T\) and \((u, 3) \in S\), the reverse arc \((u, 3) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, 2)\) has its tail in \(S\) and its head in \(T\), incurring cut penalty \(\infty\)! Thus \((u, k+1) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, k)\) enforces the unary threshold invariant \(z_{u,1} \ge z_{u,2} \ge \dots \ge z_{u,K}\) so every column chain is cut in the forward direction at most once (and exactly once when \(w(u, k) \ge 0\)).

Drill 1.5 — The Non-Convex Ishikawa Penalty Trap

In a discrete signal restoration problem (Ch 13), each vertex \(u \in V\) chooses a level \(h_u \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\), and each edge \((u, v) \in E\) incurs a smooth interaction penalty \(\phi(h_u - h_v)\). A programmer applies Ishikawa’s cross-difference formula (Ch 13.1), setting the capacity of the cross-arc \((u, i) \to (v, j)\) to: \[w_{(u, i) \to (v, j)} = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\phi(i - j + 1) - 2\phi(i - j) + \phi(i - j - 1)\bigr)\] for two different penalty functions:

  1. Truncated Linear Penalty: \(\phi_{\text{trunc}}(d) = \min(|d|, 2)\).
  2. Thresholded Quadratic Jump: \(\phi_{\text{conv}}(d) = |d| + 3 \cdot \max(0, |d| - 1)\).

Which of these two penalty functions produces a valid Dinic network, and what goes wrong with the other?

TipAnswer & Diagnosis — Drill 1.5
  • Failure Mode: FM-13 (Non-Convex Penalty Function yielding Negative Ishikawa Capacities, Chapter 13).
  • Second Finite Difference Audit (\(\Delta^2 \phi(d) = \phi(d+1) - 2\phi(d) + \phi(d-1) \ge 0\)):
    1. For \(\phi_{\text{trunc}}(d) = \min(|d|, 2)\), the values for \(d = 0, 1, 2, 3\) are \(0, 1, 2, 2\). At \(d = 2\): \[\Delta^2 \phi_{\text{trunc}}(2) = \phi_{\text{trunc}}(3) - 2\phi_{\text{trunc}}(2) + \phi_{\text{trunc}}(1) = 2 - 2(2) + 1 = -1 < 0!\] Because \(\phi_{\text{trunc}}\) is concave at \(|d| = 2\), the cross-arc \((u, 3) \to (v, 1)\) receives a negative capacity \(-1/2\), which is illegal in \(s\)-\(t\) min-cut (indeed, multi-label energy minimization with truncated penalties is NP-hard and only solvable approximately via \(\alpha\)-expansion).
    2. For \(\phi_{\text{conv}}(d) = |d| + 3\max(0, |d|-1)\), the values for \(d = 0, 1, 2, 3\) are \(0, 1, 5, 9\), with first differences \(+1, +4, +4\) (non-decreasing!). Thus \(\Delta^2 \phi_{\text{conv}}(d) \ge 0\) for all \(d \in \mathbb{Z}\), all Ishikawa cross-capacities are non-negative, and the \(s\)-\(t\) min-cut is exact.

Drill 1.6 — Coarea Level-Set Slicing vs. Explicit Unary Chain Blowup

You are asked to choose integer values \(h_1, \dots, h_N \in [0, 10^9]\) on an undirected weighted graph \(G = (V, E)\) (\(N \le 500, M \le 2000\)) to minimize: \[\sum_{u=1}^N c_u |h_u - b_u| + \sum_{\{u, v\} \in E} w_{uv} |h_u - h_v|,\] where \(b_u \in [0, 10^9]\) are given target values and \(c_u, w_{uv} > 0\). Why does building an explicit Ch 12 / Ch 13 unary chain fail immediately, and how does the Discrete Coarea Theorem (Ch 13.2) solve this in polynomial time?

TipAnswer & Diagnosis — Drill 1.6
  • Diagnostic Contrast: Ch 12 Explicit Chain (\(O(N \cdot H)\) vertices) vs. Ch 13.2 Coarea Level-Set Decoupling.
  • Why Explicit Unary Chains Fail: Because the coordinate range is \(H = 10^9\), creating a node \((u, t)\) for every \(t \in \{1, \dots, H\}\) requires \(500 \times 10^9\) vertices (Memory/Time Limit Exceeded).
  • Coarea Level-Set Decoupling (Ch 13.2):
    • Substitute the level-set indicator \(z_u(t) = [h_u > t] \in \{0, 1\}\). Since \(|x - y| = \int_0^{10^9} |[x > t] - [y > t]|\,dt\), the objective decouples completely across thresholds \(t\) with zero cross-threshold edges: \[\int_0^{10^9} \left( \sum_{u=1}^N c_u \bigl|z_u(t) - [b_u > t]\bigr| + \sum_{\{u,v\} \in E} w_{uv} |z_u(t) - z_v(t)| \right) dt.\]
    • Because the integrand at threshold \(t\) depends only on the relative order of \(t\) against the sorted target values \(b_{(1)} < b_{(2)} < \dots < b_{(K)}\) (\(K \le N \le 500\)), the optimal binary cut \(z(t) \in \{0, 1\}^N\) is constant on each elementary interval \((b_{(k)}, b_{(k+1)})\)!
    • Moreover, by submodular cut nesting (\(S^*(t_1) \supseteq S^*(t_2)\) for \(t_1 < t_2\)), all optimal \(h_u^*\) belong to \(\{b_1, \dots, b_N\}\) and can be found in \(O(K)\) cuts (or \(O(M\sqrt{N} \log N)\) via divide-and-conquer 整体二分 in Ch 24).

NoteHint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Chapter Reveal (#216)
  • Target Chapter & Archetype: Ch 09 — Maximum-Weight Closure and Project Selection (§9.4 Transitive Reduction / Last-Occurrence DAG Sparsification in Maximum-Weight Closure).
  • Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal:
    1. The problem statement is literally the textbook definition of Maximum-Weight Closure: \(i \in C \text{ and } (j < i,\; a_j \mid a_i) \implies j \in C\).
    2. However, at \(N = 3000\), adding an edge \(i \xrightarrow{\infty} j\) for every \(j < i\) with \(a_j \mid a_i\) creates up to \(\binom{3000}{2} \approx 4.5 \times 10^6\) edges!
    3. Look at the bound \(a_i \in \{1, \dots, 100\}\): why is \(A_{\max} = 100\) so tiny while \(N = 3000\)? Because divisibility is transitive! If \(j_1 < j_2 < i\) and \(a_{j_1} = a_{j_2}\) with \(a_{j_2} \mid a_i\), then \(j_2\) already has an \(\infty\)-arc \(j_2 \xrightarrow{\infty} j_1\) (since \(a_{j_1} \mid a_{j_2}\))!
NoteHint 2 — Intermediate Algebraic / Last-Occurrence Sparsification (#216)

Let \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\) (\(x_i = 0 \iff i \in S\) chosen, \(x_i = 1 \iff i \in T\) unchosen). For each index \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) and each distinct value \(v \in \{1, \dots, 100\}\) that divides \(a_i\), define: \[\operatorname{last}(i, v) = \max\{\, j < i \mid a_j = v \,\}.\]

Claim (Transitive Closure Equivalence): Wiring \(i \xrightarrow{\infty} \operatorname{last}(i, v)\) for only the most recent occurrence \(\operatorname{last}(i, v)\) of each divisor \(v \mid a_i\) preserves the exact reachability closure of the full \(O(N^2)\) graph!

Proof: Suppose \(j < i\) satisfies \(a_j = v \mid a_i\). Either \(j = \operatorname{last}(i, v)\) (direct arc \(i \xrightarrow{\infty} j\)), or \(j < j_1 = \operatorname{last}(i, v)\) with \(a_{j_1} = v\). Since \(v \mid v\), \(j_1\) in turn connects to \(j_2 = \operatorname{last}(j_1, v)\), forming a directed chain of \(\infty\)-arcs \(i \xrightarrow{\infty} j_1 \xrightarrow{\infty} j_2 \xrightarrow{\infty} \dots \xrightarrow{\infty} j\)! Thus \(i \in S\) still forces \(j \in S\), while the out-degree of vertex \(i\) drops from \(O(N)\) to at most \(\max_{a \le 100} d(a) = 12\)!

4. Readiness Bridge to Part IV: Fractional Programming, Planar Duality, Min-Cut DP & Gomory–Hu Cut Trees

In Part III (Ch 09–Ch 13), you mastered the compilation half of minimum-cut theory: translating non-graph algebraic objectives into a single static directed network \(G = (V, E, c, s, t)\) and calling Dinic once.     • In #209 (Gold Mine) and #216 (Strange Set), you maximized a linear sum \(\sum_{i \in C} b_i - \sum_{e \in \text{cut}} c_e\).

  2. From Grid Partition Cuts (Ch 10–Ch 13) to \((s, t)\)-Planar Cycle–Cut Duality & Shortest Paths (Ch 15):
    • In #210 (Wolves and Sheep) and #214 (Cross), you ran Dinic on \(100 \times 100\) grids (\(V = 10^4\)). What if the grid is \(1000 \times 1000\) (\(V = 10^6\)), where Dinic times out?
    • In Chapter 15, when the cut network is \((s, t)\)-planar (or general planar), every minimum \(s\)-\(t\) cut bijects with a shortest path in the planar dual graph \(G^*\), replacing \(O(V^{1.5})\) Dinic with \(O(V \log V)\) Dijkstra ([ICPC-Beijing 2006] 狼抓兔子 and [NOI2010] 海拔).
  3. From Explicit Cut Graphs (Ch 09–Ch 13) to Min-Cut DP, Stoer–Wagner & Gomory–Hu Cut Trees (Ch 16):
    • Whenever a Ch 10–Ch 13 cut graph has bounded treewidth, small corridor width, or complete-bipartite cardinality symmetry (like ARC 125 E Snack or CF 724 E Goods Transportation), Chapter 16 shows how to eliminate the flow solver altogether and compute \(\text{MinCut}(S, T)\) directly via Dynamic Programming over the \(S/T\) partition.
