From Edge-Only Conservation to Node Bottlenecks, Menger Connectivity, Multi-Stage Routing, and Dynamic Time Horizons

1. Motivation: Compiling Node Bottlenecks, Multi-Stage Decisions, and Time Horizons into Arcs

In Chapters 1 and 2, every capacity constraint lived directly on a directed arc \(e = (u \to v)\):

\[0 \le f(u, v) \le c(u, v).\]

Vertices \(v \notin \{s, t\}\) were treated as frictionless, infinite-bandwidth junctions whose only role was to enforce Kirchhoff flow conservation:

\[\sum_{u : (u, v) \in E} f(u, v) = \sum_{w : (v, w) \in E} f(v, w).\]

Real contest problems, however, rarely hand you a pre-built edge-capacitated graph. Instead, the problem statement hides the flow structure behind three non-arc bottlenecks:

  1. Physical Node Bottlenecks: Routers overheat after forwarding \(C_v\) packets ([CQOI2015] 网络吞吐量), stone pillars crumble after \(h_{r,c}\) lizard jumps ([SCOI2007] 蜥蜴), or thin ice floes sink after a single survivor steps off them ([UVa 11380] Down Went The Titanic). Here, the limit constrains the total flow passing through a vertex \(v\), regardless of which incoming edge it arrives on or which outgoing edge it leaves on.
  2. Multi-Stage Entity Coupling: A hotel guest is satisfied only if assigned both a liked room \(r \in \mathcal{R}\) and a liked dish \(d \in \mathcal{D}\) ([Luogu P1402] 酒店之王). If we model the guest as a single vertex \(g\) between rooms and dishes (\(r \to g \to d\)), conservation at \(g\) guarantees that the number of rooms assigned to \(g\) equals the number of dishes assigned to \(g\), but it does not stop a single guest with two liked rooms and two liked dishes from absorbing \(2\) units of flow!
  3. Temporal & Sequential Coupling: Space shuttles cycle periodically between stations over discrete time steps \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) ([CTSC1999] 家园). An edge that exists at \(t = 1\) may vanish at \(t = 2\), and passengers can wait at stations across time steps. On a static graph, flow is instantaneous: a unit of flow entering \(u\) at \(t = 5\) could illegally depart along an edge that was only available at \(t = 1\).

All three phenomena share a single mathematical cure: state-space expansion. Rather than modifying Dinic’s solver to track vertex budgets or timestamps, we compile vertex capacities, multi-stage roles, and discrete time horizons into ordinary directed arcs by splitting each logical entity into fine-grained state vertices:

  • Vertex Capacity Splitting: \(v \mapsto (v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_v} v_{\text{out}})\) turns node throughput into an internal arc capacity.
  • Time-Expanded Layering (分层图): \(v \mapsto (v, 0) \xrightarrow{\infty} (v, 1) \xrightarrow{\infty} \dots \xrightarrow{\infty} (v, T)\) turns temporal causality into a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG).
  • Hub Sparsification: Replacing an \(m\)-clique of valid jumps with a shared hub vertex \(H_{\text{row}}\) turns \(\Theta(m^2)\) transitions into \(2m\) arcs.

Before we build these state-expanded graphs, however, we need a rigorous bridge back from aggregate arc flows \(f(e)\) to individual physical trajectories (paths of lizards, survivors, astronauts, or subsequences). That bridge is the Flow Decomposition Theorem.

2. The Flow Decomposition Theorem & Constructive Path Extraction

A maximum-flow solver computes a single scalar \(f(e) \in [0, c(e)]\) on each arc \(e \in E\). Yet many problems ask not merely for the scalar throughput \(|f|\), but for the explicit trajectories of the \(|f|\) agents, or rely on the equivalence between an aggregate flow of value \(k\) and \(k\) mutually disjoint \(s\)-\(t\) paths.

2.1 Statement and Constructive Proof of the Flow Decomposition Theorem

ImportantTheorem 3.1 (Flow Decomposition Theorem — Ford & Fulkerson, 1962; 流分解定理)

Let \(G = (V, E, c)\) be a directed network with source \(s\) and sink \(t\), and let \(f: E \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) be any feasible \(s\)-\(t\) flow of value \(|f| \ge 0\). Then there exists a collection of simple directed \(s\)-\(t\) paths \(\mathcal{P} = \{P_1, \dots, P_p\}\) and simple directed cycles \(\mathcal{C} = \{C_1, \dots, C_q\}\) with positive weights \(\alpha(P_i) > 0\) and \(\beta(C_j) > 0\) such that:

  1. Arc-wise Decomposition: For every directed arc \(e \in E\),

    \[f(e) = \sum_{i : e \in P_i} \alpha(P_i) + \sum_{j : e \in C_j} \beta(C_j).\]

  2. Value Conservation: The weights of the \(s\)-\(t\) paths sum to the total flow value:

    \[\sum_{i=1}^{p} \alpha(P_i) = |f|.\]

  3. Sparsity Bound: The total number of paths and cycles satisfies

    \[p + q \le |E|.\]

  4. Integrality Preservation: If \(f(e) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) for all \(e \in E\), then every path weight \(\alpha(P_i)\) and cycle weight \(\beta(C_j)\) can be chosen as a positive integer. In particular, an integer flow of value \(|f|\) decomposes into \(|f|\) unit-weight \(s\)-\(t\) paths (possibly repeating) plus integer-weighted directed cycles.

Constructive Proof via Bottleneck Stripping

Define the support of \(f\) as the active arc set \(\operatorname{supp}(f) = \{e \in E : f(e) > 0\}\). We proceed by induction on \(|\operatorname{supp}(f)| \le |E|\).

If \(\operatorname{supp}(f) = \emptyset\), then \(|f| = 0\) and the empty collection \(\mathcal{P} = \emptyset, \mathcal{C} = \emptyset\) satisfies all claims.

Otherwise, pick any active arc \(e_0 = (u_0 \to u_1) \in \operatorname{supp}(f)\) and extend a walk forward \(u_0 \to u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots\) along active arcs whenever possible:

  • Case 1 (Positive net outflow at \(s\), i.e., \(|f| > 0\)): Start the walk at \(u_0 = s\). For every intermediate vertex \(v \notin \{s, t\}\) reached by the walk, Kirchhoff conservation guarantees

    \[\sum_{w} f(v, w) = \sum_{u} f(u, v) \ge f(u_{\text{prev}}, v) > 0,\]

    so \(v\) must possess at least one outgoing active arc \((v \to w)\) with \(f(v, w) > 0\). Thus the walk can never get stuck at an internal vertex \(v \notin \{s, t\}\). Since \(V\) is finite, the walk either:

    • reaches \(t\) without repeating a vertex, yielding a simple directed \(s\)-\(t\) path \(P\), or
    • repeats a vertex \(v\) before reaching \(t\), in which case the sub-walk between the two visits to \(v\) forms a simple directed cycle \(C\).

  • Case 2 (\(|f| = 0\) but \(\operatorname{supp}(f) \neq \emptyset\)): Start from any active arc \((u_0 \to u_1) \in \operatorname{supp}(f)\). Since every vertex now has zero net excess, the walk can never get stuck and must eventually repeat a vertex, extracting a simple directed cycle \(C\).

In either case, we obtain a simple structure \(\Gamma \in \{P, C\}\) (either a simple \(s\)-\(t\) path \(P\) or a simple directed cycle \(C\)) contained entirely in \(\operatorname{supp}(f)\). Define its bottleneck weight:

\[\delta(\Gamma) = \min_{e \in \Gamma} f(e) > 0.\]

Subtract \(\delta(\Gamma)\) from \(f(e)\) for every \(e \in \Gamma\) to obtain a residual flow \(f'\):

\[f'(e) = \begin{cases} f(e) - \delta(\Gamma) & \text{if } e \in \Gamma, \\ f(e) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\]

Observe that:

  1. If \(\Gamma = P\) is an \(s\)-\(t\) path, subtracting \(\delta(P)\) decreases both the inflow and outflow of every internal vertex of \(P\) by \(\delta(P)\), preserves conservation everywhere, and reduces \(|f'|\) to \(|f| - \delta(P)\).

  2. If \(\Gamma = C\) is a directed cycle, subtracting \(\delta(C)\) decreases both the inflow and outflow of every vertex on \(C\) by \(\delta(C)\), leaving \(|f'| = |f|\) unchanged.

  3. Because \(\delta(\Gamma) = \min_{e \in \Gamma} f(e)\), at least one bottleneck arc \(e^* \in \Gamma\) drops to \(f'(e^*) = 0\), strictly decreasing the support size:

    \[|\operatorname{supp}(f')| \le |\operatorname{supp}(f)| - 1.\]

Since \(|\operatorname{supp}(f)| \le |E|\) initially and each step extracts one path or cycle while zeroing out at least one active arc, the process terminates in \(p + q \le |E|\) steps. If \(f\) is integral, \(\delta(\Gamma) = \min_{e \in \Gamma} f(e)\) is an integer at every step, proving (4). \(\blacksquare\)

2.2 Constructive \(O(V E)\) Path & Cycle Stripping Algorithm

First, how do we recover \(f(e)\) from our Dinic adjacency list after termination?

  1. Standard Directed Arc \(e = (u \to v)\) stored at half-edge index 2k (forward) and 2k+1 (reverse, initialized to capacity \(0\)): the net flow pushed along \(e\) is stored directly in the reverse residual capacity:

    \[f(u \to v) = c_f(\text{rev}(e)) = \text{cap}[2k + 1].\]

  2. Compact Undirected Pair \(\{u, v\}\) stored at 2k and 2k+1 (both initialized to capacity \(C\)): as derived in Chapter 1, \(\text{cap}[2k] + \text{cap}[2k+1] = 2C\), and at most one direction carries positive net physical flow:

    \[f_{\text{phys}}(u \to v) = \max(0, C - \text{cap}[2k]), \qquad f_{\text{phys}}(v \to u) = \max(0, C - \text{cap}[2k+1]).\]

Once the active positive flows \(f(e) > 0\) are extracted, we strip \(s\)-\(t\) paths in \(O(V E)\) time by maintaining a stack of visited vertices vis_stack and an on-stack boolean flag on_stack[u]:

  • Walk forward from \(s\) along any active arc \(e = (u \to v)\) with \(f(e) > 0\) (using a cur[u] pointer to skip zeroed arcs).
  • If the walk hits a vertex \(v\) with on_stack[v] == true, pop the cycle \(C\) above \(v\) from the stack, compute \(\delta(C) = \min_{e \in C} f(e)\), subtract \(\delta(C)\) from all \(e \in C\), and resume the walk from \(v\).
  • When the walk reaches \(t\), the stack holds a simple \(s\)-\(t\) path \(P\); subtract \(\delta(P) = \min_{e \in P} f(e)\) and record \((P, \delta(P))\).

Each step advances at most \(V\) times before either hitting \(t\) or closing a cycle, and every path or cycle extraction zeroes at least one arc (or in the unit-capacity case, consumes \(1\) unit of flow per arc in \(O(|E|)\) total time).

NoteShortcut When the Active Support Is Already a DAG

On unit-capacity networks after shortest-path Dinic augmentation, or on Time-Expanded Layered DAGs (分层图) and Shortest-Path DAGs, the active support \(\operatorname{supp}(f^*)\) contains no directed cycles (or any residual cycle can be ignored because \(|f^*|\) paths are guaranteed to reach \(t\)). In that case, a simple DFS from \(s\) that decrements \(f(e) \gets f(e) - 1\) on each traversed arc extracts all \(|f^*|\) unit \(s\)-\(t\) paths in \(O(|f^*| \cdot V)\) time.

2.3 Why Naive Greedy Path Stripping Without Residual Uncrossing Fails

A classic beginner mistake is to confuse decomposing an already-computed maximum flow \(f^*\) (where \(f^*\) was found using reverse residual arcs) with greedily stripping shortest \(s\)-\(t\) paths directly from the original graph \(G\).

WarningBroken-Gadget Counterexample FM-01 — The 6-Vertex Diamond-Bridge Greedy Failure

Suppose we want to find the maximum number of edge-disjoint directed paths from \(s\) to \(t\), and we try the following “Greedy Shortest-Path Stripping” heuristic:

Repeat until \(t\) is unreachable from \(s\): find a shortest \(s\)-\(t\) path \(P\) in \(G\) via BFS, output \(P\), and delete all edges of \(P\) from \(G\).

Why does this fail even when we pick the unique shortest \(s\)-\(t\) path at Step 1? Consider the 6-Vertex Diamond-Bridge Graph \(G = (V, E)\) with \(V = \{s, a, b, u, v, t\}\) and \(7\) unit-capacity directed edges:

  • Top entry and bottom exit of the bridge: \(s \xrightarrow{1} u\) and \(v \xrightarrow{1} t\).
  • Central bridge: \(u \xrightarrow{1} v\).
  • Two length-2 bypasses: \(u \xrightarrow{1} b \xrightarrow{1} t\) (upper right) and \(s \xrightarrow{1} a \xrightarrow{1} v\) (lower left).

Let us trace Naive Greedy BFS vs. Dinic Residual Uncrossing + Flow Decomposition:

  1. Step 1 (Both Algorithms): What are the \(s\)-\(t\) path lengths in \(G\)?
    • \(P_{\text{bridge}} = (s \to u \to v \to t)\) has length \(3\).
    • \(P_{\text{upper}} = (s \to u \to b \to t)\) has length \(3\) (or \(4\) if we subdivide \(b \to b_1 \to b_2\) so \(P_{\text{bridge}}\) is the strictly unique shortest path of length \(3\), while \(P_{\text{upper}}\) and \(P_{\text{lower}}\) have length \(4\)).
    • Even without subdivision, suppose BFS discovers the length-\(3\) path \(P_{\text{bridge}} = (s \to u \to v \to t)\) first.
  2. Naive Greedy Deletion Terminates with \(1\) Path:
    • Greedy deletes the three edges \((s \to u)\), \((u \to v)\), and \((v \to t)\).
    • In the remaining graph \(G \setminus P_{\text{bridge}}\), the only edge leaving \(s\) is \(s \to a \to v\), and the only edge entering \(t\) is \(u \to b \to t\). Vertex \(v\) is a dead end (its only outgoing edge \(v \to t\) was deleted), and vertex \(u\) is unreachable from \(s\) (its only incoming edge \(s \to u\) was deleted).
    • Thus \(t\) is unreachable from \(s\), and Naive Greedy outputs \(1\) path instead of \(2\)!
  3. How Residual Uncrossing + Flow Decomposition Recovers \(2\) Disjoint Paths:

    • After Dinic pushes \(1\) unit along \(s \to u \to v \to t\), the residual graph \(G_f\) activates the reverse residual arc \(v \xrightarrow{1} u\).

    • In Phase 2, Dinic finds the augmenting path

      \[P_{\text{aug}} = (s \to a \to v \xrightarrow{\text{rev}} u \to b \to t).\]

    • Pushing \(1\) unit along \(v \to u\) cancels the flow on the central bridge \(u \to v\) (\(f(u \to v) = 1 - 1 = 0\)), leaving the active support \(\operatorname{supp}(f^*)\) as:

      \[f^*(s \to u) = f^*(u \to b) = f^*(b \to t) = 1, \qquad f^*(s \to a) = f^*(a \to v) = f^*(v \to t) = 1, \qquad f^*(u \to v) = 0.\]

    • Running Flow Decomposition (Theorem 3.1) on \(\operatorname{supp}(f^*)\) immediately extracts the \(2\) edge-disjoint paths \(P_1 = (s \to u \to b \to t)\) and \(P_2 = (s \to a \to v \to t)\)!

Takeaway: Never strip paths greedily before finishing all augmentations. Always compute the full maximum flow \(f^*\) using residual reverse arcs first, and only run Flow Decomposition on the final support \(\operatorname{supp}(f^*)\) after Dinic terminates.

3. Vertex Capacity Splitting (\(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}\)), Multi-Stage Chains, and Menger’s Four Connectivity Theorems

We now apply our 4-Step English-to-Graph Compilation framework to our first major non-arc constraint: vertex throughput and destruction capacities.

3.1 The 4-Step Compilation of Vertex Throughput (\(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_v} v_{\text{out}}\))

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

The problem statement restricts the throughput, durability, or destruction cost of locations / entities (vertices) rather than transitions (edges):

  • “At most \(C_v\) paths, packets, or agents may pass through vertex \(v\).”
  • “Destroying vertex \(v\) costs \(w_v\); find the minimum-cost set of vertices whose removal disconnects \(s\) from \(t\).”
  • “Each entity \(g\) must be matched to one left resource \(a \in \mathcal{A}\) AND one right resource \(b \in \mathcal{B}\), and each \(g\) can be used at most \(C_g = 1\) time.”

Why can’t we simply place capacity \(C_v\) on all incoming edges \((u \to v)\) or all outgoing edges \((v \to w)\)? Because if \(v\) has \(\deg^-(v) = 2\) incoming edges and \(\deg^+(v) = 2\) outgoing edges, giving each incident edge capacity \(C_v = 1\) still allows \(2\) units of flow to pass through \(v\) in parallel!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

In a standard edge-capacitated flow network, Kirchhoff conservation at any internal vertex \(v \notin \{s, t\}\) states that the total throughput \(F(v)\) traversing \(v\) is equal to both its total inflow and its total outflow:

\[F(v) := \sum_{e \in \delta^-(v)} f(e) = \sum_{e \in \delta^+(v)} f(e).\]

A vertex capacity constraint requires that for every \(v \in V\),

\[0 \le F(v) \le C_v.\]

To enforce \(0 \le F(v) \le C_v\) using a solver that only understands arc capacities \(0 \le f(e) \le c(e)\), we must introduce a single dedicated arc \(e_v\) whose flow \(f(e_v)\) is algebraically forced by Kirchhoff’s law to equal the entire sum \(F(v) = \sum_{e \in \delta^-(v)} f(e)\).

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

How do we force every unit of flow entering \(v\) to cross a single dedicated arc \(e_v\) before leaving \(v\)? We separate the arrival event at \(v\) from the departure event from \(v\) by splitting each vertex \(v \in V\) into two half-vertices:

  • an in-port \(v_{\text{in}}\) that collects all incoming flow \(\delta^-(v)\), and
  • an out-port \(v_{\text{out}}\) that distributes all outgoing flow \(\delta^+(v)\),

connected by a single directed internal bridge arc:

\[e_v = (v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}), \qquad c(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}) = C_v.\]

Now let us check how original transitions must be wired:

  1. The Strict \(\text{out} \to \text{in}\) Port Invariant: Every original directed edge \(e = (u \to v)\) with edge capacity \(c(u, v)\) represents a departure from \(u\) followed by an arrival at \(v\). Therefore, it must leave \(u\)’s out-port and enter \(v\)’s in-port:

    \[(u \to v) \quad\longmapsto\quad \big(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{c(u, v)} v_{\text{in}}\big).\]

    If original edges have no capacity limit of their own (pure vertex-cut problems), set \(c(u_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}}) = \infty\).

  2. Proof of Algebraic Equivalence: Because the only outgoing arc from \(v_{\text{in}}\) is the internal bridge \((v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}})\), Kirchhoff conservation at \(v_{\text{in}}\) forces:

    \[f(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}) = \sum_{u : (u, v) \in E} f(u_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}}) = F(v).\]

    Similarly, because the only incoming arc to \(v_{\text{out}}\) is \((v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}})\), conservation at \(v_{\text{out}}\) forces \(\sum_{w} f(v_{\text{out}} \to w_{\text{in}}) = f(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}) = F(v)\). Thus the arc capacity \(0 \le f(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}) \le C_v\) is 100% equivalent to the vertex throughput constraint \(0 \le F(v) \le C_v\)!

  3. Source and Sink Conventions:

    • If the source \(s\) and sink \(t\) cannot be destroyed / have infinite vertex capacity (\(C_s = C_t = \infty\)), we can either set \(c(s_{\text{in}} \to s_{\text{out}}) = c(t_{\text{in}} \to t_{\text{out}}) = \infty\) and route from \(s_{\text{in}}\) to \(t_{\text{out}}\), or simply route directly from \(s_{\text{out}}\) to \(t_{\text{in}}\).
    • If \(s\) and \(t\) do have finite vertex capacities \(C_s, C_t < \infty\), route from \(s_{\text{in}}\) to \(t_{\text{out}}\) so that all flow must cross both \(s_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_s} s_{\text{out}}\) and \(t_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_t} t_{\text{out}}\).

Exact Cut Bijection & Recovering the Minimum Vertex Cut from \(S_{\min}\)

Suppose every original edge \((u \to v)\) is compiled to \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} v_{\text{in}}\) and each vertex \(v \notin \{s, t\}\) has internal bridge \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_v} v_{\text{out}}\). Let \((S, \bar{S})\) be any finite-capacity \(s_{\text{out}}\)-\(t_{\text{in}}\) cut in the split network \(G_{\text{split}}\).

  • Because every transition arc \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} v_{\text{in}}\) has infinite capacity, a finite cut \((S, \bar{S})\) can never satisfy \(u_{\text{out}} \in S \land v_{\text{in}} \in \bar{S}\).

  • Consequently, the only arcs crossing from \(S\) to \(\bar{S}\) are internal vertex bridges \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_v} v_{\text{out}}\) where

    \[v_{\text{in}} \in S \quad\text{and}\quad v_{\text{out}} \in \bar{S}.\]

  • Define the cut vertex set \(U(S) := \{v \in V \setminus \{s, t\} : v_{\text{in}} \in S \text{ and } v_{\text{out}} \in \bar{S}\}\). Along any original \(s\)-\(t\) path \(s = v_0 \to v_1 \to \dots \to v_k = t\), we have \((v_0)_{\text{out}} = s_{\text{out}} \in S\) and \((v_k)_{\text{in}} = t_{\text{in}} \in \bar{S}\). Since no transition arc \((v_i)_{\text{out}} \to (v_{i+1})_{\text{in}}\) can cross from \(S\) to \(\bar{S}\) (otherwise the cut capacity would be \(\infty\)), there must be at least one index \(i \in \{1, \dots, k-1\}\) where \((v_i)_{\text{in}} \in S\) and \((v_i)_{\text{out}} \in \bar{S}\), meaning \(v_i \in U(S)\)!

Conversely, any vertex cut \(U \subseteq V \setminus \{s, t\}\) induces a valid finite cut \((S_U, \bar{S}_U)\) of capacity \(\sum_{v \in U} C_v\) by placing in \(S_U\) all half-vertices reachable from \(s_{\text{out}}\) after deleting \(\{v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}} : v \in U\}\).

TipRecovering the Canonical Source-Minimal Vertex Cut

After running Dinic from \(s_{\text{out}}\) to \(t_{\text{in}}\), let \(S_{\min}\) be the set of half-vertices reachable from \(s_{\text{out}}\) in the final residual graph \(G_{f^*}\). Then the canonical source-minimal minimum vertex cut is:

\[U_{\min} = \big\{v \in V \setminus \{s, t\} \;\big|\; v_{\text{in}} \in S_{\min} \;\land\; v_{\text{out}} \notin S_{\min}\big\}.\]

3.2 Step 4: Broken-Gadget Counterexample FM-04 — The Undirected Vertex-Split Bypass Trap

What happens when the underlying graph \(G = (V, E)\) is undirected and vertices have capacities \(C_v\)?

In Section 5.2 of Wave 1A (and Chapter 1), we saw that on an edge-capacitated undirected graph, an undirected edge \(\{u, v\}\) of capacity \(C\) can be represented by a single compact pair of half-edges (2k, 2k+1) between \(u\) and \(v\) with \(\text{cap}[2k] = \text{cap}[2k+1] = C\).

When vertices are split into \((u_{\text{in}}, u_{\text{out}})\) and \((v_{\text{in}}, v_{\text{out}})\), one of the deadliest bugs in competitive programming is trying to connect \(u\) and \(v\) with a single undirected pair (such as \(u_{\text{in}} \leftrightarrow v_{\text{in}}\), \(u_{\text{out}} \leftrightarrow v_{\text{out}}\), or \(u_{\text{out}} \leftrightarrow v_{\text{in}}\))!

CautionBroken-Gadget Counterexample FM-04 — Why Undirected Edges on Split Vertices Require TWO Directed Arcs (\(u_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}}\) AND \(v_{\text{out}} \to u_{\text{in}}\))

Consider a 4-vertex undirected path \(s - u - v - t\) where internal vertices \(u\) and \(v\) each have vertex capacity \(C_u = C_v = 1\), and edges have capacity \(\infty\). Obviously, at most \(1\) unit of flow can travel from \(s\) to \(t\).

Now watch how three common mis-wirings of the undirected edge \(\{u, v\}\) catastrophically fail:

  1. Bug A (Connecting in-ports: \(u_{\text{in}} \leftrightarrow v_{\text{in}}\) with cap \(\infty\)): Flow travels \(s_{\text{out}} \to u_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} v_{\text{out}} \to t_{\text{in}}\). Notice that flow arrived at \(u_{\text{in}}\) and immediately jumped to \(v_{\text{in}}\) without ever crossing the internal bridge \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} u_{\text{out}}\)! Vertex \(u\)’s capacity constraint is completely bypassed (\(C_u\) might as well be \(\infty\)).

  2. Bug B (Using a bidirectional undirected edge between \(u_{\text{out}}\) and \(v_{\text{in}}\): \(u_{\text{out}} \leftrightarrow v_{\text{in}}\) with cap \(\infty\) in both directions): Suppose we also have edges \(s - v\) and \(u - t\), and we wire \(\{u, v\}\) as an undirected edge \(u_{\text{out}} \leftrightarrow v_{\text{in}}\) (so both \(u_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}}\) and \(v_{\text{in}} \to u_{\text{out}}\) have initial capacity \(\infty\)). Then flow can travel:

    \[s_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{\text{via } v_{\text{in}} \to u_{\text{out}}} u_{\text{out}} \to t_{\text{in}}!\]

    This path carries \(\infty\) flow from \(s\) to \(t\) while bypassing BOTH \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} v_{\text{out}}\) AND \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} u_{\text{out}}\), yielding MaxFlow \(= \infty\) when the true answer is \(2\)!

The Ironclad Rule for Undirected Edges on Split Vertices: Every undirected edge \(\{u, v\}\) of capacity \(c_{uv}\) on a vertex-split graph must be compiled into two independent directed arcs (4 half-edges total in your adjacency list):

  1. Forward transition \(u \to v\): add_edge(u_out, v_in, c_uv) (whose reverse residual arc v_in -> u_out starts with capacity \(0\), so it can only cancel previously committed flow from u_out to v_in).
  2. Backward transition \(v \to u\): add_edge(v_out, u_in, c_uv) (whose reverse residual arc u_in -> v_out starts with capacity \(0\)).

Never violate the \(\text{out} \to \text{in}\) port invariant: every physical transition arc in \(G_{\text{split}}\) must originate at an out-port and terminate at an in-port.

3.3 Menger’s Four Connectivity Theorems (1927) & The \(O(E \sqrt{V})\) Unit-Vertex Regime

By combining the Integrality Theorem, the Flow Decomposition Theorem (Theorem 3.1), and Vertex Capacity Splitting (§3.1), Ford–Fulkerson duality immediately yields all four classical connectivity theorems proved by Karl Menger in 1927:

ImportantTheorem 3.2 (Menger’s Four Connectivity Theorems — Menger, 1927)

Let \(G = (V, E)\) be a directed or undirected graph and let \(s, t \in V\) be two distinct vertices.

  1. Directed Edge-Connectivity (\(\lambda_{\text{dir}}(s, t)\)): The maximum number of arc-disjoint directed \(s\)-\(t\) paths equals the minimum number of directed arcs whose removal destroys all directed \(s\)-\(t\) paths.
  2. Undirected Edge-Connectivity (\(\lambda_{\text{undir}}(s, t)\)): The maximum number of edge-disjoint undirected \(s\)-\(t\) paths equals the minimum number of undirected edges whose removal disconnects \(s\) and \(t\).
  3. Directed Vertex-Connectivity (\(\kappa_{\text{dir}}(s, t)\), assuming \((s \to t) \notin E\)): The maximum number of internally vertex-disjoint directed \(s\)-\(t\) paths equals the minimum number of vertices in \(V \setminus \{s, t\}\) whose removal destroys all directed \(s\)-\(t\) paths.
  4. Undirected Vertex-Connectivity (\(\kappa_{\text{undir}}(s, t)\), assuming \(\{s, t\} \notin E\)): The maximum number of internally vertex-disjoint undirected \(s\)-\(t\) paths equals the minimum number of vertices in \(V \setminus \{s, t\}\) whose removal disconnects \(s\) and \(t\).

3.4 Multi-Stage Chains: Compiling Three-Way Coupling (\(A \to V_{\text{in}} \to V_{\text{out}} \to B\))

In bipartite matching, an edge \((a, b) \in \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{B}\) couples two types of entities. What if each agent \(v \in \mathcal{V}\) must simultaneously consume one resource \(a \in \mathcal{A}_v\) from Stage 1 AND one resource \(b \in \mathcal{B}_v\) from Stage 2?

Examples include:

  • [Luogu P1402] 酒店之王 (#017): Guest \(g\) needs \(1\) liked Room \(r \in \mathcal{R}_g\) and \(1\) liked Dish \(d \in \mathcal{D}_g\).
  • [USACO07OPEN / Luogu P2891] Dining: Cow \(i\) needs \(1\) liked Food \(f \in \mathcal{F}_i\) and \(1\) liked Drink \(d \in \mathcal{D}_i\).

Why can’t we put the agents on the left (\(S \to g\)) and connect \(g \to r \to T\) and \(g \to d \to T\) in parallel? Because then a guest could send \(1\) unit of flow to a room and \(0\) units to a dish (or \(2\) units to rooms and \(0\) to dishes)! To force a logical AND (1 Room AND 1 Dish per unit of flow), the Room and the Dish must lie in series along the same \(s\)-\(t\) path, with the Guest \(g\) placed in the middle as the bridge between them:

\[S \xrightarrow{1} r \xrightarrow{1} g_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} g_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{1} d \xrightarrow{1} T.\]

  • Because \(r\) and \(d\) are in series, every unit of \(s\)-\(t\) flow simultaneously saturates one room \(r\), one guest \(g\), and one dish \(d\).
  • Because \(g\) is split into \(g_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} g_{\text{out}}\), no single guest can absorb two room-dish pairs!

4. Time-Expanded Layered Networks (分层图) & Dynamic Layer Addition (动态加层)

4.1 The 4-Step Compilation of Discrete Time Horizons

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal

Agents move across a physical graph \(G = (V, E)\) over discrete time steps \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots, T\):

  • Traversing an edge \(e = (u \to v)\) takes \(\tau_e \ge 1\) seconds and admits at most \(c_e\) agents per step.
  • Agents can wait (hold inventory) at vertex \(v\) from time \(t\) to \(t+1\) (up to capacity \(h_v\), typically \(\infty\)).
  • The available edges or their endpoints may vary with \(t\) (e.g., periodic shuttles moving between space stations in [CTSC1999] 家园).
  • We want either the maximum number of agents delivered by a fixed deadline \(T\), or the minimum time \(T^*\) required to deliver \(K\) agents.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Because flow conservation in a static graph has no memory of when flow arrived at \(v\), we must promote time \(t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T\}\) into the vertex index itself. Let \(f_{\text{hold}}(v, t)\) denote the number of agents waiting at location \(v\) across the interval \([t, t+1]\), and let \(f_{\text{trans}}(e, t)\) denote the number of agents departing along transition \(e = (u \to v)\) at time \(t\) (arriving at \(v\) at time \(t + \tau_e\)).

At every spacetime coordinate \((v, t) \in V \times \{0, 1, \dots, T\}\), spacetime Kirchhoff conservation requires:

\[\underbrace{f_{\text{hold}}(v, t-1)}_{\text{waited at } v} + \sum_{e=(u \to v)} \underbrace{f_{\text{trans}}(e, t - \tau_e)}_{\text{arrived at } v \text{ at time } t} = \underbrace{f_{\text{hold}}(v, t)}_{\text{stays at } v} + \sum_{e=(v \to w)} \underbrace{f_{\text{trans}}(e, t)}_{\text{departs } v \text{ at time } t}.\]

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

This spacetime balance equation compiles directly into a Time-Expanded Layered DAG (分层图) \(G_T = (V_T, E_T)\):

  1. Spacetime Vertices: For each location \(v \in V\) and each discrete timestamp \(t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T\}\), create a state vertex \((v, t)\). Add a super-source \(S\) and super-sink \(T_{\text{sink}}\).

  2. Initial Supply (\(t = 0\)): If \(a_v\) agents start at location \(v\) at \(t = 0\), add arc \(S \xrightarrow{a_v} (v, 0)\).

  3. Holdover / Waiting Arcs (滞留边): For each \(v \in V\) and \(0 \le t < T\), add a directed arc representing agents waiting at \(v\) from time \(t\) to \(t+1\):

    \[(v, t) \xrightarrow{h_v} (v, t+1) \qquad (\text{usually } h_v = \infty).\]

  4. Transit Arcs (转移边): Whenever a road or shuttle allows up to \(c_e\) agents to depart \(u\) at time \(t\) and arrive at \(v\) at time \(t + \tau_e \le T\), add:

    \[(u, t) \xrightarrow{c_e} (v, t + \tau_e).\]

  5. Destination Sink Collection: If \(v_{\text{dest}}\) is the target location, connect \((v_{\text{dest}}, t) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\) for every \(t \in \{0, 1, \dots, T\}\) (or simply connect \((v_{\text{dest}}, T) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\) if holdover arcs at \(v_{\text{dest}}\) have capacity \(\infty\)).

WarningStep 4: Broken-Gadget Guard — Omitting Holdover Arcs (FM-03)

If you forget to add the holdover arcs \((u, t) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, t+1)\), an agent arriving at station \(u\) at time \(t = 1\) is forced to immediately board an outgoing shuttle at \(t = 1\) or vanish! Whenever agents are allowed to wait at vertices for later connections, holdover arcs \((u, t) \to (u, t+1)\) are mandatory. Furthermore, if vertices also have a simultaneous occupancy limit at time \(t\), split \((u, t)\) into \((u, t)_{\text{in}} \to (u, t)_{\text{out}}\).

4.2 Incremental Dynamic Layer Addition (动态加层) vs. Binary Search on \(T\)

Suppose a problem asks for the minimum time \(T^*\) needed to route \(K\) agents from \(s\) to \(t\) (as in [CTSC1999] 家园 / 星际转移问题, #020).

A naive approach binary searches \(T \in [1, T_{\max}]\) and rebuilds the entire \(T\)-layer graph from scratch at each midpoint, costing \(O(\log T_{\max})\) full Dinic executions.

Instead, notice that adding layer \(t\) to \(G_{t-1}\) only appends new vertices \(V \times \{t\}\) and new forward arcs from layer \(t-1\) to layer \(t\) (and from \((v_{\text{dest}}, t)\) to \(T_{\text{sink}}\)). Every flow pushed in \(G_{t-1}\) remains a valid feasible flow in \(G_t\)!

TipAlgorithmic Invariant — Incremental Dynamic Layer Addition (动态加层)

Maintain a single persistent residual network \(G_f\) and a running scalar total_flow = 0:

  1. Pre-Check Reachability: Before unrolling time, use Disjoint Set Union (DSU) or BFS on the static union of all transitions to verify that \(v_{\text{dest}}\) is reachable from \(v_{\text{start}}\). If not, immediately return Impossible (preventing an infinite loop!).
  2. Incremental Unrolling (\(t = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)):
    • Allocate new vertex IDs for \((u, t)\) (\(u \in V\)).
    • Add holdover arcs \((u, t-1) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, t)\), transit arcs \((u, t-1) \xrightarrow{c_e} (v, t)\), and sink collection \((v_{\text{dest}}, t) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\) directly into the existing adjacency list of \(G_f\).
    • Execute total_flow += dinic(S, T_sink) on the existing residual graph \(G_f\).
    • Stop at the first timestamp \(t = T^*\) where total_flow >= K.

Because previous flow is preserved in \(G_f\) and reverse residual arcs in layers \(0 \dots t-1\) allow Dinic to seamlessly reroute earlier trajectories to exploit newly opened shuttles at time \(t\), the total work across all layers \(t = 1 \dots T^*\) is roughly a single Dinic run on \(G_{T^*}\)!

5. DP-Subgraph Layering (Luogu P2766 LIS) & Hub Sparsification (ARC 074 D)

5.1 Packing Disjoint Optimal Trajectories on Shortest-Path & DP DAGs

Many problems ask: “How many mutually disjoint optimal paths (or optimal subsequences) can be packed simultaneously?”

  1. Shortest-Path DAG Extraction ([CQOI2015] 网络吞吐量, #024): Suppose packets from \(1\) to \(N\) may only travel along shortest paths with respect to edge weights \(w_e > 0\), while vertices have throughput capacities \(C_v\).

    • First, run Dijkstra from vertex \(1\) to compute shortest-path distances \(d[1 \dots N]\).

    • An edge \((u \to v)\) of weight \(w_{uv}\) belongs to at least one shortest path from \(1\) if and only if it is tight:

      \[d[u] + w_{uv} = d[v].\]

    • Because \(w_{uv} > 0\), the subgraph \(E_{\text{DAG}} = \{(u \to v) : d[u] + w_{uv} = d[v]\}\) is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)! Combining \(E_{\text{DAG}}\) with vertex splitting \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_v} v_{\text{out}}\) immediately compiles the problem into standard Max-Flow.

  2. DP Layering ([Luogu P2766] 最长不下降子序列问题, #021): Given an array \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), let \(dp[i]\) be the length of the longest non-decreasing subsequence (LNDS) ending at index \(i\), and let \(s = \max_{1 \le i \le n} dp[i]\) be the global LNDS length. We want the maximum number of index-disjoint non-decreasing subsequences of length \(s\).

    • Notice that \(dp[i] \in \{1, 2, \dots, s\}\) naturally partitions the indices \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(s\) sequential DP layers!

    • A pair \((i, j)\) can serve as consecutive elements of a valid LNDS if and only if:

      \[i < j, \qquad x_i \le x_j, \qquad \text{and} \qquad dp[j] = dp[i] + 1.\]

    • Every directed path from a layer-\(1\) vertex (\(dp[i] = 1\)) to a layer-\(s\) vertex (\(dp[i] = s\)) along these admissible transitions has length exactly \(s\) and forms a valid LNDS of maximum length!

    • Splitting each index \(i\) into \(i_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} i_{\text{out}}\) and wiring \(S \xrightarrow{1} i_{\text{in}}\) (for \(dp[i]=1\)), \(i_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{1} j_{\text{in}}\) (for admissible transitions), and \(i_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{1} T\) (for \(dp[i]=s\)) reduces disjoint LNDS packing to unit-vertex-network Max-Flow in \(O(E \sqrt{V})\) time!

5.2 Row/Column Hub Sparsification ([AtCoder ARC 074 D] Lotus Leaves)

Suppose a frog on an \(H \times W\) pond (\(H, W \le 100\)) can jump between any two lotus leaves in the same row or the same column. Destroying a lotus leaf at \((r, c)\) costs \(1\), except for the start leaf \(S\) and target leaf \(T\), which cannot be destroyed. Find the minimum number of leaves to remove to disconnect \(S\) from \(T\).

Why Naive Vertex Splitting Is Too Dense—and How Hub Vertices Collapse the Graph

If we split every lotus leaf \((r, c)\) into \((r, c)_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} (r, c)_{\text{out}}\) and add directed edges between all pairs of leaves in the same row or column, each row of length \(W\) induces a directed clique of \(W(W-1)\) edges, creating \(\Theta(H W (H + W))\) edges (\(\approx 2 \times 10^6\) edges).

We can sparsify this by introducing auxiliary Hub Vertices (中介点 / 行列虚点):

  1. Step 1 (Introduce Row and Column Hubs): Create \(H\) Row Hubs \(R_1, \dots, R_H\) and \(W\) Column Hubs \(C_1, \dots, C_W\). Since rows and columns are abstract geometric lines that cannot themselves be destroyed, their vertex capacity is \(\infty\).

  2. Step 2 (Route Leaves Through Hubs): Instead of connecting leaf \((r, c)\) directly to all other leaves in row \(r\) and column \(c\), connect \((r, c)\) to its Row Hub \(R_r\) and Column Hub \(C_c\):

    \[(r, c)_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} R_r \xrightarrow{\infty} (r, c)_{\text{in}}, \qquad (r, c)_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} C_c \xrightarrow{\infty} (r, c)_{\text{in}}.\]

    Now any jump from \((r, c_1)\) to \((r, c_2)\) simply travels \((r, c_1)_{\text{out}} \to R_r \to (r, c_2)_{\text{in}}\) in two hops! The edge count drops from \(\Theta(HW(H+W))\) to \(4HW\).

  3. Step 3 (The Ultimate Bipartite Collapse — Eliminating Cell Vertices Entirely!): Look closely at a lotus leaf \((r, c)\) in the hub graph. Why would a frog ever visit \((r, c)\)? Only to switch from Row \(r\) to Column \(c\), or from Column \(c\) to Row \(r\)! (Jumping from Row \(r\) to \((r, c)\) and back to Row \(r\) is redundant since the frog was already at \(R_r\)). Therefore, each destroyable leaf \((r, c)\) is nothing more than an undirected bridge of capacity \(1\) between Row Hub \(R_r\) and Column Hub \(C_c\):

    \[R_r \xleftrightarrow{\text{cap } = 1} C_c!\]

    • For the start leaf \((r_S, c_S)\), connect \(S \xrightarrow{\infty} R_{r_S}\) and \(S \xrightarrow{\infty} C_{c_S}\).
    • For the target leaf \((r_T, c_T)\), connect \(R_{r_T} \xrightarrow{\infty} T\) and \(C_{c_T} \xrightarrow{\infty} T\).
    • For every other leaf \((r, c)\), add an undirected edge of capacity \(1\) between \(R_r\) and \(C_c\).

This algebraic simplification collapses a \(2HW\)-vertex, \(HW(H+W)\)-edge vertex-cut network into a \(200\)-vertex, \(10^4\)-edge unit-capacity bipartite graph, solving [ARC 074 D] in under \(1\) millisecond in \(O(H W \sqrt{H + W})\) time!

#017 [Stepping Stone 1] — [Luogu P1402] 酒店之王 (King of the Hotel)

  • Judge URL: https://www.luogu.com.cn/problem/P1402 ([ZH], practically identical to [USACO07OPEN / Luogu P2891] Dining [EN])
  • Constraints: \(N \le 100\) guests, \(P \le 100\) rooms, \(Q \le 100\) dishes. Time Limit: \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: A hotel has \(N\) guests, \(P\) rooms, and \(Q\) signature dishes. Each room can accommodate at most \(1\) guest, and each dish can be served to at most \(1\) guest. You are given two boolean preference matrices: \(A_{i, r} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicating whether guest \(i\) likes room \(r\), and \(B_{i, d} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicating whether guest \(i\) likes dish \(d\). A guest is satisfied if and only if they receive both one room they like and one dish they like. Compute the maximum number of guests that can be simultaneously satisfied.
NoteHint 1 — Why Parallel Matching Fails and Why Rooms & Dishes Must Be in Series

Suppose we connect \(S \xrightarrow{2} \text{Guest}_i\), and from \(\text{Guest}_i\) we add edges to liked rooms \(r \to T\) and liked dishes \(d \to T\). Why does this fail? Because a guest could push \(2\) units of flow into two rooms and \(0\) dishes! To force \(1\) room AND \(1\) dish per unit of flow, every \(s\)-\(t\) path must traverse a room first and a dish second, with the guest acting as the intermediate bridge between them:

\[S \to \text{Room}_r \to \text{Guest}_i \to \text{Dish}_d \to T.\]

NoteHint 2 — The Unsplit Central Entity Bug

If you place a single unsplit vertex \(\text{Guest}_i\) between \(\text{Room}_r\) and \(\text{Dish}_d\) with all edge capacities equal to \(1\), what happens when Guest \(1\) likes two rooms \(\{r_1, r_2\}\) and two dishes \(\{d_1, d_2\}\)? Two units of flow can pass through \(\text{Guest}_1\) simultaneously (\(S \to r_1 \to g_1 \to d_1 \to T\) and \(S \to r_2 \to g_1 \to d_2 \to T\)), counting Guest \(1\) twice! How do you cap the total throughput of \(\text{Guest}_i\) at \(1\)?

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — [Luogu P1402] 酒店之王

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

We have a three-way matching \((\text{Room } r, \text{Guest } i, \text{Dish } d)\) with \(N, P, Q \le 100\). Unlike general 3D Matching (which is NP-hard when preferences are arbitrary triples \((r, i, d)\)), here the preference factors independently into \((r, i)\) and \((i, d)\) with Guest \(i\) as the central hinge. Greedy matching of rooms first can assign a room to a guest who has no available dish left, while residual augmentation can reassign rooms and dishes simultaneously.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(x_{r, i} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether room \(r\) is assigned to guest \(i\) (allowed only if \(A_{i, r} = 1\)), and \(y_{i, d} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether dish \(d\) is assigned to guest \(i\) (allowed only if \(B_{i, d} = 1\)). Let \(F_i \in \{0, 1\}\) be the indicator that guest \(i\) is satisfied. The constraints are:

\[\sum_{i=1}^N x_{r, i} \le 1 \quad (\forall r), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^N y_{i, d} \le 1 \quad (\forall d),\]

\[\sum_{r=1}^P x_{r, i} = F_i = \sum_{d=1}^Q y_{i, d}, \qquad 0 \le F_i \le 1 \quad (\forall i).\]

Notice that \(\sum_r x_{r,i} = F_i = \sum_d y_{i,d}\) is literally Kirchhoff flow conservation at Guest \(i\), and \(0 \le F_i \le 1\) is a vertex throughput capacity of \(1\) on Guest \(i\)!

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

We construct a 4-layer unit-capacity network with \(|V| = 2 + P + 2N + Q\) vertices:

  1. Stage 1 (Rooms): For each room \(r \in \{1, \dots, P\}\), add \(S \xrightarrow{1} r\) (enforcing \(\sum_i x_{r,i} \le 1\)).
  2. Stage 2 (Split Guests): Split each guest \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) into \(i_{\text{in}}\) and \(i_{\text{out}}\) connected by internal bridge \(i_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} i_{\text{out}}\) (enforcing \(F_i \le 1\)).
  3. Room-to-Guest Transitions: For each \((i, r)\) with \(A_{i, r} = 1\), add \(r \xrightarrow{1} i_{\text{in}}\).
  4. Guest-to-Dish Transitions: For each \((i, d)\) with \(B_{i, d} = 1\), add \(i_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{1} d\).
  5. Stage 3 (Dishes): For each dish \(d \in \{1, \dots, Q\}\), add \(d \xrightarrow{1} T\) (enforcing \(\sum_i y_{i,d} \le 1\)).

Step 4: Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

Suppose \(N = 1\) guest, \(P = 2\) rooms \(\{r_1, r_2\}\), \(Q = 2\) dishes \(\{d_1, d_2\}\), and Guest \(1\) likes everything (\(A_{1,1}=A_{1,2}=B_{1,1}=B_{1,2}=1\)).

  • With \(1_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} 1_{\text{out}}\): Both \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) send capacity \(1\) into \(1_{\text{in}}\), but the bridge \(1_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} 1_{\text{out}}\) bottlenecks the total flow to \(1\) (correct).
  • Without splitting (Failure Guard): Flow \(S \to r_1 \to 1 \to d_1 \to T\) (\(1\) unit) and \(S \to r_2 \to 1 \to d_2 \to T\) (\(1\) unit) both pass, giving \(2\) satisfied guests when only \(1\) guest exists!

Solver Contract: \(|V| = P + 2N + Q + 2 \le 402\), \(|E| = P + Q + N + NP + NQ \le 20300\). Every internal vertex has either in-degree \(1\) or out-degree \(1\) with unit capacities, so Dinic runs in \(O(E \sqrt{V}) \approx 4 \times 10^5\) operations.

#018 [Stepping Stone 2] — [UVa 11380] Down Went The Titanic

  • Judge URL: https://onlinejudge.org/external/113/11380.pdf ([EN])
  • Constraints: Grid dimensions \(X, Y \le 30\), lifeboat capacity \(P \le 1000\). Time Limit: \(3.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: After the Titanic strikes an iceberg, survivors are scattered on an \(X \times Y\) grid with 5 cell types:
    • ~ (Water): Cold and impassable; no one can enter.
    • . (Floating Ice): Thin ice that vanishes after \(1\) person steps off it.
    • * (Person on Floating Ice): Contains \(1\) survivor initially, resting on thin . ice (so the cell itself also vanishes as soon as that survivor steps off it; no other survivor may pass through it!).
    • @ (Large Iceberg): Thick ice; infinitely many people may step on and off it.
    • # (Lifeboat): Thick ice (infinite pass-through capacity), and up to \(P\) people can permanently board the lifeboat at this cell to be rescued. People move 4-directionally (N, S, E, W). Compute the maximum number of survivors who can reach a lifeboat.
NoteHint 1 — Classifying Cell Durability as Vertex Capacities

Every cell \((r, c)\) restricts how many times people can pass through it—a pure vertex capacity \(C_{r, c}\):

  • ~: \(C_{r, c} = 0\) (skip).
  • . and *: \(C_{r, c} = 1\) (thin ice collapses after 1 person leaves).
  • @ and #: \(C_{r, c} = \infty\) (never collapses).
NoteHint 2 — Where Should \(S\) Connect for a * Cell: \((r, c)_{\text{in}}\) or \((r, c)_{\text{out}}\)?

A survivor starting at a * cell is standing on thin ice of capacity \(1\). If you connect \(S \xrightarrow{1} (r, c)_{\text{out}}\), then the starting survivor bypasses the internal bridge \((r, c)_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} (r, c)_{\text{out}}\), allowing a second survivor from an adjacent cell to enter \((r, c)_{\text{in}}\), cross the bridge, and also use the * cell! Which port must \(S\) connect to so that the starting survivor immediately consumes the * cell’s capacity \(1\)?

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — [UVa 11380] Down Went The Titanic

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Survivors move along grid paths to # cells, constrained by cell collapse limits (\(1\) for . and *, \(\infty\) for @ and #) and lifeboat boarding limits (\(P\) per # cell). Because multiple survivors’ paths compete for shared thin-ice cells and lifeboats, and \(X, Y \le 30\) (\(XY \le 900\)), this is a vertex-capacitated multi-source multi-sink max-flow problem.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

For each non-water cell \(u = (r, c)\), let \(F(u)\) be the total number of survivors who ever occupy and depart (or get rescued at) cell \(u\). Then:

\[F(u) = \mathbb{I}[u \text{ is } \text{'*'}] + \sum_{v \in N_4(u)} f(v \to u) = \text{rescued}(u) + \sum_{w \in N_4(u)} f(u \to w),\]

subject to the vertex durability constraint:

\[0 \le F(u) \le C_u, \qquad C_u = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } grid[u] \in \{\text{'.'}, \text{'*'}\}, \\ \infty & \text{if } grid[u] \in \{\text{'@'}, \text{'\#'}\}, \end{cases}\]

and the lifeboat boarding constraint \(0 \le \text{rescued}(u) \le P\) if \(grid[u] = \text{'\#'}\) (and \(0\) otherwise).

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

Split every non-water cell \(u = (r, c)\) into \(u_{\text{in}}\) and \(u_{\text{out}}\):

  1. Internal Cell Durability Bridge: Add \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_u} u_{\text{out}}\) where \(C_u = 1\) for . and *, and \(C_u = \infty\) for @ and #.
  2. Initial Survivors (*): If \(grid[u] = \text{'*'}\), add \(S \xrightarrow{1} u_{\text{in}}\). (By entering \(u_{\text{in}}\), the survivor starting at \(u\) immediately saturates \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} u_{\text{out}}\), blocking any other survivor from stepping onto \(u\)!)
  3. Lifeboat Boarding (#): If \(grid[u] = \text{'\#'}\), add \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{P} T\). (Note that because \(C_u = \infty\) for #, any number of survivors can walk through a # cell to reach other cells, while at most \(P\) can exit to \(T\) at this lifeboat.)
  4. 4-Directional Grid Transitions: For every pair of 4-adjacent non-water cells \((u, v)\), add two directed arcs \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} v_{\text{in}}\) and \(v_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} u_{\text{in}}\).

Step 4: Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

Consider a \(1 \times 3\) row * * # with \(P = 2\):

  • Cell \(1\) (*) has \(S \xrightarrow{1} 1_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} 1_{\text{out}}\).
  • Cell \(2\) (*) has \(S \xrightarrow{1} 2_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} 2_{\text{out}}\).
  • Because \(2_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} 2_{\text{out}}\) has capacity \(1\) and is already saturated by Survivor \(2\), Survivor \(1\) at \(1_{\text{out}} \to 2_{\text{in}}\) cannot pass through Cell \(2\)! Total rescued \(= 1\).
  • Failure Guard: If we had wired \(S \xrightarrow{1} 2_{\text{out}}\) instead of \(2_{\text{in}}\), Survivor \(2\) would leave from \(2_{\text{out}} \to 3_{\text{in}}\) while Survivor \(1\) simultaneously traversed \(1_{\text{out}} \to 2_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{1} 2_{\text{out}} \to 3_{\text{in}}\), falsely rescuing \(2\) people across a thin-ice cell that can only support \(1\)!

Solver Contract: \(|V| \le 2XY + 2 \le 1802\), \(|E| \le 6XY \le 5400\). Dinic runs in \(O((XY)^{3/2}) \approx 8 \times 10^4\) operations per testcase.

#019 [Intermediate 1] — [SCOI2007 / Luogu P2472] 蜥蜴 (Lizards)

  • Judge URL: https://www.luogu.com.cn/problem/P2472 ([ZH])
  • Constraints: Grid \(R, C \le 20\), jump distance \(d \le 4\), pillar heights \(h_{r, c} \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}\). Time Limit: \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: In an \(R \times C\) maze, each cell \((r, c)\) (\(1 \le r \le R, 1 \le c \le C\)) has a stone pillar of height \(h_{r, c} \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}\) (\(0\) means no pillar). Some pillars initially have a single lizard standing on them. In one jump, a lizard can leap to any pillar (or outside the boundary of the \(R \times C\) grid) whose Euclidean distance from its current cell is at most \(d\). Every time a lizard jumps off a pillar \((r, c)\), that pillar’s height decreases by \(1\); once \(h_{r, c} = 0\), the pillar collapses and no lizard can land on it anymore. At most one lizard may stand on any remaining pillar at a time (though pillars only block lizards when they collapse to height \(0\)). Find the minimum number of lizards that cannot escape outside the grid.
NoteHint 1 — Complement Objective & Pillar Durability

Let \(L_{\text{total}}\) be the initial number of lizards. Then:

\[\min(\text{trapped lizards}) = L_{\text{total}} - \max(\text{escaped lizards}).\]

Since a pillar at \((r, c)\) loses \(1\) unit of height each time a lizard jumps off it, at most \(h_{r, c}\) lizards can ever pass through (land on and jump off) cell \((r, c)\). How do we model this?

NoteHint 2 — Boundary Escape Condition vs. Pillar-to-Pillar Euclidean Distance

  1. When can a lizard on pillar \((r, c)\) (\(1 \le r \le R, 1 \le c \le C\)) jump directly outside the grid in a single leap of length \(\le d\)? Whenever \((r, c)\) is within distance \(d\) of Row \(0\), Row \(R+1\), Col \(0\), or Col \(C+1\):

    \[r \le d \quad\lor\quad R + 1 - r \le d \quad\lor\quad c \le d \quad\lor\quad C + 1 - c \le d.\]

  2. When can a lizard jump from \((r_1, c_1)\) to another pillar \((r_2, c_2)\)? Whenever \((r_1 - r_2)^2 + (c_1 - c_2)^2 \le d^2\) (Euclidean distance, not Manhattan distance!).

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — [SCOI2007 / Luogu P2472] 蜥蜴

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Each pillar \((r, c)\) has a finite jump-off durability \(h_{r, c} \in \{1, 2, 3\}\), and \(R, C \le 20\) (\(RC \le 400\)). Because pillars only restrict how many total jumps can originate from \((r, c)\), the order in which lizards move does not matter: any set of path-flows satisfying the vertex throughput bounds \(F(r, c) \le h_{r, c}\) can be scheduled sequentially without collisions.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

For every pillar \(u = (r, c)\) with \(h_u > 0\), let \(F(u)\) be the total number of lizards that jump off \(u\):

\[F(u) = \mathbb{I}[\text{lizard starts at } u] + \sum_{v : \|v - u\|_2 \le d} f(v \to u) = f(u \to T) + \sum_{w : \|u - w\|_2 \le d} f(u \to w),\]

subject to \(0 \le F(u) \le h_u\). We want to maximize \(|f| = \sum_u f(u \to T)\) and output \(L_{\text{total}} - |f^*|\).

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

Create source \(S\), sink \(T\), and for each cell \(u = (r, c)\) with \(h_{r, c} > 0\), split \(u\) into \(u_{\text{in}}\) and \(u_{\text{out}}\):

  1. Pillar Durability Bridge: Add \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{h_{r, c}} u_{\text{out}}\).
  2. Initial Lizards: If a lizard starts at \(u = (r, c)\), add \(S \xrightarrow{1} u_{\text{in}}\) and increment \(L_{\text{total}}\).
  3. Boundary Escape Arcs: If \(\min(r, R + 1 - r, c, C + 1 - c) \le d\), the lizard can leap off the grid in one jump: add \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} T\).
  4. Pillar-to-Pillar Jump Arcs: For every ordered pair of distinct pillars \(u = (r_1, c_1)\) and \(v = (r_2, c_2)\) with \(h_u > 0, h_v > 0\), and \((r_1 - r_2)^2 + (c_1 - c_2)^2 \le d^2\), add \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} v_{\text{in}}\).

Step 4: Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

Suppose \(R = 1, C = 3, d = 1\), heights are 1 2 1, and lizards start at \((1, 1)\) and \((1, 2)\). Since \(R = 1\) and \(d = 1\), every cell has \(r = 1 \le d\), so every pillar can jump directly outside to \(T\) ((1,1) jumps north to row \(0\), (1,2) jumps north to row \(0\)). Both lizards escape immediately (\(L_{\text{total}} - |f^*| = 2 - 2 = 0\)).

  • Failure Guard 1 (Manhattan vs. Euclidean Distance): When \(d = 2\), a diagonal jump of \((\Delta r, \Delta c) = (1, 1)\) has Manhattan distance \(2 \le 2\) AND Euclidean distance \(\sqrt{2} \le 2\), whereas \((\Delta r, \Delta c) = (2, 1)\) has Euclidean distance \(\sqrt{5} > 2\). Always check dr * dr + dc * dc <= d * d!
  • Failure Guard 2 (Connecting \(S \to u_{\text{out}}\)): Just as in #018, a lizard starting on \(u\) uses up \(1\) unit of \(u\)’s height when it jumps off \(u\). Thus \(S\) must connect to \(u_{\text{in}}\), not \(u_{\text{out}}\).

Solver Contract: \(|V| \le 2RC + 2 \le 802\), \(|E| \le RC(2 + \pi d^2) \approx 2 \times 10^4\), max flow \(\le RC = 400\). Dinic terminates in \(< 5\text{ ms}\).

#020 [Intermediate 2] — [CTSC1999 / Luogu P2754] 家园 / 星际转移问题 (网络流 24 题 #16)

  • Judge URL: https://www.luogu.com.cn/problem/P2754 ([ZH])
  • Constraints: \(N \le 13\) space stations, \(M \le 20\) periodic space shuttles, \(K \le 50\) people. Time Limit: \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: There are \(N\) space stations numbered \(1 \dots N\), plus Earth (numbered \(0\)) and the Moon (numbered \(-1\), which we remap to \(N + 1\)). There are \(M\) space shuttles. Shuttle \(i\) has passenger capacity \(H_i\) and cyclically visits a sequence of \(r_i\) stations \((S_{i, 0}, S_{i, 1}, \dots, S_{i, r_i - 1})\):
    • At time \(t = 0\), shuttle \(i\) is at \(S_{i, 0}\).
    • In general, at time \(t \ge 0\), shuttle \(i\) is at station \(\operatorname{pos}_i(t) := S_{i, \, t \bmod r_i}\), and travels from \(\operatorname{pos}_i(t - 1)\) to \(\operatorname{pos}_i(t)\) during the interval \([t - 1, t]\) (taking \(1\) unit of time).
    • Stations can hold infinitely many waiting passengers across time steps. At each integer timestamp \(t\), shuttles at the same station can exchange passengers instantaneously. Initially at \(t = 0\), all \(K\) people are on Earth (\(0\)). Find the minimum time \(T^*\) at which all \(K\) people can reach the Moon (\(N + 1\)), or output 0 if it is impossible.
NoteHint 1 — Why You Must Check Reachability Before Unrolling Time

What if Earth (\(0\)) and the Moon (\(N + 1\)) belong to completely disconnected components of shuttle routes? If you dynamically add layers \(t = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) until total_flow >= K, an unreachable instance will loop forever! How can you check in \(O(M \cdot \max r_i)\) time whether all \(K\) people can eventually reach the Moon?

NoteHint 2 — Incremental Dynamic Layer Addition (动态加层)

Once reachability is verified with a DSU (unioning all stations on each shuttle’s cyclic route), do not binary search on \(T\). Instead, maintain a single Dinic residual graph \(G_f\):

  • At \(t = 0\), connect \(S \xrightarrow{K} (0, 0)\) and \((N+1, 0) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\).
  • In a loop \(t = 1, 2, 3, \dots\), allocate layer-\(t\) vertices \((u, t)\) for \(u \in \{0, \dots, N+1\}\), add holdover arcs \((u, t-1) \xrightarrow{\infty} (u, t)\), add shuttle transit arcs \((\operatorname{pos}_i(t-1), t-1) \xrightarrow{H_i} (\operatorname{pos}_i(t), t)\) for \(i = 1 \dots M\), connect \((N+1, t) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\), and call flow += dinic(S, T_sink) on the existing residual network!
TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — [CTSC1999 / Luogu P2754] 家园

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Shuttles move deterministically as a function of \(t \bmod r_i\), passengers can wait at stations across time steps, and we seek the earliest timestamp \(T^*\) where cumulative arrivals at the Moon reach \(K \le 50\). This is the canonical archetype for a Time-Expanded Layered DAG (分层图) with Incremental Dynamic Layer Addition (动态加层) (§4.1–4.2).

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(V = \{0, 1, \dots, N, N+1\}\) where \(0\) is Earth and \(N+1\) is the Moon. For each station \(u \in V\) and timestamp \(t \ge 0\):

  • Let \(f_{\text{hold}}(u, t-1 \to t) \in [0, \infty)\) be the number of passengers waiting at station \(u\) from \(t - 1\) to \(t\).
  • Let \(f_{\text{shuttle}}(i, t-1 \to t) \in [0, H_i]\) be the number of passengers aboard shuttle \(i\) during \([t - 1, t]\), moving from \(u_i(t-1) = S_{i, (t-1)\bmod r_i}\) to \(v_i(t) = S_{i, t \bmod r_i}\).

Spacetime conservation at \((u, t)\) requires:

\[f_{\text{hold}}(u, t-1 \to t) + \sum_{i : v_i(t) = u} f_{\text{shuttle}}(i, t-1 \to t) = f_{\text{hold}}(u, t \to t+1) + \sum_{j : u_j(t) = u} f_{\text{shuttle}}(j, t \to t+1).\]

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  1. Step 0 (DSU Reachability Guard): Initialize a DSU over \(\{0, 1, \dots, N+1\}\). For each shuttle \(i\) and each \(j \in \{0, \dots, r_i - 1\}\), union \(S_{i, j}\) with \(S_{i, (j+1) \bmod r_i}\). If find(0) != find(N + 1), output 0 and terminate immediately.
  2. Base Layer (\(t = 0\)): Define vertex ID encoding id(u, t) = 2 + t * (N + 2) + u (reserving 0 for \(S\) and 1 for \(T_{\text{sink}}\)). Add:
    • \(S \xrightarrow{K} \operatorname{id}(0, 0)\) (all \(K\) people start on Earth at \(t = 0\); or simply \(S \xrightarrow{\infty} \operatorname{id}(0, 0)\) and check when total_flow >= K).
    • \(\operatorname{id}(N + 1, 0) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\).
  3. Incremental Layer Loop (\(t = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)):
    • Station Holdover Arcs: For every \(u \in \{0, \dots, N\}\), add \(\operatorname{id}(u, t - 1) \xrightarrow{\infty} \operatorname{id}(u, t)\).
    • Shuttle Transit Arcs: For each shuttle \(i \in \{1, \dots, M\}\), let \(u = S_{i, (t-1) \bmod r_i}\) and \(v = S_{i, t \bmod r_i}\). Add \(\operatorname{id}(u, t - 1) \xrightarrow{H_i} \operatorname{id}(v, t)\).
    • Moon Collection Arc: Add \(\operatorname{id}(N + 1, t) \xrightarrow{\infty} T_{\text{sink}}\).

At first glance, radiation spreading over \(T \le 60\) steps on an \(N \times N\) grid looks like a time-expanded DAG with \(T N^2 \le 6000\) vertices. However, because intermediate corridor transitions have \(\infty\) capacity, a group of scientists at \(u\) can reach a capsule at \(v\) before the deadline if and only if there exists at least one safe spacetime walk from \((u, 0)\) to \((v, t)\) with \(t \le \min(T, \operatorname{rad}(v))\). Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(\operatorname{rad}(v) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \cup \{\infty\}\) be the shortest-path distance from the reactor Z to cell \(v\) avoiding walls Y. For each cell \(u\), let \(\operatorname{Reach}(u)\) be the set of cells \(v\) reachable by a walk \(u = w_0 \to w_1 \to \dots \to w_k = v\) of length \(k \le T\) such that:

      • If \(nt < \operatorname{rad}(y)\) and \(y\) has not been pushed to \(Q\) yet, set \(\operatorname{dist}[y] = nt\) and push \(y\) into \(Q\).

NoteHint 2 — Structural Geometry Lemma: Why an Optimal Grid Cut Is Either Row-Monochromatic or Column-Monochromatic

Suppose a cut colors the \(N \times M\) grid so that neither does any monochromatic horizontal stripe span all the way across, nor do all rows/columns stay monochromatic.

Consider any connected component \(C\) of Red cells. If \(C\) does not touch all four sides, smoothing its boundary into straight horizontal or vertical lines across the entire grid never increases the internal perimeter length! Specifically, one can prove that there always exists a minimum cut on the \(N \times M\) grid where either:

  1. Every row \(r \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) is monochromatic (all \(M\) cells in row \(r\) have the same color \(c_r \in \{\text{R}, \text{B}\}\)), OR
  2. Every column \(c \in \{1, \dots, M\}\) is monochromatic (all \(N\) cells in column \(c\) have the same color \(d_c \in \{\text{R}, \text{B}\}\))!
TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — [CF 1368H1] Breadboard Capacity

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

The problem asks for the maximum edge-disjoint routing between Red (\(S\)) and Blue (\(T\)) boundary ports on an \(N \times M\) grid with \(N, M \le 10^5\). This is our first glimpse of Cut-DP Elimination (explored systematically in Part IV): when a flow network is a highly regular geometric grid of size \(10^{10}\), we dualize Max-Flow to Min-Cut and optimize the cut partition \((S, T)\) directly via Dynamic Programming!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(x_{r, c} \in \{0, 1\}\) denote the cut side of cell \((r, c)\) (\(0 = \text{Red}, 1 = \text{Blue}\)). The capacity of the cut \(x\) is:

\[\operatorname{Cut}(x) = \sum_{r=1}^N \sum_{c=1}^{M-1} |x_{r,c} - x_{r,c+1}| + \sum_{r=1}^{N-1} \sum_{c=1}^M |x_{r,c} - x_{r+1,c}| + \text{BoundaryMismatch}(x).\]

Geometric Rectification Theorem: Suppose row \(r\) is not monochromatic, so \(\sum_{c=1}^{M-1} |x_{r,c} - x_{r,c+1}| \ge 1\). Each non-monochromatic row pays at least \(1\) internal horizontal cut edge, plus at most \(2\) boundary mismatches (L[r] and R[r]) can be saved by making row \(r\) bichromatic instead of monochromatic—and only when L[r] != R[r], where a monochromatic row also pays \(1\) boundary mismatch! By submodular cut uncrossing and boundary projection, any minimum cut can be rectified without increasing its cost so that either all rows are monochromatic (\(x_{r, 1} = \dots = x_{r, M} = c_r\)) OR all columns are monochromatic (\(x_{1, c} = \dots = x_{N, c} = d_c\)).

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Two 1D Sweeping DPs)

  1. Case 1: Row-Monochromatic Cut (\(x_{r, c} = c_r \in \{0, 1\}\) for all \(c\)):

    • Within row \(r\), horizontal internal wires cost \(0\).

    • Left and right ports of row \(r\) cost \([L_r \neq c_r] + [R_r \neq c_r]\).

    • At row \(1\), top ports cost \(\sum_{c=1}^M [U_c \neq c_1]\). At row \(N\), bottom ports cost \(\sum_{c=1}^M [D_c \neq c_N]\).

    • Between row \(r-1\) and row \(r\), all \(M\) vertical wires are cut if \(c_r \neq c_{r-1}\) (cost \(M\)), or \(0\) if \(c_r = c_{r-1}\).

    • Define dp_row[r][col] for \(r = 1 \dots N, \text{col} \in \{0, 1\}\):

      \[\text{dp\_row}[1][\text{col}] = \sum_{c=1}^M [U_c \neq \text{col}] + [L_1 \neq \text{col}] + [R_1 \neq \text{col}],\]

      \[\text{dp\_row}[r][\text{col}] = [L_r \neq \text{col}] + [R_r \neq \text{col}] + \min\big(\text{dp\_row}[r-1][\text{col}],\;\; \text{dp\_row}[r-1][1 - \text{col}] + M\big),\]

      and add \(\sum_{c=1}^M [D_c \neq \text{col}]\) at \(r = N\).

  2. Case 2: Column-Monochromatic Cut (\(x_{r, c} = d_c \in \{0, 1\}\) for all \(r\)): By exact \(90^\circ\) symmetry (swapping \(N \leftrightarrow M\), \((L, R) \leftrightarrow (U, D)\)), run the identical 2-state DP dp_col[c][col] across columns \(c = 1 \dots M\) with transition penalty \(N\).
  3. Final Answer: \(\min(\text{ans}_{\text{row}}, \text{ans}_{\text{col}})\).

Step 4: Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

Suppose \(N = 2, M = 3\), all U ports are Red (R), all D ports are Blue (B), and all L, R ports are Red (R).

  • If we assign Row \(1\) all Red (\(c_1 = \text{R}\)) and Row \(2\) all Blue (\(c_2 = \text{B}\)):
    • Row \(1\) pays \(0\) (U, L[1], R[1] are all Red).
    • Transition \(c_1 \neq c_2\) cuts \(M = 3\) vertical internal wires.

The problem combines two independent structural constraints:

  1. Routing Policy Constraint: Packets only traverse shortest paths from \(1\) to \(N\) (with respect to delays \(w_e\)).
  2. Hardware Bottleneck Constraint: Intermediate routers \(u \in \{2, \dots, N-1\}\) have vertex throughput capacities \(c_u \le 10^9\).

As established in §5.1, a shortest-path restriction on an undirected weighted graph compiles via Dijkstra’s tightness condition \(d[u] + w_{uv} = d[v]\) into a directed DAG, and router CPU limits compile via vertex splitting \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{c_u} u_{\text{out}}\).

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(d: V \to \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) be the unique shortest-path distance vector from vertex \(1\):

\[d[1] = 0, \qquad d[v] = \min_{\{u, v\} \in E} (d[u] + w_{uv}).\]

Define the directed Shortest-Path Admissibility Set:

\[E_{\text{SP}} = \big\{(u \to v) \;\big|\; \{u, v\} \in E \;\land\; d[u] + w_{uv} = d[v]\big\}.\]

Any directed path from \(1\) to \(N\) using only arcs in \(E_{\text{SP}}\) has total length \(\sum_{(u \to v) \in P} w_{uv} = \sum_{(u \to v) \in P} (d[v] - d[u]) = d[N] - d[1] = d[N]\), which is a shortest \(1\)-\(N\) path! Conversely, any shortest \(1\)-\(N\) path uses only arcs in \(E_{\text{SP}}\). Thus we seek a maximum \(1\)-\(N\) flow supported on \(E_{\text{SP}}\) subject to vertex throughput \(F(u) \le c_u\) for \(u \in \{2, \dots, N-1\}\).

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  1. Phase 1 (Dijkstra from Vertex \(1\)): Compute d[1..N] in \(O((N + M) \log N)\) time using int64_t distances.
  2. Phase 2 (Vertex Splitting): Split each router \(u \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) into \(u_{\text{in}} = u\) and \(u_{\text{out}} = u + N\):
    • For \(u \in \{1, N\}\), add \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{\infty} u_{\text{out}}\).
    • For \(u \in \{2, \dots, N-1\}\), add \(u_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{c_u} u_{\text{out}}\).
  3. Phase 3 (Wiring Tight Shortest-Path Arcs): For each original undirected link \(\{u, v\}\) with delay \(w\):
    • If \(d[u] + w == d[v]\), add directed arc \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} v_{\text{in}}\).
    • If \(d[v] + w == d[u]\), add directed arc \(v_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\infty} u_{\text{in}}\).
  4. Phase 4 (Max-Flow): Run Dinic from \(S = 1_{\text{in}}\) (or \(1_{\text{out}}\)) to \(T = N_{\text{out}}\) (or \(N_{\text{in}}\)) using 64-bit integer capacities (long long / int64_t, with \(\infty = 10^{18}\)).

Step 4: Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

Suppose \(N = 4\), links are \(\{1, 2\}\) (\(w=2\)), \(\{2, 4\}\) (\(w=2\)), \(\{1, 3\}\) (\(w=1\)), \(\{3, 2\}\) (\(w=1\)), \(\{3, 4\}\) (\(w=5\)), and router capacities are \(c_2 = 10, c_3 = 4\):

  • Dijkstra distances from \(1\): \(d[1] = 0\), \(d[3] = 1\), \(d[2] = 2\) (via both \(1 \to 2\) and \(1 \to 3 \to 2\)!), \(d[4] = 4\) (via \(2 \to 4\); note \(3 \to 4\) has \(d[3] + 5 = 6 > 4\), so \(3 \to 4\) is not tight!).
  • Tight DAG arcs: \(1_{\text{out}} \to 2_{\text{in}}\), \(1_{\text{out}} \to 3_{\text{in}}\), \(3_{\text{out}} \to 2_{\text{in}}\), and \(2_{\text{out}} \to 4_{\text{in}}\).
  • Notice that even though \(1 \to 2\) and \(1 \to 3 \to 2\) can together supply \(10 + 4 = 14\) units into \(2_{\text{in}}\), router \(2\)’s internal bridge \(2_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{10} 2_{\text{out}}\) caps total throughput at \(10\).
  • Failure Guard (int32_t Overflow): Because \(c_u \le 10^9\) and up to \(N = 500\) routers can contribute in parallel, the maximum flow and \(\infty\) constants exceed \(2^{31} - 1\). Using int instead of long long overflows immediately!

Solver Contract: \(|V| = 2N \le 1000\), \(|E| \le N + M \le 10^5\). Because \(E_{\text{SP}}\) is a sparse DAG, Dinic terminates in \(< 20\text{ ms}\).

FM-01 Greedy Shortest-Path Stripping Before Full Augmentation Deleting a shortest \(s\)-\(t\) path from \(G\) without reverse residual arcs strands crossing paths (6-Vertex Diamond-Bridge, §2.3). Run Dinic to completion first; apply Flow Decomposition (Theorem 3.1) only on the final support \(\operatorname{supp}(f^*)\).

Explicitly guard if (s == 1) before building the network (or keep transition arcs at cap \(1\) and handle \(s=1\) separately).

8. Chapter Revision Summary & Multilingual Bibliography

Key Takeaways

  1. Flow Decomposition (流分解定理, Theorem 3.1): Every feasible flow \(f\) decomposes into \(\le |E|\) simple \(s\)-\(t\) paths and simple cycles in \(O(VE)\) time via bottleneck stripping on \(\operatorname{supp}(f)\).
  2. Vertex Capacity Splitting (\(\text{out} \to \text{in}\) Invariant): A vertex throughput bound \(0 \le F(v) \le C_v\) compiles into \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{C_v} v_{\text{out}}\) with all external transitions wired strictly from out-ports to in-ports (\(u_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}}\)). On unit-capacity vertex-split graphs, \(\min(\deg^-(x), \deg^+(x)) = 1\) holds at every internal vertex, unlocking Even and Tarjan’s \(O(E \sqrt{V})\) complexity bound and proving all Four Variants of Menger’s Theorem.
  3. Time-Expanded Layered DAGs (分层图) & Incremental Dynamic Layer Addition (动态加层): Promoting discrete time \(t \in \{0, \dots, T\}\) into the vertex state \((u, t)\) turns temporal routing into a DAG with holdover arcs \((u, t-1) \to (u, t)\) and transit arcs \((u, t-1) \to (v, t)\). Appending layers incrementally onto a persistent residual graph \(G_f\) finds the minimum completion time \(T^*\) without binary search.
  4. Graph Sparsification & Dual Elimination: Shortest-path restrictions (\(d[u] + w_{uv} = d[v]\)) and DP transitions (\(dp[j] = dp[i] + 1\)) extract sparse DAGs; Row/Column Hub vertices ([ARC 074 D]) collapse \(\Theta(HW(H+W))\) cliques into \(O(H+W)\) bipartite graphs; and on \(10^{10}\)-vertex regular grids ([CF 1368H1]), Min-Cut geometry eliminates the interior graph entirely in favor of \(O(N+M)\) Cut-DP.
