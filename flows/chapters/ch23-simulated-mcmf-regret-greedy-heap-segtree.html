23.1 Direction B for MCMF — Why Successive Shortest Paths Collapses to a Data Structure When \(N \le 2 \times 10^5\)

In Chapter 13, we saw that when a Maximum Flow or Minimum Cut problem has \(N \le 2 \times 10^5\) vertices, running Dinic’s algorithm on an explicit graph is hopeless; instead, we use Direction B (Graph \(\to\) Data Structure) to simulate the minimum cut via dynamic programming, segment trees, or greedy sweeps.

The exact same phenomenon occurs—with even greater frequency in modern contests—for Minimum-Cost Maximum Flow (MCMF). Suppose a problem has \(N = 2 \times 10^5\) items, days, or tree vertices. Even if the underlying MCMF network is sparse (\(|V|, |E| = O(N)\)), running the explicit Successive Shortest Path (SSP) algorithm requires \(F = \Theta(N)\) augmentations, each taking \(\Omega(N)\) or \(\Omega(N \log N)\) time via SPFA or Dijkstra with Johnson potentials—a fatal \(\Theta(N^2 \log N)\) bottleneck.

Yet in competitive programming, huge-scale MCMF networks are never arbitrary graphs. Their topology is almost always one of four structured families:

  1. A 1D Directed Spine (Time / Prefix Order): Supply arrives at index \(i\) and can only travel rightward to demand at index \(j \ge i\).
  2. A 1D Doubly-Linked Adjacency Chain or Interval Line: Augmenting paths correspond to flipping the selection state of a contiguous subarray or alternating chain.
  3. A Weighted Tree Metric: Flow moves between subtrees, and the cost between \(u\) and \(v\) telescopes at \(w = \operatorname{lca}(u, v)\) as \(\operatorname{dep}(u) + \operatorname{dep}(v) - 2\operatorname{dep}(w)\).
  4. A Constant-Size Hub or Vertex-State Automaton: All \(N\) items interact only through \(O(1)\) global hub nodes (such as a shared token pool of capacity \(K - L\)).

In Chinese competitive programming literature, the technique of executing the exact steps of SSP or Cycle Cancelling on these structured graphs using priority queues, segment trees, or mergeable heaps—without ever instantiating adjacency lists—is known as Simulated Cost Flow (模拟费用流). Its priority-queue specialization is known as Regret Greedy (反悔贪心) or Buy-Back Greedy.

The Regret-Heap Residual Isomorphism Theorem

Why do “regret heaps” work, and why do ad-hoc greedy intuition attempts so often fail on edge cases? Because Regret Greedy is not a heuristic at all: it is an exact, step-by-step algebraic encoding of the residual graph in the Successive Shortest Path algorithm.

ImportantTheorem 23.1 (The Regret-Heap Residual Isomorphism Theorem)

Consider a 1D prefix bipartite transportation network on indices \(1, 2, \dots, N\) where:

  • Every supply node \(i\) can inject \(1\) unit of flow from the source \(S\) at cost \(-c_i\) (or acquisition cost \(+c_i\)).
  • Every demand node \(j \ge i\) can absorb \(1\) unit of flow into the sink \(T\) at cost \(+c_j\).
  • Thus, matching supply \(i\) to demand \(j\) (\(i \le j\)) along the forward spine costs \(c_j - c_i\).

Suppose that at step \(j\), a unit of flow from \(i\) is matched to \(j\), contributing \(c_j - c_i\) to the objective. In the MCMF residual network \(G_f\), saturating the path \(S \to i \to j \to T\) creates a reverse residual arc \(T \to j \to i\) of residual cost \(-(c_j - c_i)\).

If a later demand node \(k > j\) augments \(1\) unit of flow by traversing this reverse residual arc \(j \to i\) and continuing forward along \(i \to k \to T\), the net change in total cost is:

\[ \Delta \text{Cost}(j \mapsto k) = \underbrace{-(c_j - c_i)}_{\text{reverse arc } j \to i} + \underbrace{(c_k - c_i)}_{\text{forward arc } i \to k} = c_k - c_j. \]

Notice that the original supply cost \(c_i\) cancels out identically! After this augmentation, the total accumulated cost is:

\[ (c_j - c_i) + (c_k - c_j) = c_k - c_i, \]

which is the exact cost of having matched \(i\) directly to \(k\) in the first place. Therefore, inserting a matched demand \(j\) back into the candidate supply priority queue with a “regret key” equal to \(c_j\) is \(100\%\) isomorphic to exposing the reverse residual path \(T \to j \to i\) in \(G_f\).

Proof. In the explicit residual network \(G_f\) on the 1D spine \(1 \to 2 \to \dots \to N\):

  1. Before matching \((i, j)\), the arc \(S \to i\) has capacity \(1\) and cost \(-c_i\), the spine arcs \(t \to t+1\) have capacity \(\infty\) and cost \(0\), and the arc \(j \to T\) has capacity \(1\) and cost \(+c_j\).
  2. Pushing \(1\) unit of flow along \(S \to i \to \dots \to j \to T\) saturates \(S \to i\) and \(j \to T\), and sends \(1\) unit of positive flow along the spine segment \([i, j]\).
  3. Consequently, in \(G_f\), the arc \(T \to j\) (or equivalently, a virtual source arc \(S_{\text{regret}} \to j\) representing the ability to un-match \(j\) from \(T\)) appears with capacity \(1\) and cost \(-c_j\), while reverse spine arcs \(t+1 \to t\) carry cost \(0\) on \([i, j]\).
  4. Any future demand \(k > j\) looking for the cheapest available supply in \([1, k]\) sees two classes of residual paths from \(S\):
    • Fresh supplies \(i' \le k\) via \(S \to i' \to \dots \to k \to T\) at cost \(c_k - c_{i'}\).
    • Regret supplies (redirected demands) \(j \le k\) via \(S \to i \to \dots \to j \xrightarrow{\text{cancel } j \to T} j \to \dots \to k \to T\) at incremental cost \((c_j - c_i) - (c_j - c_i) + (c_k - c_j) = c_k - c_j\).
  5. Both classes have the exact functional form \(c_k - \text{key}\), where \(\text{key} = c_{i'}\) for a fresh supply and \(\text{key} = c_j\) for a regret supply. A single min-heap (for minimization of \(c_i\), or max-heap for maximization of \(c_i\)) over all active keys in \([1, k]\) therefore returns the exact shortest augmenting path in \(G_f\) in \(O(\log N)\) time. \(\blacksquare\)

Worked Trace: CF 865 D — Buy Low Sell High and the Zero-Net-Position Relay

Let us see how Theorem 23.1 immediately resolves a classic problem where naive greedy fails, and why a subtle double-push invariant is forced by the residual graph.

Problem Statement (CF 865 D)

You are given the stock price \(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_N\) over \(N \le 3 \times 10^5\) days. On each day \(t\), you may do at most one of three actions:

  • Buy \(1\) share at price \(p_t\) (contributing \(-p_t\) to cash),
  • Sell \(1\) share at price \(p_t\) (contributing \(+p_t\) to cash, provided you hold at least \(1\) share), or
  • Do nothing (\(0\) change).

You start with \(0\) shares andunlimited cash. Find the maximum total profit after day \(N\).

4-Step English-to-Graph Compilation

  1. Step 1 (Diagnostic Signal): \(N = 3 \times 10^5\) rules out \(O(N^2)\) DP (tracking (day, shares_held)) and explicit MCMF. Because each day’s decision is a unit-capacity assignment \(x_t \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\) with prefix non-negativity \(\sum_{t=1}^k x_t \ge 0\), this is a 1D spine maximum-weight matching where a buy at \(i\) pairs with a sell at \(j > i\) for profit \(p_j - p_i\).
  2. Step 2 (Algebraic & Residual Model): On day \(j\), the node \(j\) has capacity \(1\): its net change in shares is \(x_j \in \{-1, 0, +1\}\) (where \(+1\) means buy, \(-1\) means sell). Suppose day \(j\) is initially matched as a sell (\(x_j = -1\)) paired with an earlier buy at day \(i < j\) (\(x_i = +1\)), earning \(p_j - p_i\). Now, as we move to future days \(k > j\), what can the residual network do with vertex \(j\)? Because \(x_j\) starts at \(-1\) and is allowed to increase all the way to \(+1\), vertex \(j\) has \(2\) units of residual upward capacity (\(-1 \to 0\) and \(0 \to +1\)), both at marginal cost \(p_j\)!
    • Unit 1 (The Regret Token, \(x_j : -1 \to 0\)): Cancel the sale at day \(j\), paying back \(p_j\), and route the share from \(i\) onward to sell at day \(k\) for \(+p_k\). Net change at \(j\): \(x_j = 0\) (do nothing!). Telescoped profit: \((p_j - p_i) + (p_k - p_j) = p_k - p_i\).
    • Unit 2 (The Fresh Buy Token, \(x_j : 0 \to +1\)): After cancelling the sale at \(j\) (so \(x_j = 0\)), day \(j\) is now completely idle and can also buy a fresh share (\(x_j = +1\)) at price \(p_j\) to sell at some second future day \(k' > j\)!
  3. Step 3 (Mechanical Heap Compilation — Why We Push \(p_j\) Twice!): Maintain a min-heap \(H\) of available buy/regret prices. On day \(j\):
    • Always push \(p_j\) once into \(H\) (representing the fresh buy transition \(x_j : 0 \to +1\) at cost \(p_j\)).
    • Check the minimum element \(x = \min(H)\). If \(p_j > x\), selling at \(p_j\) against \(x\) is profitable:
      • Add \(p_j - x\) to profit and pop \(x\) from \(H\).
      • Push \(p_j\) a second time into \(H\) (representing the regret transition \(x_j : -1 \to 0\) that un-sells at \(p_j\))! Notice the symmetry: if we did not match at \(j\), \(p_j\) is in \(H\) once (residual capacity \(1\): \(0 \to +1\)). If we matched at \(j\) (\(x_j = -1\)), \(p_j\) is in \(H\) twice (residual capacity \(2\): \(-1 \to 0\) and \(0 \to +1\))!
  4. Step 4 (Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Broken-Gadget Counterexample): Consider \(N = 4\) days with prices \(p = [1, 5, 10, 20]\).
    • Day 1 (\(p_1 = 1\)): Push \(1\). \(H = \{1\}\), profit = 0.
    • Day 2 (\(p_2 = 5\)): Push \(5 \implies H = \{1, 5\}\). Since \(5 > \min(H) = 1\), pop \(1\), add \(5 - 1 = 4\) to profit, and push regret key \(5 \implies H = \{5_{\text{fresh}}, 5_{\text{regret}}\}\), profit = 4. (Current state: Buy at 1, Sell at 2).
    • Day 3 (\(p_3 = 10\)): Push \(10 \implies H = \{5_{\text{fresh}}, 5_{\text{regret}}, 10\}\). Since \(10 > \min(H) = 5\), pop \(5_{\text{regret}}\), add \(10 - 5 = 5\) to profit, and push regret key \(10 \implies H = \{5_{\text{fresh}}, 10_{\text{fresh}}, 10_{\text{regret}}\}\), profit = 4 + 5 = 9. (Current state: Buy at 1, Do Nothing at 2, Sell at 3).
    • Day 4 (\(p_4 = 20\)): Push \(20 \implies H = \{5_{\text{fresh}}, 10, 10, 20\}\). Since \(20 > \min(H) = 5_{\text{fresh}}\), pop \(5_{\text{fresh}}\), add \(20 - 5 = 15\) to profit, and push regret key \(20 \implies\) profit = 9 + 15 = 24!
    • Check: Buying on Days 1 and 2 (\(-1 - 5 = -6\)) and selling on Days 3 and 4 (\(+10 + 20 = +30\)) yields \(30 - 6 = 24\).
    • What breaks if we only push \(p_j\) once on a match? On Day 2, if we only pushed one \(5\) (the regret token) and forgot the fresh-buy token \(5_{\text{fresh}}\), then after Day 3 popped \(5\) to form \((1, 3)\), Day 2 would have no token left in \(H\)! Day 4 would be forced to match with \(10\) for profit \(9 + (20 - 10) = 19 < 24\), missing the optimal disjoint pairs \((1, 3)\) and \((2, 4)\).
NoteThe Slope-Trick Dual Isomorphism (CF 713C \(\leftrightarrow\) CF 865D)

Compare the 5-line inner loop of CF 865 D (min-heap):

H.push(p);
if (H.top() < p) { ans += p - H.top(); H.pop(); H.push(p); }

with the 5-line inner loop of 1D \(\ell_1\) Isotonic Regression (CF 713 C — Sonya and Problem Wihtout a Legend, minimizing \(\sum |b'_i - a'_i|\) subject to \(b'_1 \le \dots \le b'_N\) via a Slope-Trick max-heap):

H.push(a);
if (H.top() > a) { ans += H.top() - a; H.pop(); H.push(a); }

They are literally identical up to heap orientation! Why? By Linear Programming Duality (Chapter 21), minimizing \(\sum_i |b'_i - a'_i|\) subject to \(b'_i - b'_{i-1} \ge 0\) is the dual of a 1D spine circulation problem where each node \(i\) has capacity \(2\) (\(f_i \in [-1, +1]\)) and cost \(a'_i\). Adding \(|x - a'_i|\) to the Slope-Trick convex function inserts two slope-change breakpoints at \(a'_i\), corresponding to the exact two units of residual capacity (\(-1 \to 0\) and \(0 \to +1\)) in CF 865 D!

23.2 Prefix-Constrained Bipartite Matching (\(i \le j\)) & WQS / Aliens Convex Penalty Elimination (CF 802 O — April Fools' Problem (hard))

In CF 865 D, we were allowed to make any number of profitable transactions, and a single node \(j\) could simultaneously cancel a sale and buy a fresh share. Now consider a strictly harder and ubiquitous archetype: cardinality-constrained prefix matching where left and right endpoints are distinct resources.

Problem Statement (CF 802 O — April Fools' Problem (hard))

Over \(N \le 5 \times 10^5\) days, on day \(i\) you can prepare at most \(1\) problem at cost \(a_i\), and on day \(j\) you can print at most \(1\) problem at cost \(b_j\). A problem prepared on day \(i\) can be printed on day \(j\) if and only if \(i \le j\). You must prepare and print at least \(K\) (equivalently, exactly \(K\) since \(a_i, b_j \ge 0\)) problems. Find the minimum total cost.

Why Naive \(K\)-Step Online Regret Fails (FM-23 / H-24)

Suppose we try to avoid binary search and simply run a left-to-right sweep \(j = 1 \dots N\), maintaining \(K\) best pairs online. Why does that fail?

Because the constraint \(i \le j\) is directional! In an online sweep without a global slope threshold \(\lambda\), you only know whether a pair \((i, j)\) is “worth taking” if you know the marginal cost of the \(K\)-th augmentation across the entire array \([1, N]\). Worse still, in an explicit MCMF network:

  • Source \(S \to u_i\) has capacity \(1\), cost \(a_i\).
  • Spine \(u_i \to u_{i+1}\) has capacity \(\infty\), cost \(0\).
  • Sink \(u_j \to T\) has capacity \(1\), cost \(b_j\).

When an augmenting path uses a reverse spine arc \(u_{t+1} \to u_t\) (meaning positive flow was already moving rightward across \(u_t \to u_{t+1}\)), it splits one long interval \([i_1, j_1]\) paired with an internal point into two valid intervals \([i_1, j_2]\) and \([i_2, j_1]\). A single left-to-right pass at a fixed Lagrangian slope \(\lambda\) automatically simulates all such interval-splitting augmentations without ever tracking interval endpoints!

Step 1: WQS / Aliens Convex Penalty Elimination

By Theorem 17.3 (Convexity of the Min-Cost Flow Function) from Chapter 17, let \(C(k)\) be the minimum cost to send \(k\) units of flow from \(S\) to \(T\) in our prefix network (\(0 \le k \le N\)). Because each SSP step chooses the shortest augmenting path, the marginal costs \(\Delta_k = C(k) - C(k-1)\) are non-decreasing:

\[ \Delta_1 \le \Delta_2 \le \dots \le \Delta_N \implies C(k) \text{ is convex on } \{0, 1, \dots, N\}. \]

Instead of constraining the flow to equal \(K\), we apply the WQS / Aliens Lagrangian relaxation: subtract a reward \(\lambda\) from every unit of flow reaching \(T\) (equivalently, change every print cost from \(b_j\) to \(b_j - \lambda\)). For a fixed \(\lambda\), we solve the unconstrained minimum-cost circulation problem:

\[ g(\lambda) = \min_{0 \le k \le N} \big(C(k) - k \cdot \lambda\big), \]

which simply pushes flow along every augmenting path whose penalized cost \(a_i + b_j - \lambda\) is negative!

Step 2: The Two-Candidate Regret Heap at Fixed \(\lambda\)

Once \(\lambda\) is fixed, we sweep \(j = 1, 2, \dots, N\) from left to right. Every matched pair has the form \(a_i + (b_j - \lambda) < 0\) for some \(i \le j\). What are the available options in the residual graph on day \(j\)?

  1. Candidate Type A (Unmatched Preparation \(i \le j\)): At the start of day \(j\), preparation \(u_j\) becomes available, so we push (key = a_j, type = A) into our min-heap \(Q\). If day \(j\)’s penalized print option \(b_j - \lambda\) matches with a Type-A entry key = a_i (because \(a_i + b_j - \lambda < 0\)), we:
    • Add \(a_i + b_j - \lambda\) to total penalized cost,
    • Increment the matched pair count cnt by \(+1\) (since we sent \(1\) new unit of flow from \(S\) to \(T\)),
    • Pop \(a_i\) from \(Q\), and
    • Push a Type-B Regret Token (key = -(b_j - \lambda), type = REGRET_B) into \(Q\)!
  2. Candidate Type B (Stealing a Previous Print \(j_0 < j\)): Why did we push key = -(b_{j_0} - \lambda) with type = REGRET_B when \(i\) matched with \(j_0\)? Suppose a later day \(j > j_0\) has a cheaper print cost \(b_j < b_{j_0}\). If day \(j\) pops the regret token key = -(b_{j_0} - \lambda) from \(Q\) and pairs with it, the incremental cost added is: \[ \text{key} + (b_j - \lambda) = -(b_{j_0} - \lambda) + (b_j - \lambda) = b_j - b_{j_0} < 0. \] Adding this to the earlier cost \(a_i + b_{j_0} - \lambda\) gives: \[ (a_i + b_{j_0} - \lambda) + (b_j - b_{j_0}) = a_i + b_j - \lambda, \] which redirects the preparation at \(i\) from print \(j_0\) to print \(j\)! Crucially:
    • Because this transition only redirects an existing unit of flow from \(j_0 \to T\) to \(j \to T\), the total flow count cnt changes by \(0\) (cnt += 0)!
    • And because day \(j\) is now matched to \(T\), we push a new regret token (key = -(b_j - \lambda), type = REGRET_B) into \(Q\) so a still later day \(j' > j\) can steal from \(j\)!
WarningCritical Invariant: Collinear Slope Tie-Breaking in WQS (FM-23)

When the convex function \(C(k)\) has a flat segment of slope \(\lambda^*\) (so multiple values of \(k\) tie for the minimum of \(C(k) - k \lambda^*\)), our inner check solve(lambda) must break ties consistently—for instance, always returning the minimum (or maximum) possible pair count cnt among all optimal solutions for \(\lambda\).

To return the minimum optimal pair count cnt (and binary search for the smallest \(\lambda\) where cnt >= K, or largest \(\lambda\) where cnt <= K):

  1. Only take an augmenting path if its penalized cost is strictly negative (key + b[j] - lambda < 0).
  2. Inside the min-heap \(Q\) of (key, type), if two entries have the exact same key, order type = REGRET_B (which adds \(0\) to cnt) before type = A (which adds \(+1\) to cnt)! Conversely, if you order type = A before type = REGRET_B and take paths with <= 0, you consistently maximize cnt. Either convention works cleanly as long as the binary search condition matches the tie-breaker!

Complete Reference Implementation (CF 802 O Engine)

#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

struct Node {
    long long key;
    int delta_cnt; // 1 for fresh Type-A prep, 0 for Type-B regret token
    // Min-heap comparator: smaller key first; on tie, prefer delta_cnt = 0 (minimum pair count)
    bool operator>(const Node& other) const {
        if (key != other.key) return key > other.key;
        return delta_cnt > other.delta_cnt;
    }
};

pair<long long, int> evaluate_slope(int n, const vector<long long>& a,
                                    const vector<long long>& b, long long lambda) {
    priority_queue<Node, vector<Node>, greater<Node>> pq;
    long long penalized_cost = 0;
    int cnt = 0;
    for (int j = 0; j < n; ++j) {
        // 1. Day j's preparation option becomes available
        pq.push({a[j], 1});
        // 2. Check if day j's print option (b[j] - lambda) can profitably match
        long long print_val = b[j] - lambda;
        if (pq.top().key + print_val < 0) {
            Node best = pq.top();
            pq.pop();
            penalized_cost += best.key + print_val;
            cnt += best.delta_cnt;
            // Push regret token: cancelling print j later refunds -(b[j] - lambda) with 0 net count change
            pq.push({-print_val, 0});
        }
    }
    return {penalized_cost, cnt};
}

long long solve_cf802o(int n, int k, const vector<long long>& a, const vector<long long>& b) {
    long long lo = 0, hi = 2000000005LL, best_lambda = 0;
    while (lo <= hi) {
        long long mid = lo + (hi - lo) / 2;
        auto [cost, cnt] = evaluate_slope(n, a, b, mid);
        if (cnt <= k) {
            best_lambda = mid;
            lo = mid + 1;
        } else {
            hi = mid - 1;
        }
    }
    auto [cost, cnt] = evaluate_slope(n, a, b, best_lambda);
    // Reconstruct exact unpenalized cost C(K) from the supporting line of slope best_lambda
    return cost + 1LL * k * best_lambda;
}

23.3 Segment-Tree Simulated SSP (CF 280 D) & Tree Mergeable-Heap Flow (ICPC WF 2018 Conquer the World, UOJ #455)

Priority queues are not the only data structure capable of simulating residual graphs. Whenever the residual augmenting path corresponds to a contiguous 1D interval or a path between two nodes in a tree, the shortest-path query in SSP compiles into a Segment Tree or a Mergeable Heap on subtrees.

Part A: Segment-Tree Simulated SSP (\(k\)-Disjoint Maximum Subarrays, CF 280 D)

Problem Statement (CF 280 D — k-Maximum Subsequence Sum)

Given an integer array \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N\) (\(N \le 10^5\), \(|a_i| \le 500\)), process \(Q \le 10^5\) operations of two types:

  1. Point update: set \(a_p \gets v\).
  2. Range query (L, R, k): choose at most \(k\) (\(1 \le k \le 20\)) mutually disjoint subarrays inside \([L, R]\) to maximize the sum of their elements.

4-Step English-to-Graph Compilation

  1. Step 1 (Diagnostic Signal): For a single static array, \(k\)-disjoint subarrays takes \(O(N k)\) DP. With \(Q = 10^5\) range queries, combining DP states of size \(O(k^2)\) across a Segment Tree costs \(O(Q k^2 \log N)\), which is tight and messy. However, \(k \le 20\) is tiny! If we can simulate \(1\) unit of MCMF augmentation on \([L, R]\) in \(O(\log N)\) time, then \(k\) augmentations take only \(O(k \log N)\) per query.
  2. Step 2 (Explicit MCMF Network & Residual Edge Reversal): Build a 1D spine flow network for the query range \([L, R]\):
    • Each index \(i \in [L, R]\) is split into \(u_i \to v_i\) with capacity \(1\) and weight \(+a_i\).
    • Spine edges \(v_i \to u_{i+1}\) have capacity \(1\) and weight \(0\) (ensuring selected subarrays are vertex-disjoint).
    • Source \(S\) connects to every \(u_i\) (capacity \(1\), weight \(0\)) and every \(v_i\) connects to sink \(T\) (capacity \(1\), weight \(0\)). Sending \(1\) unit of flow from \(S\) to \(T\) picks a single subarray \([l_1, r_1] \subseteq [L, R]\) of weight \(\sum_{i=l_1}^{r_1} a_i\). What happens to the residual graph \(G_f\) after saturating \([l_1, r_1]\)?
    • Every forward arc \(u_i \to v_i\) (\(l_1 \le i \le r_1\)) is saturated and replaced by its reverse residual arc \(v_i \to u_i\) of weight \(-a_i\)!
    • If a subsequent SSP step pushes \(1\) unit of flow through a sub-interval \([l_2, r_2] \subseteq [l_1, r_1]\) using these reverse arcs of weight \(-a_i\), the flow on \([l_2, r_2]\) cancels back to \(0\), splitting \([l_1, r_1]\) into two disjoint subarrays \([l_1, l_2 - 1]\) and \([r_2 + 1, r_1]\) while increasing the total number of subarrays by \(+1\)!
    • Even better: since both forward arcs (outside \([l_1, r_1]\)) and reverse arcs (inside \([l_1, r_1]\)) simply contribute their signed weights to any candidate walk, every SSP augmentation is literally just a Maximum-Weight Subarray query on \([L, R]\) after negating the elements of previously chosen subarrays (\(a_i \gets -a_i\))!
  3. Step 3 (Segment Tree Node Contract): To support point updates, maximum-subarray queries on \([L, R]\) (returning both the sum and the exact endpoints \([l^*, r^*]\)), and range sign-flips \(a_i \gets -a_i\) on \([l^*, r^*]\):
    • Each Segment Tree node maintains both the Maximum Subarray (max_sum, max_pref, max_suff with their endpoint indices) and the Minimum Subarray (min_sum, min_pref, min_suff with their endpoint indices), plus the total_sum and a boolean lazy flip tag rev.
    • When rev is applied to a node, total_sum is negated, and the max_* tuples are swapped with the negated min_* tuples in \(O(1)\) time!
    • To answer a query (L, R, k): repeat up to \(k\) times:
      1. Query the maximum subarray \((w^*, [l^*, r^*])\) on \([L, R]\).
      2. If \(w^* \le 0\), break early (since we need at most \(k\) subarrays and \(C(f)\) is concave).
      3. Add \(w^*\) to ans, record \([l^*, r^*]\) on a rollback stack, and apply a range sign-flip flip(l*, r*) on the Segment Tree!
      4. After the loop, pop all recorded intervals \([l^*, r^*]\) from the stack and flip them back to restore the original array!
  4. Step 4 (Hand-Checkable Toy Trace): Suppose \(a = [4, -1, 5]\) and \(k = 2\).
    • Augmentation 1: Max subarray on \([1, 3]\) is \([1, 3]\) with sum \(4 - 1 + 5 = 8 > 0\). We add \(8\) to ans and flip \([1, 3]\), so the active array becomes \([-4, +1, -5]\).
    • Augmentation 2: Max subarray on \([1, 3]\) is now \([2, 2]\) with sum \(+1 > 0\)! We add \(+1\) to ans (total \(8 + 1 = 9\)) and flip \([2, 2]\), leaving \([-4, -1, -5]\).
    • Notice how picking \(+1\) at index \(2\) in Augmentation 2 cancelled the \(-1\) from Augmentation 1, leaving the two disjoint subarrays \([1, 1]\) (val \(4\)) and \([3, 3]\) (val \(5\)) of total sum \(9\)!

Part B: Tree-Structured Simulated MCMF via Mergeable Heaps ([ICPC WF 2018] Conquer the World & [UOJ #455] 雪灾与外卖)

What if supplies \(x_u\) and demands \(y_v\) live on the vertices of an edge-weighted tree \(T = (V, E, w)\) (\(N \le 2.5 \times 10^5\), \(w_e \ge 0\)), and routing \(1\) unit of flow from \(u\) to \(v\) costs the tree path distance \(\operatorname{dist}_T(u, v)\)?

The LCA Telescoping Identity

Root the tree arbitrarily at vertex \(1\), and let \(\operatorname{dep}(u)\) denote the weighted distance from the root \(1\) to \(u\). For any supply node \(u\) and demand node \(v\) whose Lowest Common Ancestor is \(l = \operatorname{lca}(u, v)\), their tree distance decomposes into:

\[ \operatorname{dist}_T(u, v) = \operatorname{dep}(u) + \operatorname{dep}(v) - 2\operatorname{dep}(l). \]

Notice that \(\operatorname{dep}(u)\) depends only on \(u\), \(\operatorname{dep}(v)\) depends only on \(v\), and \(-2\operatorname{dep}(l)\) is a constant shared by all pairs whose path peaks at \(l\)!

The Two-Sided Tree Regret Equations (FM-23 / H-23)

Suppose we process the tree bottom-up in post-order DFS. At each vertex \(l\), we maintain two mergeable min-heaps for the subtree \(T_l\):

  • \(H_X(l)\): candidate supply tokens inside \(T_l\) (initially storing key \(a = \operatorname{dep}(u)\) for each supply unit at \(u\)).
  • \(H_Y(l)\): candidate demand tokens inside \(T_l\) (initially storing key \(b = \operatorname{dep}(v) - M_{\infty}\) for each mandatory demand unit at \(v\), where \(M_{\infty} = 10^{12}\) forces all demands to be saturated).

When we are at \(l\) (after merging the heaps of all children of \(l\)), matching the minimum supply token \(a \in H_X(l)\) with the minimum demand token \(b \in H_Y(l)\) costs:

\[ \Delta = a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l). \]

If \(\Delta < 0\), we greedily match them at \(l\) and add \(\Delta\) to ans. Now, how could a later augmentation at a higher ancestor \(l'\) (\(\operatorname{dep}(l') \le \operatorname{dep}(l)\)) modify this match using the residual graph? There are two symmetric residual possibilities:

  1. A better supply \(a' \in H_X(l')\) outside \(T_l\) steals the demand \(b \in T_l\): Instead of matching \((a, b)\) at LCA \(l\) for cost \(a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l)\), we want to match \((a', b)\) at LCA \(l'\) for cost \(a' + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')\). The incremental change in cost at \(l'\) must be: \[ \big(a' + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')\big) - \big(a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l)\big) = a' + \underbrace{\big(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a\big)}_{\text{new } Y\text{-regret key } b_{\text{reg}} \in H_Y(l)} - 2\operatorname{dep}(l'). \]
  2. A better demand \(b' \in H_Y(l')\) outside \(T_l\) steals the supply \(a \in T_l\): Instead of matching \((a, b)\) at LCA \(l\), we match \((a, b')\) at LCA \(l'\) ( provided \(b\) itself was a regret token or non-mandatory, or if another supply simultaneously covers \(b\)). The incremental change in cost at \(l'\) is: \[ \big(a + b' - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')\big) - \big(a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l)\big) = \underbrace{\big(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b\big)}_{\text{new } X\text{-regret key } a_{\text{reg}} \in H_X(l)} + b' - 2\operatorname{dep}(l'). \]
ImportantTheorem 23.2 (The Dual-Heap Tree Regret Rule & Non-Interference Guarantee)

Whenever a supply token \(a \in H_X(l)\) and a demand token \(b \in H_Y(l)\) match at subtree root \(l\) with \(\Delta = a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l) < 0\):

  1. We must push both complementary regret tokens: \[ a_{\text{reg}} = 2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b \quad\text{into } H_X(l), \qquad b_{\text{reg}} = 2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a \quad\text{into } H_Y(l). \]
  2. Non-Interference Invariant: The two regret tokens \(a_{\text{reg}}\) and \(b_{\text{reg}}\) can never match with each other at \(l\) or at any ancestor \(l'\) of \(l\).

Proof of (2). At any ancestor \(l'\) of \(l\), we have \(\operatorname{dep}(l') \le \operatorname{dep}(l)\) (since edge weights \(w_e \ge 0\)). The hypothetical cost of matching \(a_{\text{reg}}\) with \(b_{\text{reg}}\) at \(l'\) is:

\[ a_{\text{reg}} + b_{\text{reg}} - 2\operatorname{dep}(l') = \big(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b\big) + \big(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a\big) - 2\operatorname{dep}(l') = -\underbrace{\big(a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l)\big)}_{< \, 0} + 2\underbrace{\big(\operatorname{dep}(l) - \operatorname{dep}(l')\big)}_{\ge \, 0} > 0. \]

Because their combined cost is strictly positive, the condition \(\text{cost} < 0\) is never satisfied for \((a_{\text{reg}}, b_{\text{reg}})\)! And if a third supply \(a'\) matches with \(b_{\text{reg}}\) and a third demand \(b'\) also matches with \(a_{\text{reg}}\) at \(l'\), the total accumulated cost is:

\[ (a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l)) + (a' + (2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a) - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')) + ((2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b) + b' - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')) = (a' + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')) + (a + b' - 2\operatorname{dep}(l')), \]

which is the exact cost of routing \(a' \to b\) across \(l'\) and \(a \to b'\) across \(l'\) (i.e., cancelling the double flow on the path \(l' \leftrightarrow l\))! \(\blacksquare\)

Run-Length Multiplicity Batching ([UOJ #455] 雪灾与外卖)

When node supplies \(x_u\) or capacities \(c_v\) can be up to \(10^9\) (as in [UOJ #455] 雪灾与外卖), pushing individual tokens one by one would take \(\Omega(\sum x_u)\) time. Instead, we store run-length compressed pairs (key, count) in \(H_X\) and \(H_Y\).

Why is the number of heap operations still \(O(N \log N)\)? Whenever we pop (a, cnt_a) from \(H_X(l)\) and (b, cnt_b) from \(H_Y(l)\), we match \(k = \min(\text{cnt}_a, \text{cnt}_b)\) units in a single \(O(1)\) batch:

  • At least one of the two popped tuples is completely exhausted (\(k = \text{cnt}_a\) or \(k = \text{cnt}_b\)).
  • Wait—what if we push a new regret tuple (2*dep(l) - b, k) into \(H_X(l)\) and (2*dep(l) - a, k) into \(H_Y(l)\) on every batch match? Notice that on a 1D line (UOJ #455), when sweeping left-to-right and processing a single deliveryman (\(x_i = 1\)) or a restaurant of capacity \(c_j\), all deliverymen stolen by restaurant \(j\) at coordinate \(y_j\) receive the exact same restaurant regret key \(-y_j + w_j\) (or all restaurants matched with \(x_i\) have count \(1\)), so we coalesce all \(k\) units absorbed by restaurant \(j\) into a single regret entry in \(H_X\)! Similarly, on a tree (Conquer the World), \(\sum y_v \le 10^6\) bounds the total number of unit matches directly.

23.4 Multi-Heap Residual State Automata & Flagship Case Study ([NOI2019] 序列 #183)

We now arrive at the crowning general methodology of Simulated Cost Flow: Multi-Heap Residual State Automata. Whenever \(N \le 2 \times 10^5\) items each have a small finite set of selection states and interact through a single global capacity bottleneck, we can compile the explicit MCMF network into a constant number of priority queues with lazy deletion.

Flagship Case Study: [NOI2019 / Luogu P5470] 序列 (Sequence) (#183)

Problem Statement

You are given two arrays of positive integers \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N\) and \(b_1, b_2, \dots, b_N\) (\(N \le 2 \times 10^5\), \(1 \le L \le K \le N\)). You must choose a subset \(I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) of size \(|I| = K\) from array \(A\) and a subset \(J \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) of size \(|J| = K\) from array \(B\) such that at least \(L\) indices are common to both subsets:

\[ |I \cap J| \ge L. \]

Maximize the total sum \(\sum_{i \in I} a_i + \sum_{j \in J} b_j\).

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

If \(L = 0\), we simply pick the \(K\) largest elements of \(A\) and the \(K\) largest elements of \(B\) independently. If \(L = K\), we must pick the same \(K\) indices for both, so we pick the \(K\) largest values of \(a_i + b_i\). For general \(0 \le L \le K\), the condition \(|I \cap J| \ge L\) is equivalent to saying that at most \(K - L\) pairs of chosen elements can be unaligned (\(i \ne j\))!

Step 2: Explicit Small-Graph MCMF Formulation & Vertex Automaton

Before touching a single heap, let us draw the exact MCMF network that solves the problem for small \(N\):

  • For each index \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), create a left node \(u_i\) (representing picking \(i \in I\), connected from source \(S \to u_i\) with capacity \(1\), weight \(a_i\)) and a right node \(v_i\) (representing picking \(i \in J\), connected to sink \(v_i \to T\) with capacity \(1\), weight \(b_i\)).
  • Aligned Pairs (Free of Bottleneck): For each \(i\), add a direct edge \(u_i \to v_i\) of capacity \(1\) and weight \(0\). Sending \(1\) unit of flow along \(S \to u_i \to v_i \to T\) selects both \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) at index \(i\).
  • Unaligned Pairs (Shared Bottleneck Hub \(P \to Q\)): Create two central hub vertices \(P\) and \(Q\) connected by a single bottleneck edge \(P \to Q\) of capacity \(K - L\) and weight \(0\). For every \(i\), add \(u_i \to P\) (capacity \(1\), weight \(0\)) and \(Q \to v_i\) (capacity \(1\), weight \(0\)). Sending \(1\) unit of flow along \(S \to u_i \to P \to Q \to v_j \to T\) selects \(a_i\) and \(b_j\) (\(i \ne j\)) and consumes \(1\) unit of capacity on \(P \to Q\)!

Now look at each index \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\). Depending on whether \(u_i\) is in \(I\) and \(v_i\) is in \(J\), index \(i\) is in one of 4 possible vertex states:

  • State 0 (0, 0): Neither \(a_i\) nor \(b_i\) is selected.
  • State 1 (1, 0): Only \(a_i\) is selected (so \(u_i\) is routing flow through \(u_i \to P\)).
  • State 2 (0, 1): Only \(b_i\) is selected (so \(v_i\) is receiving flow from \(Q \to v_i\)).
  • State 3 (1, 1): Both \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) are selected (always routed along the direct edge \(u_i \to v_i\) so as not to waste bottleneck capacity on \(P \to Q\)!).

Step 3: Enumerating All 5 Elementary Residual Augmenting Walks

We need to send \(K\) units of flow from \(S\) to \(T\), one unit at a time. Let free_rem denote the remaining residual capacity on the bottleneck edge \(P \to Q\) (initially free_rem = K - L). At each step, what are the elementary (simple) augmenting paths from \(S\) to \(T\) in the residual network \(G_f\)?

  1. Candidate Walk 1 (Direct Aligned Pair, \(\Delta\text{free} = 0\)): Pick an index \(i\) in State 0 and push flow along \(S \to u_i \to v_i \to T\).
    • State transition: State[i]: 0 -> 3.
    • Gain: \(a_i + b_i\). Bottleneck change: \(\Delta\text{free} = 0\).
  2. Candidate Walk 2 (Direct Unaligned Pair via \(P \to Q\), requires free_rem > 0, \(\Delta\text{free} = -1\)): Pick \(i\) in State 0 (maximizing \(a_i\)) and \(j\) in State 0 (maximizing \(b_j\)), and push flow along \(S \to u_i \to P \to Q \to v_j \to T\).
    • State transition: if \(i \ne j\), State[i]: 0 -> 1 and State[j]: 0 -> 2, consuming \(1\) free token (free_rem--). (If \(i = j\), this degenerates to Walk 1 with free_rem unchanged!).
    • Gain: \(a_i + b_j\).
  3. Candidate Walk 3 (Complete an \(A\)-only node \(j\) + Pick a fresh \(A\)-node \(i\), \(\Delta\text{free} = 0\)): Suppose index \(j\) is in State 1 (1, 0), meaning flow currently goes \(u_j \to P\). In the residual graph, we can traverse: \[ S \to u_i \to P \xrightarrow{\text{reverse of } u_j \to P} u_j \to v_j \to T! \] In words: we add \(b_j\) to complete index \(j\) into an aligned pair (1, 1), which frees up \(j\)’s slot into hub \(P\), and we hand that slot in \(P\) to a fresh \(A\)-node \(u_i\) (State[i] == 0)!
    • State transition: State[j]: 1 -> 3, State[i]: 0 -> 1.
    • Gain: \(b_j + a_i\). Bottleneck change: \(\Delta\text{free} = 0\) (since \(P \to Q\) flow does not change!).
  4. Candidate Walk 4 (Complete a \(B\)-only node \(i\) + Pick a fresh \(B\)-node \(j\), \(\Delta\text{free} = 0\)): Symmetrically, suppose index \(i\) is in State 2 (0, 1) (receiving flow from \(Q \to v_i\)). We traverse: \[ S \to u_i \to v_i \xrightarrow{\text{reverse of } Q \to v_i} Q \to v_j \to T! \] In words: we add \(a_i\) to complete index \(i\) into (1, 1), freeing \(i\)’s slot out of \(Q\), and hand that slot to a fresh \(B\)-node \(v_j\) (State[j] == 0)!
    • State transition: State[i]: 2 -> 3, State[j]: 0 -> 2.
    • Gain: \(a_i + b_j\). Bottleneck change: \(\Delta\text{free} = 0\).
  5. Candidate Walk 5 (Double Completion / Cross-Swap Regret, REFUNDS \(+1\) Free Token: \(\Delta\text{free} = +1\)!): Suppose index \(j\) is in State 1 (1, 0) (sending flow \(u_j \to P\)) and index \(i\) is in State 2 (0, 1) (receiving flow \(Q \to v_i\)), which implies there is positive flow on \(P \to Q\), so the reverse residual edge \(Q \to P\) is open! We can traverse: \[ S \to u_i \to v_i \xrightarrow{\text{reverse of } Q \to v_i} Q \xrightarrow{\text{reverse of } P \to Q} P \xrightarrow{\text{reverse of } u_j \to P} u_j \to v_j \to T! \] In words: we select \(a_i\) (turning \(i\) from (0, 1) into aligned (1, 1)) AND select \(b_j\) (turning \(j\) from (1, 0) into aligned (1, 1)), while pushing \(1\) unit of flow backward along \(Q \to P\)!
    • State transition: State[i]: 2 -> 3, State[j]: 1 -> 3.
    • Gain: \(a_i + b_j\).
    • Bottleneck change: \(\Delta\text{free} = +1\) (free_rem++)! Because two previously unaligned indices \(i\) and \(j\) both became aligned (1, 1), we refunded a free-pair token back to the pool!

Maintaining the 5 Priority Queues in \(O(N \log N)\)

To find the maximum-gain walk among Candidates 1–5 in \(O(\log N)\) time per step, we maintain 5 max-heaps with lazy state validation:

  • \(H_{AB}\): indices \(i\) with State[i] == 0, keyed by \(a_i + b_i\).
  • \(H_{A0}\): indices \(i\) with State[i] == 0, keyed by \(a_i\).
  • \(H_{B0}\): indices \(j\) with State[j] == 0, keyed by \(b_j\).
  • \(H_{B1}\): indices \(j\) with State[j] == 1 (\(A\)-only, needing \(b_j\) to become (1, 1)), keyed by \(b_j\).
  • \(H_{A2}\): indices \(i\) with State[i] == 2 (\(B\)-only, needing \(a_i\) to become (1, 1)), keyed by \(a_i\).

Look how each of the 5 candidate walks is just the top of one or two heaps:

  • Walk 1: \(\max(H_{AB})\)
  • Walk 2 (if free_rem > 0): \(\max(H_{A0}) + \max(H_{B0})\)
  • Walk 3: \(\max(H_{B1}) + \max(H_{A0})\)
  • Walk 4: \(\max(H_{A2}) + \max(H_{B0})\)
  • Walk 5: \(\max(H_{A2}) + \max(H_{B1})\)

Since each index \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) only ever advances monotonically in total selected count \((0 \to 1 \to 2)\), each node changes state at most twice! Thus there are at most \(2N\) heap pushes and \(2N\) lazy pops across the entire algorithm, running in \(O(N \log N)\) time with \(O(N)\) memory!

Broken-Gadget Counterexample FM-23: Omitting Cross-Swap Token-Refunding Regret Walks

CautionBroken-Gadget Counterexample FM-23 — What Breaks If You Omit Walk 5 (Token-Refunding Cross-Swap)?

A classic failure mode in contest implementations of [NOI2019] 序列 is to include Walks 1, 2, 3, and 4, while forgetting Walk 5 (\(\max(H_{A2}) + \max(H_{B1})\), which turns an \(A\)-only node \(j\) and a \(B\)-only node \(i\) into two aligned (1, 1) nodes and increments free_rem++), or forgetting to increment free_rem++ when Walk 5 is taken!

Explicit 4-Element Numerical Counterexample

Consider \(N = 4\), \(K = 3\), \(L = 2\) (so at most \(K - L = 1\) unaligned pair is allowed, i.e., initial free_rem = 1):

Index \(i\) \(1\) \(2\) \(3\) \(4\)
\(a_i\) \(100\) \(90\) \(10\) \(10\)
\(b_i\) \(90\) \(100\) \(85\) \(85\)
\(a_i + b_i\) \(190\) \(190\) \(95\) \(95\)

Let us trace the \(K = 3\) augmentation steps:

  • Step 1 (free_rem = 1):
    • Walk 1 (\(\max H_{AB}\)): \(a_1 + b_1 = 190\).
    • Walk 2 (\(\max H_{A0} + \max H_{B0}\)): \(a_1 + b_2 = 100 + 100 = 200\)!
    • Walk 2 wins! We pick \(a_1\) (State[1] = 1) and \(b_2\) (State[2] = 2), consuming our single free token: free_rem = 0, sum = 200.
  • Step 2 (free_rem = 0):
    • Since free_rem == 0, Walk 2 is disabled.
    • Walk 1 (\(\max H_{AB}\) on \(\{3, 4\}\)): \(a_3 + b_3 = 95\).
    • Walk 3 (\(\max H_{B1} + \max H_{A0}\)): \(b_1 + a_3 = 90 + 10 = 100\).
    • Walk 4 (\(\max H_{A2} + \max H_{B0}\)): \(a_2 + b_3 = 90 + 85 = 175\).
    • Walk 5 (\(\max H_{A2} + \max H_{B1}\)): \(a_2 + b_1 = 90 + 90 = 180\)!
    • With Walk 5 (Correct): Walk 5 wins with \(+180\) (sum = 380), completing both index \(1\) and index \(2\) into aligned (1, 1) pairs AND refunding the free token (free_rem becomes \(1\))! Then in Step 3 (free_rem = 1), Walk 2 or Walk 4 picks \(a_1, a_2, a_3\) and \(b_1, b_2, b_3\) (or \(a_3 + b_4 = 10 + 85 = 95\)), reaching the true global optimum: \[ I = \{1, 2, 3\},\; J = \{1, 2, 4\} \implies |I \cap J| = |\{1, 2\}| = 2 \ge L,\quad \text{Sum} = (100+90+10) + (90+100+85) = 475! \]
    • Without Walk 5 (Broken Gadget!): At Step 2, the algorithm takes Walk 4 (\(a_2 + b_3 = 175\), sum = 375, State[2] = 3, State[3] = 2, free_rem = 0). Then at Step 3, since free_rem = 0, it is forced to take Walk 3 (\(b_1 + a_4 = 90 + 10 = 100\)) or Walk 4 (\(a_3 + b_4 = 10 + 85 = 95\))— Wait: if it takes Walk 4 at Step 2 (sum = 375) and Walk 3 at Step 3 (sum = 475), what if \(a_3 = a_4 = 0\) and we only had \(K = 2, L = 2\) (or \(K = 2, L = 1\) with a third step that requires free_rem = 1, e.g., \(a_3 = 80, b_3 = 0\) and \(a_4 = 0, b_4 = 80\))? Let \(a = [100, 90, 80, 0]\) and \(b = [90, 100, 0, 80]\) with \(K = 3, L = 2\):
      • Step 1 (free_rem = 1): Walk 2 picks \(a_1 + b_2 = 200\) (free_rem = 0).
      • Step 2 (free_rem = 0):
        • With Walk 5: Picks \(a_2 + b_1 = 180\) (sum = 380) and refunds free_rem = 1! Then Step 3 uses free_rem = 1 via Walk 2 to pick the unaligned pair \(a_3 + b_4 = 80 + 80 = 160\), for a total of \(380 + 160 = 540\) (\(I=\{1,2,3\}, J=\{1,2,4\}, |I \cap J|=2\))!
        • Without Walk 5 (or without free_rem++): At Step 2, Walk 3 gives \(b_1 + a_3 = 90 + 80 = 170\) (State[1]=3, State[3]=1) and Walk 4 gives \(a_2 + b_4 = 90 + 80 = 170\). Even if it takes Walk 3 (\(+170\)), at Step 3 it cannot pick both \(a_3\) and \(b_4\) without completing both \(1\) and \(2\), getting stuck at \(200 + 170 + 100 = 470 < 540\) (or if it takes Walk 5 without incrementing free_rem++, Step 3 is forbidden from taking Walk 2 \(a_3 + b_4 = 160\) and only gets \(\max(80+0, 0+80) = 80\), scoring \(380 + 80 = 460 < 540\))!

Moral: Every reverse residual arc on a capacity bottleneck (\(Q \to P\)) corresponds to a transition that refunds capacity to the bottleneck. Never guess transitions by intuition—always trace the simple \(S \to T\) walks in \(G_f\)!

23.5 The 4-Step Simulated-MCMF Compilation Protocol

Whenever you suspect a problem requires Simulated Cost Flow (模拟费用流) or Regret Greedy (反悔贪心), never start by guessing what to push into a priority queue. Follow this strict 4-Step Compilation Protocol:

Step 1: Write Down the Explicit Small-\(N\) MCMF Network

Pretend \(N \le 50\) and draw the exact vertices, directed edges, capacities, and costs of a standard Minimum-Cost Flow (or Maximum-Weight Flow) network \(G = (V, E, u, c)\). Verify that:

  • \(G\) has no negative-cost (or positive-gain) cycles initially, so the Successive Shortest Path (SSP) algorithm is valid.
  • The total flow \(F\) is either unconstrained (stop when shortest-path cost \(\ge 0\)), small (\(F = K \le 20\)), reducible via WQS binary search (convex \(C(K)\) on a 1D spine), or bounded by \(O(N)\) unit steps.

Step 2: Classify Vertex States & Enumerate Elementary Residual Walks

Inspect what happens to \(G_f\) after \(t\) units of flow have been pushed:

  • 1D Spine / Tree Topology: If flow moves along a 1D line or tree, write the path cost between supply \(u\) and demand \(v\) using prefix potentials or LCA depth: \[ \operatorname{cost}(u, v) = \operatorname{pot}(u) + \operatorname{pot}(v) - 2\operatorname{dep}(\operatorname{lca}(u, v)). \] Verify that matching \((a, b)\) at \(l\) creates the two complementary residual keys \(a_{\text{reg}} = 2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b\) and \(b_{\text{reg}} = 2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a\).
  • Hub / Automaton Topology: If items interact through \(O(1)\) global hubs (like \(P \to Q\) in [NOI2019] 序列), list the possible local flow states of an item \(i\) (e.g., State 0, 1, 2, 3) and write out every simple directed walk from \(S\) to \(T\) in \(G_f\). Check whether any walk traverses a reverse hub edge \(Q \to P\) (which refunds a bottleneck token!).

Step 3: Compile Each Residual Walk Family into a Data-Structure Invariant

Assign one data structure to each family of residual transitions:

1D Relay Spine (CF 865D, CF 713C) Cancel sale at \(j\) (\(-1 \to 0\)) or buy fresh at \(j\) (\(0 \to +1\)) Single Min/Max-Heap; push \(p_j\) twice when matched \(O(N \log N)\)

If a transition path (like Walk 5 in [NOI2019] 序列) or a tie-breaker in WQS is missing, a 1,000-test random stress script on \(N = 6\) will catch it in under 0.5 seconds!

#177 (Stepping Stone 1) — [CF 865D] Buy Low Sell High & Its Slope-Trick Dual [CF 713C] Sonya and Problem Wihtout a Legend

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 23 — Stepping Stone 1 (SS-1) | Wave Provenance: 4B-P06 / W6-01 & W6-09
  • Judge URLs:
    • Primary (Simulated MCMF): https://codeforces.com/problemset/problem/865/D
    • Dual Companion (Slope Trick \(\ell_1\) Isotonic Regression): https://codeforces.com/problemset/problem/713/C

Constraints & Problem Statements

  • CF 865D (Primal Simulated Flow): Given stock prices \(p_1, \dots, p_N\) over \(N \le 3 \times 10^5\) days (\(1 \le p_i \le 10^6\)), each day you may buy \(1\) share, sell \(1\) share, or do nothing. Maximize total profit.
  • CF 713C (Dual \(\ell_1\) Isotonic Regression): Given array \(a_1, \dots, a_N\) (\(N \le 3 \times 10^5\), \(1 \le a_i \le 10^9\)), increment or decrement elements at unit cost to make the final integer array strictly increasing (\(b_1 < b_2 < \dots < b_N\)). Minimize \(\sum_{i=1}^N |b_i - a_i|\).

4-Step Compilation & Duality Synthesis

  1. Step 1 (Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal): In CF 865D, naive greedy matching \((p_i, p_j)\) blocks better future sales \(p_k > p_j\). In CF 713C, after shifting \(a'_i = a_i - i\) and \(b'_i = b_i - i\) to convert strict \(b_1 < \dots < b_N\) into non-decreasing \(b'_1 \le \dots \le b'_N\), standard DP \(f_i(x) = \min_{y \le x} f_{i-1}(y) + |x - a'_i|\) takes \(O(N^2)\) time. Both problems ask for \(O(N \log N)\) maintenance of a 1D spine residual/slope structure where each vertex has capacity \(2\)!
  2. Step 2 (Algebraic Representation):
    • For CF 865D: Matching buy \(i\) with sell \(j\) earns \(p_j - p_i\). Re-routing \(j \to k\) earns \((p_j - p_i) + (p_k - p_j) = p_k - p_i\), leaving day \(j\) with \(0\) net action so a second copy of \(p_j\) can still buy a fresh share.
    • For CF 713C: By LP duality, \(\min_{b'_1 \le \dots \le b'_N} \sum_{i=1}^N |b'_i - a'_i|\) is dual to sending flow \(f_i \in [-1, +1]\) along the spine with node values \(a'_i\). In Slope Trick, adding \(|x - a'_i|\) shifts slopes \(< a'_i\) by \(-1\) and slopes \(> a'_i\) by \(+1\) (inserting two copies of breakpoint \(a'_i\)), and taking the prefix minimum \(\min_{y \le x}\) pops the single largest breakpoint from the max-heap \(H\)!
  3. Step 3 (Heap Invariant):
    • CF 865D (Min-Heap \(H\)): On day \(j\), push \(p_j\) twice and pop \(\min(H)\), adding \(p_j - \min(H)\) to ans! (Notice: if \(\min(H) == p_j\), popping \(\min(H)\) and pushing \(p_j\) twice leaves one \(p_j\) in \(H\) and adds \(0\) to ans—identical to our if form in Section 23.1!).
    • CF 713C (Max-Heap \(H\)): At index \(i\), let \(x = a_i - i\). Push \(x\) twice into \(H\), add \(\max(H) - x\) to ans, and pop \(\max(H)\)!
  4. Step 4 (Failure-Mode Guard):
    • In CF 865D, never push \(p_j\) only once when \(p_j > \min(H)\), or middle relay days cannot buy their own share.
    • In CF 713C, always subtract the index \(a'_i = a_i - i\) first when strict inequality \(b_1 < b_2 < \dots < b_N\) is required!
Note3-Tier Progressive Hints (#177)
  • Hint 1: In CF 865D, if you bought at \(p_i\) and sold at \(p_j\), how much extra profit do you get if you change your mind and sell at \(p_k\) instead? In CF 713C, how do you transform \(b_1 < b_2 < \dots < b_N\) on integers into \(b'_1 \le b'_2 \le \dots \le b'_N\)?
  • Hint 2: In CF 865D, selling at \(p_j\) creates two residual possibilities at day \(j\) of price \(p_j\): cancelling the sale (\(-1 \to 0\)) and buying a new share (\(0 \to +1\)). In CF 713C, adding \(|x - a'_i|\) changes the derivative of \(f_i(x)\) by \(+2\) at \(x = a'_i\).
  • Hint 3: Both algorithms boil down to an unconditional 4-statement loop body per element: push \(x\) twice into the heap, add \(|H.\text{top}() - x|\) to ans, and pop \(H.\text{top}()\)!

#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

int main() {
    ios::sync_with_stdio(false);
    cin.tie(nullptr);
    int n;
    if (!(cin >> n)) return 0;
    priority_queue<long long, vector<long long>, greater<long long>> pq;
    long long total_profit = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        long long p;
        cin >> p;
        // Push twice: 1 for fresh buy (0 -> +1), 1 for regret token (-1 -> 0) if matched
        // If p is the minimum, popping it once leaves 1 copy and adds p - p = 0!
        pq.push(p);
        pq.push(p);
        total_profit += p - pq.top();
        pq.pop();
    }
    cout << total_profit << "\n";
    return 0;
}

4-Step Compilation (CF 280D)

  1. Step 1 (Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal): \(k \le 20\) per query screams Successive Shortest Path (SSP) simulated on a Segment Tree: each of the \(k\) steps extracts the single maximum subarray \([l^*, r^*] \subseteq [L, R]\) and negates its elements to represent reverse residual arcs!
  2. Step 2 (Intermediate Residual Representation): Saturating \(u_i \to v_i\) (weight \(+a_i\)) for \(i \in [l^*, r^*]\) replaces those arcs in \(G_f\) with \(v_i \to u_i\) (weight \(-a_i\)).
  3. Step 3 (Segment Tree Merging & Lazy Flip Contract): Define a struct Sub { int sum, l, r; } ordered by sum. Each tree node stores tot (range sum), mx, lmx, rmx (best subarray, prefix, suffix for maximum), mn, lmn, rmn (best subarray, prefix, suffix for minimum), and bool rev. Flipping a node negates tot and swaps (mx, lmx, rmx) with negated (mn, lmn, rmn).
  4. Step 4 (Failure-Mode Guard): Always push all flipped intervals \([l^*, r^*]\) onto a vector and flip them back in reverse order at the end of the query, even if the loop breaks early because mx.sum <= 0!

TipComplete C++ Solution (CF 280D — k-Maximum Subsequence Sum) 
// CF 280D — k-Maximum Subsequence Sum (Segment-Tree Simulated SSP)
// Time: O((N + Q * k) log N), Space: O(N)
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

struct Seg {
    int sum, l, r;
    Seg operator+(const Seg& o) const { return {sum + o.sum, l, o.r}; }
    bool operator<(const Seg& o) const { return sum < o.sum; }
};

struct Node {
    Seg tot, mx, lmx, rmx, mn, lmn, rmn;
    bool rev = false;

    void init(int idx, int val) {
        tot = mx = lmx = rmx = mn = lmn = rmn = {val, idx, idx};
        rev = false;
    }

    void apply_flip() {
        tot.sum = -tot.sum;
        swap(mx, mn); mx.sum = -mx.sum; mn.sum = -mn.sum;
        swap(lmx, lmn); lmx.sum = -lmx.sum; lmn.sum = -lmn.sum;
        swap(rmx, rmn); rmx.sum = -rmx.sum; rmn.sum = -rmn.sum;
        rev ^= 1;
    }
};

Node merge_nodes(const Node& A, const Node& B) {
    Node C;
    C.tot = A.tot + B.tot;
    C.lmx = max(A.lmx, A.tot + B.lmx);
    C.rmx = max(B.rmx, A.rmx + B.tot);
    C.mx = max({A.mx, B.mx, A.rmx + B.lmx});
    C.lmn = min(A.lmn, A.tot + B.lmn);
    C.rmn = min(B.rmn, A.rmn + B.tot);
    C.mn = min({A.mn, B.mn, A.rmn + B.lmn});
    C.rev = false;
    return C;
}

int n, q, arr[100005];
Node tree[400005];

void push_down(int u) {
    if (tree[u].rev) {
        tree[u << 1].apply_flip();
        tree[u << 1 | 1].apply_flip();
        tree[u].rev = false;
    }
}

void build(int u, int l, int r) {
    if (l == r) { tree[u].init(l, arr[l]); return; }
    int mid = (l + r) >> 1;
    build(u << 1, l, mid);
    build(u << 1 | 1, mid + 1, r);
    tree[u] = merge_nodes(tree[u << 1], tree[u << 1 | 1]);
}

void update_point(int u, int l, int r, int p, int val) {
    if (l == r) { tree[u].init(l, val); return; }
    push_down(u);
    int mid = (l + r) >> 1;
    if (p <= mid) update_point(u << 1, l, mid, p, val);
    else update_point(u << 1 | 1, mid + 1, r, p, val);
    tree[u] = merge_nodes(tree[u << 1], tree[u << 1 | 1]);
}

void update_flip(int u, int l, int r, int ql, int qr) {
    if (ql <= l && r <= qr) { tree[u].apply_flip(); return; }
    push_down(u);
    int mid = (l + r) >> 1;
    if (ql <= mid) update_flip(u << 1, l, mid, ql, qr);
    if (qr > mid) update_flip(u << 1 | 1, mid + 1, r, ql, qr);
    tree[u] = merge_nodes(tree[u << 1], tree[u << 1 | 1]);
}

Node query_range(int u, int l, int r, int ql, int qr) {
    if (ql <= l && r <= qr) return tree[u];
    push_down(u);
    int mid = (l + r) >> 1;
    if (qr <= mid) return query_range(u << 1, l, mid, ql, qr);
    if (ql > mid) return query_range(u << 1 | 1, mid + 1, r, ql, qr);
    return merge_nodes(query_range(u << 1, l, mid, ql, qr),
                       query_range(u << 1 | 1, mid + 1, r, ql, qr));
}

int main() {
    ios::sync_with_stdio(false);
    cin.tie(nullptr);
    if (!(cin >> n)) return 0;
    for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i) cin >> arr[i];
    build(1, 1, n);
    cin >> q;
    while (q--) {
        int type;
        cin >> type;
        if (type == 0) {
            int p, v;
            cin >> p >> v;
            update_point(1, 1, n, p, v);
        } else {
            int l, r, k;
            cin >> l >> r >> k;
            vector<pair<int, int>> flipped;
            int ans = 0;
            for (int step = 0; step < k; ++step) {
                Node res = query_range(1, 1, n, l, r);
                if (res.mx.sum <= 0) break;
                ans += res.mx.sum;
                flipped.push_back({res.mx.l, res.mx.r});
                update_flip(1, 1, n, res.mx.l, res.mx.r);
            }
            for (auto [fl, fr] : flipped) update_flip(1, 1, n, fl, fr);
            cout << ans << "\n";
        }
    }
    return 0;
}

struct Node {

    long long cnt;

};

int main() {

    vector<long long> x(n);
    for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) cin >> x[i];

    vector<Rest> r(m);

    for (int j = 0; j < m; ++j) {
        cin >> r[j].y >> r[j].w >> r[j].c;
        sum_c += r[j].c;
    }

        cout << -1 << "\n";
        return 0;

    int i = 0, j = 0;

    auto process_deliveryman = [&](long long pos) {
        auto top = pqY.top();
        pqY.pop();
        ans += pos + top.key;
        if (top.cnt > 1) pqY.push({top.key, top.cnt - 1});
        // Push regret token into pqX so a later restaurant can steal this deliveryman
        pqX.push({-2 * pos - top.key, 1});
    };

    auto process_restaurant = [&](long long pos, long long w, long long cap) {
        long long total_stolen = 0;
        while (!pqX.empty() && cap > 0) {
            auto top = pqX.top();
            if (top.key + pos + w >= 0) break;
            pqX.pop();
            long long take = min(cap, top.cnt);
            ans += take * (top.key + pos + w);
            cap -= take;
            total_stolen += take;
            if (top.cnt > take) pqX.push({top.key, top.cnt - take});
            // Push restaurant regret token into pqY so a later deliveryman can reclaim top.key
            pqY.push({-2 * pos - w - top.key, take});
        }
        if (total_stolen > 0) {
            // Coalesce all stolen deliverymen into ONE batched regret token in pqX!
            pqX.push({-pos - w, total_stolen});
        }
        if (cap > 0) {
            pqY.push({w - pos, cap});
        }

            process_restaurant(r[j].y, r[j].w, r[j].c);
            j++;
        }
    }
    cout << ans << "\n";
    return 0;

// Solution 1: [ICPC WF 2018 / Luogu P6943] Conquer the World
// Mergeable Leftist Heaps + Dual Regret Tokens
// Time: O((N + sum_Y) log(N + sum_Y)), Space: O(N + sum_Y)
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

const long long INF = 1e12;
const int MAX_NODES = 2500005;

struct LeftistPool {
    long long key[MAX_NODES], cnt[MAX_NODES];
    int lc[MAX_NODES], rc[MAX_NODES], dist[MAX_NODES];
    int tot = 0;

    int new_node(long long k, long long c) {
        int u = ++tot;
        key[u] = k; cnt[u] = c;
        lc[u] = rc[u] = dist[u] = 0;
        return u;
    }

    int merge(int x, int y) {
        if (!x || !y) return x | y;
        if (key[x] > key[y]) swap(x, y);
        rc[x] = merge(rc[x], y);
        if (dist[lc[x]] < dist[rc[x]]) swap(lc[x], rc[x]);
        dist[x] = dist[rc[x]] + 1;
        return x;
    }

    void pop(int& root) { root = merge(lc[root], rc[root]); }
} hp;

    if (y_dem[u] > 0) rootY[u] = hp.merge(rootY[u], hp.new_node(dep[u] - INF, y_dem[u]));

        dfs(v, u);
        rootX[u] = hp.merge(rootX[u], rootX[v]);
        rootY[u] = hp.merge(rootY[u], rootY[v]);

    while (rootX[u] && rootY[u]) {
        long long a = hp.key[rootX[u]];
        long long b = hp.key[rootY[u]];
        if (a + b - 2 * dep[u] >= 0) break;

        long long ca = hp.cnt[rootX[u]];
        long long cb = hp.cnt[rootY[u]];
        long long k = min(ca, cb);

        total_cost += k * (a + b - 2 * dep[u]);

        if (ca == k) hp.pop(rootX[u]);
        else hp.cnt[rootX[u]] -= k;

        if (cb == k) hp.pop(rootY[u]);
        else hp.cnt[rootY[u]] -= k;

        // Dual-Heap Regret Rule (Theorem 23.2): push both complementary regret keys!
        rootX[u] = hp.merge(rootX[u], hp.new_node(2 * dep[u] - b, k));
        rootY[u] = hp.merge(rootY[u], hp.new_node(2 * dep[u] - a, k));
    }
}

struct Entry {

    int id;

        hA0.push({a[i], i});
        hB0.push({b[i], i});

        while (!hAB.empty() && state[hAB.top().id] != 0) hAB.pop();
        while (!hA0.empty() && state[hA0.top().id] != 0) hA0.pop();
        while (!hB0.empty() && state[hB0.top().id] != 0) hB0.pop();
        while (!hB1.empty() && state[hB1.top().id] != 1) hB1.pop();
        while (!hA2.empty() && state[hA2.top().id] != 2) hA2.pop();
    };

        state[u] = new_st;

        else if (new_st == 2) hA2.push({a[u], u});

                       ? (hA2.top().val + hB0.top().val) : NEG_INF;

                       ? (hA2.top().val + hB1.top().val) : NEG_INF;

            set_state(u, 3);

                set_state(u, 1);
                set_state(v, 2);

// Solution 1: [NOI2017 / Luogu P3826] 蔬菜 (Vegetables)
// Reverse-Time Greedy Packing + Minimum-Value Rollback
// Time: O((N + P_max * M) log N + Q), Space: O(N + P_max * M)
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

const int P_MAX = 100000;

struct Veg {
    long long a, s, c, x;
};

int main() {
    ios::sync_with_stdio(false);
    cin.tie(nullptr);
    int n, m, q;
    if (!(cin >> n >> m >> q)) return 0;
    vector<Veg> v(n + 1);
    vector<vector<int>> appear(P_MAX + 1);

    for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i) {
        cin >> v[i].a >> v[i].s >> v[i].c >> v[i].x;
        if (v[i].c == 0) continue;
        long long last_day = (v[i].x == 0) ? P_MAX : min<long long>(P_MAX, (v[i].c + v[i].x - 1) / v[i].x);
        appear[last_day].push_back(i);
    }

    // Max-heap of (unit_value, veg_id). If veg_id > 0, it's the bonus first unit (a_i + s_i);
    // if veg_id < 0, it represents regular units of type (-veg_id) with value a_i.
    priority_queue<pair<long long, int>> pq;
    vector<long long> used_cnt(n + 1, 0);
    vector<pair<long long, long long>> chosen; // (value, count)
    long long total_units = 0, current_profit = 0;

    for (int day = P_MAX; day >= 1; --day) {
        for (int id : appear[day]) {
            pq.push({v[id].a + v[id].s, id});
        }
        int quota = m;
        vector<int> reuse_today;
        while (quota > 0 && !pq.empty()) {
            auto [val, raw_id] = pq.top();
            pq.pop();
            if (raw_id > 0) {
                // First unit of type raw_id (with bonus s_i)
                int id = raw_id;
                quota--;
                used_cnt[id]++;
                total_units++;
                current_profit += val;
                chosen.push_back({val, 1});
                if (v[id].c > 1) {
                    pq.push({v[id].a, -id});
                }
            } else {
                // Regular units of type id
                int id = -raw_id;
                long long avail = v[id].c - used_cnt[id] - (day - 1) * v[id].x;
                long long take = min<long long>(quota, avail);
                if (take > 0) {
                    quota -= take;
                    used_cnt[id] += take;
                    total_units += take;
                    current_profit += take * val;
                    chosen.push_back({val, take});
                }
                if (used_cnt[id] < v[id].c) {
                    reuse_today.push_back(id);
                }
            }
        }
        for (int id : reuse_today) {
            pq.push({v[id].a, -id});
        }
    }

    // Sort chosen batches by value ascending so we can rollback cheapest units first
    sort(chosen.begin(), chosen.end());
    int ptr = 0;
    vector<long long> ans(P_MAX + 1, 0);
    for (int p = P_MAX; p >= 1; --p) {
        long long cap = 1LL * p * m;
        while (total_units > cap) {
            long long drop = min(total_units - cap, chosen[ptr].second);
            current_profit -= drop * chosen[ptr].first;
            total_units -= drop;
            chosen[ptr].second -= drop;
            if (chosen[ptr].second == 0) ptr++;
        }
        ans[p] = current_profit;
    }

    while (q--) {
        int p;
        cin >> p;
        cout << ans[p] << "\n";
    }
    return 0;
}

// Matroid Exchange Graph + L2 Isotonic Regression via Parallel Binary Search & Min-Cut
// Time: O(N M + MinCut(N, N M) log(max_C)), Space: O(N M)
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

using ull = unsigned long long;
const long long INF = 1e18;

struct Dinic {
    struct Edge { int to, rev; long long cap; };
    int n, s, t;
    vector<vector<Edge>> adj;
    vector<int> lev, ptr;

    Dinic(int n, int s, int t) : n(n), s(s), t(t), adj(n), lev(n), ptr(n) {}

    void add_edge(int u, int v, long long cap) {
        adj[u].push_back({v, (int)adj[v].size(), cap});
        adj[v].push_back({u, (int)adj[u].size() - 1, 0});
    }

    bool bfs() {
        fill(lev.begin(), lev.end(), -1);
        queue<int> q;
        lev[s] = 0; q.push(s);
        while (!q.empty()) {
            int u = q.front(); q.pop();
            for (auto& e : adj[u]) {
                if (e.cap > 0 && lev[e.to] == -1) {
                    lev[e.to] = lev[u] + 1;
                    q.push(e.to);
                }
            }
        }
        return lev[t] != -1;
    }

    long long dfs(int u, long long pushed) {
        if (pushed == 0 || u == t) return pushed;
        for (int& cid = ptr[u]; cid < (int)adj[u].size(); ++cid) {
            auto& e = adj[u][cid];
            if (e.cap > 0 && lev[e.to] == lev[u] + 1) {
                long long tr = dfs(e.to, min(pushed, e.cap));
                if (tr == 0) continue;
                e.cap -= tr;
                adj[e.to][e.rev].cap += tr;
                return tr;
            }
        }
        return 0;
    }

    void solve_cut() {
        while (bfs()) {
            fill(ptr.begin(), ptr.end(), 0);
            while (dfs(s, INF));
        }
    }
};

struct LinearBasis {
    ull b[64] = {0};
    ull mask[64] = {0};
    int id_at[64] = {0};

    void build(const vector<int>& basis_ids, const vector<ull>& val) {
        for (int idx = 0; idx < (int)basis_ids.size(); ++idx) {
            int u = basis_ids[idx];
            ull x = val[u];
            ull m = 1ULL << idx;
            for (int bit = 63; bit >= 0; --bit) {
                if ((x >> bit) & 1) {
                    if (!b[bit]) {
                        b[bit] = x;
                        mask[bit] = m;
                        id_at[idx] = u;
                        break;
                    }
                    x ^= b[bit];
                    m ^= mask[bit];
                }
            }
        }
    }

    vector<int> get_rep(ull x) const {
        ull m = 0;
        for (int bit = 63; bit >= 0; --bit) {
            if ((x >> bit) & 1) {
                x ^= b[bit];
                m ^= mask[bit];
            }
        }
        vector<int> res;
        for (int idx = 0; idx < 64; ++idx) {
            if ((m >> idx) & 1) res.push_back(id_at[idx]);
        }
        return res;
    }
};

int n, m;
vector<ull> c_vec;
vector<long long> price, opt_w;
vector<pair<int, int>> dag_edges; // (u -> v) means w[u] <= w[v]

void parallel_bs(long long L, long long R, const vector<int>& nodes) {
    if (nodes.empty()) return;
    if (L == R) {
        for (int u : nodes) opt_w[u] = L;
        return;
    }
    long long mid = L + (R - L) / 2;
    int K = (int)nodes.size();
    vector<int> loc(n + 1, -1);
    for (int i = 0; i < K; ++i) loc[nodes[i]] = i;

    int S = K, T = K + 1;
    Dinic dinic(K + 2, S, T);
    for (int i = 0; i < K; ++i) {
        int u = nodes[i];
        // Gain of increasing w_u from mid to mid + 1: (mid - c_u)^2 - (mid + 1 - c_u)^2
        long long gain = 2 * price[u] - 2 * mid - 1;
        if (gain > 0) dinic.add_edge(S, i, gain);
        else dinic.add_edge(i, T, -gain);
    }
    for (auto [u, v] : dag_edges) {
        if (loc[u] != -1 && loc[v] != -1) {
            // If w_u > mid (loc[u] in S), then w_v >= w_u > mid requires loc[v] in S!
            dinic.add_edge(loc[u], loc[v], INF);
        }
    }
    dinic.solve_cut();
    vector<int> left_set, right_set;
    for (int i = 0; i < K; ++i) {
        if (dinic.lev[i] != -1) right_set.push_back(nodes[i]); // w_u > mid
        else left_set.push_back(nodes[i]);                    // w_u <= mid
    }
    for (int u : nodes) loc[u] = -1;
    parallel_bs(L, mid, left_set);
    parallel_bs(mid + 1, R, right_set);
}

int main() {
    ios::sync_with_stdio(false);
    cin.tie(nullptr);
    if (!(cin >> n >> m)) return 0;
    c_vec.resize(n + 1);
    price.resize(n + 1);
    opt_w.resize(n + 1);
    for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i) cin >> c_vec[i];
    for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i) cin >> price[i];

    vector<int> A(m), B(m);
    vector<bool> inA(n + 1, false), inB(n + 1, false);
    for (int i = 0; i < m; ++i) { cin >> A[i]; inA[A[i]] = true; }
    for (int i = 0; i < m; ++i) { cin >> B[i]; inB[B[i]] = true; }

    LinearBasis basisA, basisB;
    basisA.build(A, c_vec);
    basisB.build(B, c_vec);

    for (int y = 1; y <= n; ++y) {
        if (!inA[y]) {
            for (int x : basisA.get_rep(c_vec[y])) dag_edges.push_back({x, y}); // w_x <= w_y
        }
        if (!inB[y]) {
            for (int x : basisB.get_rep(c_vec[y])) dag_edges.push_back({y, x}); // w_y <= w_x
        }
    }

    vector<int> all_nodes(n);
    iota(all_nodes.begin(), all_nodes.end(), 1);
    parallel_bs(0, 1000000, all_nodes);

    long long ans = 0;
    for (int i = 1; i <= n; ++i) {
        long long diff = opt_w[i] - price[i];
        ans += diff * diff;
    }
    cout << ans << "\n";
    return 0;
}

23.7 Pre-Submission Checklist (FM-23 Audit)

Before submitting any Simulated Cost Flow (模拟费用流) or Regret Greedy (反悔贪心) solution, run through this 6-point verification audit:

  1. Double-Push Relay Check (CF 865D / CF 713C): If a single vertex \(j\) on a 1D spine can act as a sell (\(-1\)), a zero-net-position relay (\(0\)), or a fresh buy (\(+1\)), did you push \(p_j\) twice into the heap when \(j\) is matched?
  2. WQS Collinear Slope Tie-Breaking (CF 802O / PA 2013 Raper, FM-23 / H-24): In WQS binary search with a 2-type regret heap (dcnt = 1 for new pairs, dcnt = 0 for regret replacements), does your heap comparator break ties on equal key values using dcnt, and does that tie-breaker match < 0 vs <= 0 in your binary search condition?
  3. Dual-Heap Tree Regret Check (Conquer the World / UOJ #455, FM-23 / H-23): When matching supply \(a \in H_X(l)\) with demand \(b \in H_Y(l)\) at LCA \(l\) with \(a + b - 2\operatorname{dep}(l) < 0\), did you push both \(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b\) into \(H_X(l)\) and \(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a\) into \(H_Y(l)\)?
  4. Multiplicity Batching Check (UOJ #455): When capacities \(c_j \le 10^9\) are large, did you coalesce all units stolen in one restaurant’s while loop into a single (key, total_stolen) tuple in pqX so the amortized number of heap nodes stays \(O(N + M)\)?
  5. Token-Refunding Cross-Swaps in Multi-Heap Automata ([NOI2019] 序列, FM-23): Did you include the augmenting walk that completes an \(A\)-only node \(j\) and a \(B\)-only node \(i\) simultaneously (Walk 5: H_A2.top() + H_B1.top()), and did you increment free_rem++ when it is taken?
  6. Segment-Tree Rollback Integrity (CF 280D): Did you track both max_subarray and min_subarray in each Segment Tree node so sign-flips take \(O(1)\) per node, and did you undo every flip before the next query?

23.8 Chapter Summary & Bridge to Chapter 24

In this chapter, we unified three techniques that are often taught as disconnected tricks—Regret Greedy (反悔贪心), Slope Trick (Slope Trick / 保序回归), and Segment-Tree / Mergeable-Heap Augmentation—under a single mathematical theorem: Simulated Minimum-Cost Flow (模拟费用流).

  • Every regret key pushed into a priority queue is a reverse residual arc in \(G_f\).
  • Every range sign-flip on a Segment Tree (CF 280D) is the reversal of saturated spine arcs after an SSP step.
  • Every subtree heap merge at \(l = \operatorname{lca}(u, v)\) (Conquer the World) is the telescoping decomposition \(\operatorname{dep}(u) + \operatorname{dep}(v) - 2\operatorname{dep}(l)\) equipped with two-sided residual regret tokens \(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - b\) and \(2\operatorname{dep}(l) - a\).
  • Every multi-heap state automaton ([NOI2019] 序列) is the complete enumeration of simple \(S \to T\) walks across a constant-size hub network.

In Chapter 24 (Cost-Scaling, Capacity-Scaling, and High-Performance Exact MCMF Solvers), we close Part VI by studying what to do when the graph topology is not a 1D spine, tree, or constant-hub automaton, yet we still need blazing-fast exact Maximum Flow and Minimum-Cost Circulation on general graphs.
