Chapter 09 — Minimum Cut as Binary Variable Assignment & Project Selection

From Pseudo-Boolean Cut Monomials and Optimistic Regret Accounting (先拿后退) to Picard’s Maximum-Weight Closure, Topological Deadlock Pruning, and Hall-Shifted Closures

1. The Paradigm Shift — From Physical Pipes to Logical Binary Variables (\(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\))

In Parts I and II of this textbook, flow networks represented physical movement or explicit matching: units of fluid travelling along pipes from \(S\) to \(T\), trajectories stitching across a DAG, or disjoint pairings between left and right vertices.

Beginning in Part III (Chapters 09–13), we abandon the physical pipe metaphor entirely. Whenever we invoke a minimum \((S, T)\)-cut solver in this part of the book, no physical commodity is flowing through the problem’s domain. Instead, the directed network \(G = (V, E)\) is a compiled algebraic circuit whose \((S, T)\)-partitions \((V_S, V_T)\) are in one-to-one correspondence with truth assignments to \(N\) binary decision variables \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_N) \in \{0, 1\}^N\).

In the Japanese competitive programming literature (noshi91, maspypy, tatyam), this paradigm is celebrated as 燃やす埋める (Burn or Bury): every decision item \(i\) must ultimately be thrown into either the \(S\)-furnace (“burn”) or the \(T\)-pit (“bury”), and each directed edge \((u \to v, c)\) acts as a tax collector that charges penalty \(c \ge 0\) if and only if \(u\) lands in \(S\) while \(v\) lands in \(T\).

1.1 Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

How do you recognize a Minimum-Cut Binary Assignment or Project Selection problem when the English problem statement mentions no graphs, no pipes, and no flows?

  1. Binary Decision Universe: Every decision entity \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) must be assigned to one of two mutually exclusive states:
    • Select vs. Abandon, Build vs. Skip, Keep vs. Smash, Assign to Group A vs. Group B, or Fire vs. Retain.
  2. Overlapping Prerequisites or Coupled Pairwise Penalties:
    • Choosing entity \(u\) forces you to also choose (or pay for) entity \(v\) (\(x_u = 1 \implies x_v = 1\)), or choosing \(u\) without \(v\) incurs a penalty \(c_{uv} > 0\).
    • Why Tree / Bitmask DP Fails: In a general dependency DAG or interaction hypergraph, two projects \(u_1, u_2\) can share a common prerequisite instrument \(v\) (forming diamond subgraphs \(u_1 \to v \leftarrow u_2\)). A subtree DP cannot merge the profit of \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) without either double-counting the cost of \(v\) or tracking the entire frontier of already-purchased instruments (\(2^N\) states).
    • Why Greedy Fails: An instrument \(v\) may be unprofitable for any single experiment \(u_1\) (\(p_{u_1} < c_v\)), so a local greedy check rejects \(u_1\); yet when five experiments \(u_1, \dots, u_5\) share \(v\), their combined profit \(\sum_{k=1}^5 p_{u_k} \gg c_v\) makes the bundle overwhelmingly profitable.
  3. Constraint Bound Signature: \(N, M \in [50, 5 \cdot 10^4]\) entities/interactions with arbitrary large weights \(V_{\max} \le 10^9\).
    • Even when weights are \(10^9\), if finite capacities appear primarily on \(S\)-incident and \(T\)-incident arcs while internal dependency arcs carry capacity \(+\infty\), Dinic’s algorithm runs in strongly polynomial time (\(O(V^2 E)\) in general and \(O(E\sqrt{V})\) on bipartite closure networks), completely independent of \(V_{\max}\).

1.2 Step 2: The Cut Monomial \(c \cdot x_u (1 - x_v)\) & The Dual-Convention Rosetta Stone

Let \(G = (V, E)\) be a directed network with source \(S\), sink \(T\), decision vertices \(U = V \setminus \{S, T\}\), and non-negative edge capacities \(c: E \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \cup \{+\infty\}\).

An \((S, T)\)-cut is a partition \(V = V_S \sqcup V_T\) satisfying \(S \in V_S\) and \(T \in V_T\). By definition (Chapter 1), its capacity is the sum of capacities of all directed edges whose tail lies in \(V_S\) and whose head lies in \(V_T\):

\[\text{cap}(V_S, V_T) = \sum_{\substack{(u \to v) \in E \\ u \in V_S, \, v \in V_T}} c(u \to v).\]

Crucially, because the cut is directed, an edge \((u \to v)\) with \(u \in V_T\) and \(v \in V_S\) (crossing backward from \(V_T\) to \(V_S\)) contributes \(0\) to \(\text{cap}(V_S, V_T)\).

To turn \(\text{cap}(V_S, V_T)\) into an algebraic polynomial, we assign each vertex \(v \in V\) a binary indicator variable. In the global literature, two opposite conventions exist for mapping \(V_S\) and \(V_T\) to \(\{0, 1\}\). Mixing them up is the single most common source of reversed edges in contest implementations, so we state both explicitly and establish a permanent translation dictionary.

Convention A ([ZH] Selection / Closure Convention — Primary Convention of This Chapter)

For every vertex \(v \in V\), define \(x_v \in \{0, 1\}\) as the membership indicator of \(V_S\):

\[x_v = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } v \in V_S \quad (\text{"selected / active / kept on the } S\text{-side"}), \\ 0 & \text{if } v \in V_T \quad (\text{"abandoned / inactive / relegated to the } T\text{-side"}), \end{cases}\]

with fixed boundary constants \(x_S = 1\) and \(x_T = 0\).

Under Convention A, when does a directed edge \((u \to v)\) of capacity \(c \ge 0\) cross the cut from \(V_S\) to \(V_T\)? Precisely when \(u \in V_S\) (\(x_u = 1\)) and \(v \in V_T\) (\(x_v = 0\)). Over \(\{0, 1\}^2\), the unique polynomial that evaluates to \(1\) at \((x_u, x_v) = (1, 0)\) and \(0\) on the other three states \((0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1)\) is the product \(x_u (1 - x_v)\):

\[\boxed{\text{Directed Edge } (u \xrightarrow{c} v) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Cut Monomial } c \cdot x_u (1 - x_v)}\]

Substituting \(x_S = 1\) and \(x_T = 0\) into this single rule immediately yields all three atomic building blocks of a minimum-cut network:

  1. Source-to-Vertex Edge \((S \xrightarrow{c} u)\): Since \(x_S = 1\), the cut monomial is \(c \cdot x_S (1 - x_u) = c(1 - x_u)\). It charges penalty \(c\) if and only if \(x_u = 0\) (\(u \in V_T\)).
  2. Vertex-to-Sink Edge \((u \xrightarrow{c} T)\): Since \(x_T = 0\), the cut monomial is \(c \cdot x_u (1 - x_T) = c x_u\). It charges penalty \(c\) if and only if \(x_u = 1\) (\(u \in V_S\)).
  3. Internal Directed Edge \((u \xrightarrow{c} v)\): Charges penalty \(c \cdot x_u(1 - x_v)\) if and only if \(x_u = 1 \land x_v = 0\) (\(u \in V_S, v \in V_T\)).

Summing over all edges of \(G\), the capacity of the cut \((V_S, V_T)\) induced by \(\mathbf{x} \in \{0, 1\}^U\) is the Quadratic Pseudo-Boolean Cut Polynomial (a quadratic posiform in literals \(x_u\) and \(\bar{x}_v = 1 - x_v\)):

\[\Phi_G(\mathbf{x}) = \underbrace{\sum_{u \in U} c(S \to u)\,(1 - x_u)}_{\text{severed } S \to u \text{ when } x_u = 0} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{u \in U} c(u \to T)\,x_u}_{\text{severed } u \to T \text{ when } x_u = 1} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{(u \to v) \in E(U)} c(u \to v)\,x_u(1 - x_v)}_{\text{severed } u \to v \text{ when } x_u = 1, \, x_v = 0}.\]

Convention B ([JA] 燃やす埋める / Kolmogorov–Zabih Convention)

In Japanese treatises (noshi91, maspypy) and computer vision papers (Kolmogorov–Zabih 2004), the binary variable \(y_v \in \{0, 1\}\) is defined as the membership indicator of \(V_T\) (\(y_v = 1 - x_v\)):

\[y_v = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } v \in V_S \quad (y_S = 0), \\ 1 & \text{if } v \in V_T \quad (y_T = 1). \end{cases}\]

Under Convention B, an edge \((u \to v)\) is severed iff \(y_u = 0\) and \(y_v = 1\), producing the cut monomial \(c \cdot (1 - y_u) y_v\). Both conventions compile the exact same directed graph when we identify “selected” with \(V_S\) (\(x_v = 1 \iff y_v = 0\)).

Modelling Concept Convention A (\(x_S = 1, x_T = 0\): \(x_v = [v \in V_S]\)) Convention B (\(y_S = 0, y_T = 1\): \(y_v = [v \in V_T]\))
State Meaning of \(v \in V_S\) (Selected) \(x_v = 1\) \(y_v = 0\) (\(y_v = 1 - x_v\))
State Meaning of \(v \in V_T\) (Abandoned) \(x_v = 0\) \(y_v = 1\)
Internal Edge \((u \xrightarrow{c} v)\) \(c \cdot x_u (1 - x_v)\) (penalizes \(x_u = 1, x_v = 0\)) \(c \cdot (1 - y_u) y_v\) (penalizes \(y_u = 0, y_v = 1\))
Source Edge \((S \xrightarrow{c} u)\) \(c \cdot (1 - x_u)\) (cut when \(u \in V_T\)) \(c \cdot y_u\) (cut when \(u \in V_T\))
Sink Edge \((u \xrightarrow{c} T)\) \(c \cdot x_u\) (cut when \(u \in V_S\)) \(c \cdot (1 - y_u)\) (cut when \(u \in V_S\))
Implication (\(u \in V_S \implies v \in V_S\)) \(\infty \cdot x_u(1 - x_v) \iff (u \xrightarrow{\infty} v)\) \(\infty \cdot (1 - y_u)y_v \iff (u \xrightarrow{\infty} v)\)

1.3 Step 3: The Optimistic Baseline Transformation (先拿后退) & Unary Normalization

A minimum \((S, T)\)-cut solver computes \(\min_{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^U} \Phi_G(\mathbf{x})\) with non-negative edge capacities \(c(e) \ge 0\). Yet project selection and closure problems almost always ask to maximize a net profit function \(\Psi(\mathbf{x})\) containing both positive rewards and negative costs.

How do we turn \(\max_{\mathbf{x}} \Psi(\mathbf{x})\) into \(\min_{\mathbf{x}} \Phi_G(\mathbf{x})\) without introducing negative edge capacities? Through the two-step accounting identity known in Chinese OI literature (Hu Botao 2007) as 先拿后退 (Pre-Claim Positive Gains, Then Subtract Regret):

1. The Optimistic Baseline Identity (先拿后退)

Suppose each variable \(x_i \in \{0, 1\}\) has a weight \(w_i \in \mathbb{R}\) gained when \(x_i = 1\) (and \(0\) when \(x_i = 0\)). Partition the index set \(U\) into positive-weight items \(U^+ = \{i \in U : w_i > 0\}\) and non-positive-weight items \(U^- = \{i \in U : w_i \le 0\}\).

For every positive item \(i \in U^+\), apply the elementary identity \(x_i = 1 - (1 - x_i)\):

\[w_i x_i = \underbrace{w_i}_{\text{Optimistic Baseline}} - \underbrace{w_i (1 - x_i)}_{\text{Forfeited Profit (Regret)}}.\]

For every negative item \(j \in U^-\) (\(w_j \le 0\)), write \(w_j = -|w_j|\), so its contribution is already a non-negative subtraction:

\[w_j x_j = -\underbrace{|w_j| x_j}_{\text{Incurred Cost}}.\]

Summing over all \(i \in U\) yields the Fundamental Optimistic Baseline Identity:

\[\sum_{i \in U} w_i x_i = \underbrace{\sum_{i \in U^+} w_i}_{\text{Baseline } W^+} \;-\; \left(\underbrace{\sum_{i \in U^+} w_i (1 - x_i)}_{\text{Edges } S \xrightarrow{w_i} i} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{j \in U^-} |w_j| x_j}_{\text{Edges } j \xrightarrow{|w_j|} T}\right).\]

Because \(W^+ = \sum_{w_i > 0} w_i\) is a constant independent of \(\mathbf{x}\), maximizing the net gain \(\sum_{i \in U} w_i x_i\) is 100% equivalent to minimizing the non-negative regret-plus-cost sum inside the parentheses:

\[\boxed{\max_{\mathbf{x}} \text{NetGain}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{w_i > 0} w_i - \min\text{Cut}(S, T)}\]

Every term inside the minimum cut now has a strictly non-negative capacity and a crystal-clear economic meaning:

  • Pre-claim \(W^+ = \sum_{w_i > 0} w_i\) upfront: Pretend we simultaneously collect every positive reward in the problem.
  • Severing \((S \xrightarrow{w_i} i)\) when \(x_i = 0\) (\(i \in V_T\)): We decided not to select positive project \(i\), so we refund (forfeit) its pre-claimed reward \(w_i > 0\).
  • Severing \((j \xrightarrow{|w_j|} T)\) when \(x_j = 1\) (\(j \in V_S\)): We decided to select cost-bearing item \(j\), so we pay its purchase cost \(|w_j| \ge 0\).

2. General Two-State Unary Normalization (差値正規化 vs. ゲタを履かせる)

What if an item \(i\) has non-zero payoffs in both states: gaining \(u_i(1) \in \mathbb{R}\) when \(x_i = 1\) and \(u_i(0) \in \mathbb{R}\) when \(x_i = 0\) (or equivalently in minimization, paying unary penalty \(A_i(1 - x_i) + B_i x_i\) where \(A_i\) or \(B_i\) might be negative)? Two algebraic normalization recipes reduce any unary expression to non-negative cut edges:

  1. Recipe 1 — Minimum Extraction / \(O(1)\) 2-Edge Path Pre-Flow (差値正規化, Recommended Default): In minimization of \(U_i(x_i) = A_i(1 - x_i) + B_i x_i\), let \(m_i = \min(A_i, B_i)\). Since \((1 - x_i) + x_i = 1\), extract \(m_i\) into the global constant offset:

    \[A_i(1 - x_i) + B_i x_i = m_i + (A_i - m_i)(1 - x_i) + (B_i - m_i)x_i.\]

    Now both \(A_i - m_i \ge 0\) and \(B_i - m_i \ge 0\), and at least one of the two capacities is zero!

    • In maximization of \(u_i(1)x_i + u_i(0)(1 - x_i)\), let \(M_i = \max(u_i(1), u_i(0))\), add \(M_i\) to \(W_{\text{base}}\), and wire either \(S \to i\) of capacity \(u_i(1) - u_i(0)\) (if \(u_i(1) \ge u_i(0)\)) or \(i \to T\) of capacity \(u_i(0) - u_i(1)\) (if \(u_i(0) > u_i(1)\)).
    • Flow-Theoretic Meaning: If \(A_i > 0\) and \(B_i > 0\) are both positive, wiring both \(S \xrightarrow{A_i} i\) and \(i \xrightarrow{B_i} T\) creates an immediate 2-edge augmenting path \(S \to i \to T\) of capacity \(m_i = \min(A_i, B_i)\). Recipe 1 simply pre-pushes \(m_i\) units of flow along \(S \to i \to T\) in \(O(1)\) time before Dinic even starts!

  2. Recipe 2 — Uniform Constant Pedestal Shift (ゲタを履かせる / “Putting on Geta Clogs”): Add a large constant \(M > \max_i(|A_i|, |B_i|)\) via \(0 = -M + M(1 - x_i) + M x_i\), shifting both capacities to \(A_i + M > 0\) and \(B_i + M > 0\). While Recipe 1 is superior for routine unary normalization (avoiding capacity inflation), Recipe 2 is an indispensable theoretical weapon when we want to force a cardinality invariant across a bipartite graph (as we will see in Section 4.3 on CF 103 E — Buying Sets).

1.4 Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Broken-Gadget Counterexamples

Concrete Numerical Trace (\(N = 3\) Binary Variables)

Suppose we wish to maximize the score \(\Psi(x_1, x_2, x_3)\) over \(x_1, x_2, x_3 \in \{0, 1\}\) subject to the following rules:

  • Variable 1: \(x_1 = 1\) yields \(+10\); \(x_1 = 0\) yields \(-4\).
  • Variable 2: \(x_2 = 1\) costs \(-6\); \(x_2 = 0\) yields \(+5\).
  • Variable 3: \(x_3 = 1\) yields \(+8\); \(x_3 = 0\) yields \(0\).
  • Coupling Rules:
    1. Hard logical implication: if \(x_1 = 1\), then we must have \(x_2 = 1\) (\(x_1 = 1 \implies x_2 = 1\)).
    2. Soft directional penalty: if \(x_3 = 1\) while \(x_2 = 0\), we pay a penalty of \(9\).

Let us compile \(\Psi(x_1, x_2, x_3)\) mechanically using Step 2 and Step 3:

  1. Normalize Unary Terms via 先拿后退 (Recipe 1):
    • Vertex \(1\): \(10 x_1 - 4(1 - x_1) = 10 - 14(1 - x_1) \implies\) add \(+10\) to \(W_{\text{base}}\), wire \(S \xrightarrow{14} 1\).
    • Vertex \(2\): \(-6 x_2 + 5(1 - x_2) = 5 - 11 x_2 \implies\) add \(+5\) to \(W_{\text{base}}\), wire \(2 \xrightarrow{11} T\).
    • Vertex \(3\): \(8 x_3 + 0(1 - x_3) = 8 - 8(1 - x_3) \implies\) add \(+8\) to \(W_{\text{base}}\), wire \(S \xrightarrow{8} 3\).
    • Total Optimistic Baseline: \(W_{\text{base}} = 10 + 5 + 8 = 23\).
  2. Compile Coupling Terms:
    • Hard implication \(x_1 = 1 \implies x_2 = 1\) forbids \((x_1, x_2) = (1, 0)\), compiled as \(\infty \cdot x_1(1 - x_2) \iff\) directed arc \(1 \xrightarrow{\infty} 2\).
    • Soft penalty \(9\) when \(x_3 = 1 \land x_2 = 0\) is \(9 x_3(1 - x_2) \iff\) directed arc \(3 \xrightarrow{9} 2\).

2. Picard’s Maximum-Weight Closure Theorem (最大权闭合子图)

We now formalize the cornerstone theorem of Part III, introduced by Jean-Claude Picard in 1976 (“Maximal Closure of a Graph and Applications to Combinatorial Problems”, Management Science) and popularized in competitive programming by Hu Botao’s 2007 national team thesis.

2.1 Definition of a Closed Subgraph & Implication Compilation

ImportantDefinition 2.1 (Closed Subset / Closure of a Directed Graph)

Let \(G_{\text{dep}} = (U, E_{\text{dep}})\) be a directed dependency graph with vertex weights \(w: U \to \mathbb{R}\) (where \(w_u > 0\) represents a net profit and \(w_u < 0\) represents a net cost). A subset of vertices \(C \subseteq U\) is called closed (闭合子图) if no directed edge of \(E_{\text{dep}}\) leaves \(C\):

\[\forall (u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}, \qquad u \in C \implies v \in C.\]

The Maximum-Weight Closure Problem (最大权闭合子图) asks for a closed subset \(C^* \subseteq U\) maximizing the total vertex weight:

\[w(C^*) = \max_{\substack{C \subseteq U \\ C \text{ is closed}}} \sum_{u \in C} w_u.\]

Notice that \(C = \emptyset\) is always a valid closed subset with \(w(\emptyset) = 0\), so the maximum closure weight is always \(\ge 0\).

Algebraic-to-Graph Compilation of Picard’s Network \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\)

Let \(x_u = [u \in C] \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether vertex \(u \in U\) is included in the closed subset \(C\).

  1. Logical Implication Compilation: For each dependency edge \((u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}\), the closure condition \(u \in C \implies v \in C\) is the boolean implication \(x_u = 1 \implies x_v = 1\). Its unique forbidden state is \((x_u, x_v) = (1, 0)\), which is algebraically equivalent to:

    \[x_u(1 - x_v) = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Penalty } +\infty \cdot x_u(1 - x_v) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Directed Arc } (u \xrightarrow{+\infty} v).\]

  2. Unary Weight Compilation (先拿后退): Partition \(U\) into \(U^+ = \{u \in U : w_u > 0\}\), \(U^0 = \{u \in U : w_u = 0\}\), and \(U^- = \{u \in U : w_u < 0\}\), and let \(W^+ = \sum_{u \in U^+} w_u\).

    • For each \(u \in U^+\) (\(w_u > 0\)): wire \(S \xrightarrow{w_u} u\) (monomial \(w_u(1 - x_u)\), severed if \(u \notin C\)).
    • For each \(v \in U^-\) (\(w_v < 0\)): wire \(v \xrightarrow{|w_v|} T\) (monomial \(|w_v| x_v\), severed if \(v \in C\)).
    • For each \(z \in U^0\) (\(w_z = 0\)): no edge to \(S\) or \(T\) is needed (capacity \(0\)).

2.2 Complete Double-Inequality Proof of Picard’s Theorem (1976)

ImportantTheorem 2.1 (Picard’s Maximum-Weight Closure Theorem, 1976)

In the compiled network \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\) with vertex set \(V = \{S, T\} \cup U\):

  1. Bijection between Closed Sets and Finite Cuts: A subset \(C \subseteq U\) is closed in \(G_{\text{dep}}\) if and only if the partition \((V_S, V_T) = (\{S\} \cup C, \{T\} \cup (U \setminus C))\) is an \((S, T)\)-cut of finite capacity \(\text{cap}(V_S, V_T) < +\infty\).

  2. Exact Cut-Weight Identity: For every closed subset \(C \subseteq U\), the capacity of its corresponding cut \((V_S, V_T)\) satisfies:

    \[\text{cap}(V_S, V_T) = W^+ - \sum_{u \in C} w_u, \qquad \text{where } W^+ = \sum_{u \in U^+} w_u.\]

  3. Min-Cut Duality: Consequently, the maximum weight of a closed subset \(C^* \subseteq U\) equals:

    \[\max_{C \text{ closed}} \sum_{u \in C} w_u = W^+ - \text{MinCut}(S, T) = \sum_{w_u > 0} w_u - \text{MaxFlow}(S, T).\]

Complete Proof

Let \((V_S, V_T)\) be any \((S, T)\)-cut in \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\), and define \(C = V_S \setminus \{S\} \subseteq U\) (so \(U \setminus C = V_T \setminus \{T\}\)). Every edge of \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\) belongs to one of three disjoint families:

  1. Dependency arcs \(E_{\text{dep}} = \{(u \xrightarrow{+\infty} v)\}\): An arc \((u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}\) crosses from \(V_S\) to \(V_T\) if and only if \(u \in C\) and \(v \notin C\).
    • \((\implies)\) If \(C\) is closed, no edge \((u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}\) has \(u \in C\) and \(v \notin C\), so zero \(\infty\)-capacity arcs cross from \(V_S\) to \(V_T\). Since all other edges have finite capacity, \(\text{cap}(V_S, V_T) < +\infty\).
    • \((\Longleftarrow)\) Conversely, if \(\text{cap}(V_S, V_T) < +\infty\), then no \(\infty\)-capacity arc can cross from \(V_S\) to \(V_T\), which means \(\neg\exists (u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}\) with \(u \in C \land v \notin C\); hence \(C\) is closed.
  2. Source-incident arcs \(\{(S \xrightarrow{w_u} u) : u \in U^+\}\): Since \(S \in V_S\), the arc \((S \to u)\) crosses from \(V_S\) to \(V_T\) if and only if \(u \in V_T\), i.e., \(u \in U^+ \setminus C\).
  3. Sink-incident arcs \(\{(v \xrightarrow{|w_v|} T) : v \in U^-\}\): Since \(T \in V_T\), the arc \((v \to T)\) crosses from \(V_S\) to \(V_T\) if and only if \(v \in V_S\), i.e., \(v \in C \cap U^-\).

For any closed subset \(C \subseteq U\), summing the capacities of the severed edges across these three families yields:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{cap}(\{S\} \cup C, \{T\} \cup (U \setminus C)) &= 0 + \sum_{u \in U^+ \setminus C} w_u + \sum_{v \in C \cap U^-} |w_v| \\ &= \left(\sum_{u \in U^+} w_u - \sum_{u \in C \cap U^+} w_u\right) + \sum_{v \in C \cap U^-} (-w_v) - \sum_{z \in C \cap U^0} 0 \\ &= W^+ - \left(\sum_{u \in C \cap U^+} w_u + \sum_{v \in C \cap U^-} w_v + \sum_{z \in C \cap U^0} w_z\right) \\ &= W^+ - \sum_{u \in C} w_u. \end{aligned}\]

From this exact identity, we establish the double inequality:

  • Direction 1 (\(\max_{C \text{ closed}} w(C) \le W^+ - \text{MinCut}(S, T)\)): For every closed subset \(C \subseteq U\), its induced partition \((V_S, V_T)\) is a valid \((S, T)\)-cut, so \(\text{MinCut}(S, T) \le \text{cap}(V_S, V_T) = W^+ - w(C)\), which rearranges to \(w(C) \le W^+ - \text{MinCut}(S, T)\).
  • Direction 2 (\(\max_{C \text{ closed}} w(C) \ge W^+ - \text{MinCut}(S, T)\)): Since \(C = \emptyset\) gives a finite cut of capacity \(W^+ < +\infty\), the minimum cut \((V_S^*, V_T^*)\) has finite capacity \(\text{MinCut}(S, T) \le W^+ < +\infty\). By Part 1, \(C^* = V_S^* \setminus \{S\}\) is a valid closed subset of \(U\) with \(w(C^*) = W^+ - \text{cap}(V_S^*, V_T^*) = W^+ - \text{MinCut}(S, T)\).

Combining both inequalities completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

2.3 Constructive Closure Extraction via \(S_{\min}\) & Zero-Weight Tie-Breaking

In many problems (such as #065 Luogu P2762 — Space Flight Planning and #066 POJ 2987 — Firing), we must output not only the maximum net profit \(w(C^*)\), but also the explicit list of vertices in \(C^*\)—and in POJ 2987, if multiple closed subsets achieve the maximum weight \(w(C^*)\), we must output one with the minimum number of vertices \(|C^*|\)!

How do we extract \(C^*\) from Dinic’s final residual network \(G_{f^*}\)?

ImportantCorollary 2.1 (Canonical Minimal Closure via Residual Reachability \(S_{\min}\))

Let \(f^*\) be any maximum \((S, T)\)-flow in \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\), and let \(S_{\min}\) be the set of vertices reachable from \(S\) via directed paths of strictly positive residual capacity (\(c_{f^*}(e) > 0\)) in \(G_{f^*}\):

\[S_{\min} = \big\{v \in V : S \leadsto_{G_{f^*}} v\big\}, \qquad C_{\min}^* = S_{\min} \setminus \{S\}.\]

Then:

  1. \(C_{\min}^*\) is an optimal maximum-weight closed subset of \(G_{\text{dep}}\).
  2. Unique Inclusion-Minimal Property: For every maximum-weight closed subset \(C^* \subseteq U\), we have \(C_{\min}^* \subseteq C^*\). In particular, \(C_{\min}^*\) is the unique minimum-cardinality maximum-weight closure (automatically excluding all optional zero-net-gain bundles and isolated \(w_z = 0\) vertices without requiring any weight perturbation!).

Why \(C_{\min}^* \subseteq C^*\) Holds

By Chapter 2 (The Lattice of Minimum Cuts), the family of minimum \((S, T)\)-cuts is closed under intersection \(V_S^{(1)} \cap V_S^{(2)}\), and the residual reachability set \(S_{\min}\) is the unique inclusion-minimal source side among all minimum \((S, T)\)-cuts. Since Theorem 2.1 establishes a bijection \(C \leftrightarrow \{S\} \cup C\) between maximum-weight closures and minimum \((S, T)\)-cuts, \(C_{\min}^* = S_{\min} \setminus \{S\}\) is contained in every maximum-weight closure \(C^*\)!

[!CAUTION] Critical Implementation Trap in Closure Reconstruction (#065 Luogu P2762): Never attempt to reconstruct the chosen projects \(u \in U^+\) or instruments \(v \in U^-\) by checking whether the single edge \((S \to u)\) is unsaturated (\(c_{f^*}(S \to u) > 0\)) or whether \((v \to T)\) is saturated (\(c_{f^*}(v \to T) = 0\))!

  • A project \(u \in C_{\min}^*\) can have its direct arc \((S \to u)\) 100% saturated (\(c_{f^*}(S \to u) = 0\)) and still belong to \(S_{\min}\) by being reached from \(S\) through another project \(u'\) and a backward residual arc on \((u \to v, \infty)\)!
  • Conversely, an instrument \(v \notin C_{\min}^*\) can have \((v \to T)\) 100% saturated (\(c_{f^*}(v \to T) = 0\)) if the projects that pushed flow through \(v\) were ultimately cut on the \(S\)-side!

Golden Rule: Always reconstruct \(C_{\min}^*\) by running a full BFS/DFS from \(S\) in \(G_{f^*}\) traversing edges with e.cap - e.flow > 0 and collecting {v in U : vis[v] == true}.

3. Pre-Flow Topological Cycle Pruning & Mutual Dependency Traps

What happens when the prerequisite graph \(G_{\text{dep}} = (U, E_{\text{dep}})\) is not a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), but contains directed cycles \(u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots \to u_k \to u_1\)?

Before writing a single line of Dinic code, you must ask a fundamental modelling question about the physical rules of the problem: Are decisions made simultaneously as a static set, or sequentially one step at a time?

3.1 Simultaneous Set Bundles (Semantics A) vs. Sequential Causal Unlocking (Semantics B)

Semantics A: Simultaneous Set Selection (Cycle = Equivalence Super-Node)

Suppose the problem states: “Select a subset of items \(C \subseteq U\) all at once such that if \(u \in C\) and \((u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}\), then \(v \in C\).”

Under Semantics A, a directed cycle \(u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots \to u_k \to u_1\) of \(\infty\)-capacity arcs simply enforces the chain of implications \(x_{u_1} \implies x_{u_2} \implies \dots \implies x_{u_k} \implies x_{u_1}\), which is logically equivalent to:

\[x_{u_1} = x_{u_2} = \dots = x_{u_k} \in \{0, 1\}.\]

Either every vertex in the strongly connected component (SCC) is placed in \(V_S\), or every vertex in the SCC is placed in \(V_T\). Under Semantics A, Picard’s network \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\) works without any modification: if the cycle’s combined weight \(\sum_{i=1}^k w_{u_i}\) exceeds the cost of its external prerequisites, placing the entire cycle in \(V_S\) severs zero internal \(\infty\)-edges and is completely valid.

Semantics B: Sequential / Causal Unlocking ([NOI2009] 植物大战僵尸 — Cycle = Permanent Deadlock)

Now contrast Semantics A with #070 Luogu P2805 — [NOI2009] 植物大战僵尸 (Plants vs. Zombies):

  • Zombies enter each row from the right edge and walk left: to eat plant \((r, c)\), a zombie must first eat plant \((r, c + 1)\).
  • Furthermore, each plant \(v\) can shoot fireballs protecting a set of squares \(u_1, u_2, \dots\); to eat a protected square \(u\), zombies must first eat the protector plant \(v\)!

Here, eating a subset of plants \(C \subseteq U\) is valid if and only if there exists a sequential linear ordering (topological ordering) \((v_1, v_2, \dots, v_{|C|})\) of \(C\) such that when \(v_t\) is eaten at step \(t\), all prerequisites of \(v_t\) have already been eaten at steps \(< t\).

What if Plant \(u\) protects Plant \(v\) AND Plant \(v\) protects Plant \(u\) (\(u \leftrightarrow v\))?

  • Physical Reality (Semantics B): To eat \(u\), you must first eat \(v\); to eat \(v\), you must first eat \(u\). Neither plant can ever be eaten first! Furthermore, any third plant \(w\) that stands behind \(u\) (or is protected by \(u\)) can never be reached either, even if \(w\) has a positive score of \(+10^9\) and does not belong to any cycle itself!
  • Mathematical Closure Fallacy (Semantics A): As a static set, \(C = \{u, v\}\) has zero outgoing dependency edges leaving \(\{u, v\}\)! If \(w_u + w_v > 0\), Picard’s unmodified min-cut network happily places \(\{u, v\} \subseteq V_S\), severing zero \(\infty\)-arcs and collecting phantom points from an invincible mutual-protection loop!

3.2 Broken-Gadget Counterexample FM-09 & Why Tarjan’s SCC Deletion Also Fails

[!WARNING] Broken-Gadget Counterexample FM-09 — Unpruned Prerequisite Cycles & The Tarjan SCC Trap:

Consider a \(1 \times 4\) single-row lawn with cells \(u_1 = (0, 0), u_2 = (0, 1), u_3 = (0, 2), u_4 = (0, 3)\) (zombies enter from the right at \(u_4\)), with scores:

\[w(u_1) = +1000, \qquad w(u_2) = -10, \qquad w(u_3) = -10, \qquad w(u_4) = +50.\]

  • Row-entry prerequisites: \(u_4\) must be eaten before \(u_3\); \(u_3\) before \(u_2\); \(u_2\) before \(u_1\).
  • Attack protection: Plant \(u_2\) attacks square \(u_3\), so \(u_2\) must be eaten before \(u_3\).

Thus \(u_2\) and \(u_3\) form a deadlocked 2-cycle (\(u_2 \leftrightarrow u_3\)), while \(u_1\) (worth \(+1000\)!) is an acyclic singleton SCC that depends on \(u_2\). Look at what happens under three implementation attempts:

3.3 The Two-Phase Causal Pruning Algorithm (Kahn’s BFS + Picard Closure)

To solve any Sequential Causal Closure problem (Semantics B), always execute the following two-phase pipeline:

  1. Phase 1 — Build the Causal Prerequisite Graph \(G_{\text{pre}}\) (Protector -> Protected): Orient edges in the direction of physical unlocking order: add a directed edge \((v \xrightarrow{\text{unlocks}} u) \in E_{\text{pre}}\) whenever \(v\) must be activated/eaten before \(u\). Record in_deg[u] = number of prerequisites of \(u\).
    • Push all vertices with in_deg[v] == 0 into a queue \(Q\).
    • While \(Q\) is non-empty, pop \(v\), mark reachable[v] = true, and for each \((v \to u) \in E_{\text{pre}}\), decrement in_deg[u]; if in_deg[u] == 0, push \(u\) into \(Q\).
  2. Phase 2 — Build Picard’s Closure Network on reachable Vertices Only: Discard every vertex \(u\) with reachable[u] == false. On the surviving Induced DAG \(U_{\text{valid}} = \{u \in U : \text{reachable}[u]\}\):
    • Wire \(S \xrightarrow{w_u} u\) for \(u \in U_{\text{valid}}\) with \(w_u > 0\) (adding \(w_u\) to \(W^+\)).
    • Wire \(u \xrightarrow{|w_u|} T\) for \(u \in U_{\text{valid}}\) with \(w_u < 0\).
    • For every \((v \xrightarrow{\text{unlocks}} u) \in E_{\text{pre}}\) with \(u, v \in U_{\text{valid}}\), reverse the edge to form the logical implication \((u \xrightarrow{\text{requires}, \, +\infty} v)\) in the cut network!

4. Non-Graph Closure Archetypes — Divisibility, Interval-Triangle, and Hall-Shifted Closures

The true power of Picard’s theorem in competitive programming is that the dependency graph \(G_{\text{dep}}\) rarely comes given in the input. Instead, it lies hidden inside number-theoretic divisibility, contiguous array intervals, or Hall’s Marriage Theorem. Let us dissect the three canonical non-graph closure archetypes.

4.1 Archetype 1: Divisibility Poset Closure & Complemented Conventions (ARC 085 E — MUL)

In #068 AtCoder ARC 085 E — MUL, we are given \(N \le 100\) gems indexed \(1, \dots, N\) with signed values \(a_i \in [-10^9, 10^9]\). We may perform the following operation any number of times:

  • Choose a positive integer \(d \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) and smash all gems whose index is a multiple of \(d\) (\(d, 2d, 3d, \dots\)).
  • Maximize the sum of values of the remaining (unsmashed) gems.

Step 1 & Step 2: Why Transitivity of Divisibility Turns Button Choices into Gem Closure

At first glance, the decisions are which buttons \(d \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) to press, while the payoffs belong to which gems \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) survive. Why don’t we need separate vertices for buttons and gems?

Suppose gem \(i\) is smashed by pressing some divisor button \(d \mid i\). Because divisibility is transitive (\(d \mid i \land i \mid j \implies d \mid j\)), that very same button \(d\) already smashes every multiple \(j = k i\) of \(i\)! Conversely, if gem \(i\) is not smashed, then button \(i\) was certainly not pressed. Therefore:

\[\text{A subset of gems } D \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\} \text{ can be the set of smashed gems} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \forall i \in D, \; \forall k \ge 2 \text{ with } ki \le N, \; ki \in D.\]

Taking the contrapositive for the complementary set of kept (surviving) gems \(K = \{1, \dots, N\} \setminus D\):

\[\boxed{j \text{ is kept } (j \in K) \;\land\; i \mid j \quad \Longrightarrow \quad i \text{ is kept } (i \in K)}\]

[!IMPORTANT] Sanity-Checking the Implication Direction in ARC 085 E: Can we keep gem \(1\) and smash gem \(2\)? Yes—just press button \(2\)! So \(1 \in K\) does not imply \(2 \in K\). Can we smash gem \(1\) and keep gem \(2\)? No—pressing button \(1\) smashes every gem! Thus keeping a multiple \(j\) (\(x_j = 1\)) requires keeping all of its divisors \(i \mid j\) (\(x_i = 1\)).

  • Convention 1 (\(x_i = 1 \iff i \in V_S\) means Gem \(i\) is KEPT):
    • Implication \(x_j = 1 \implies x_i = 1\) for \(i \mid j\) compiles to directed arc \(j \xrightarrow{+\infty} i\).
    • Positive gem \(a_i > 0\): wire \(S \xrightarrow{a_i} i\) (pre-claim \(a_i\) in \(W^+\); forfeited if \(i \in V_T\) is smashed).
    • Negative gem \(a_i < 0\): wire \(i \xrightarrow{|a_i|} T\) (paid if \(i \in V_S\) is kept).
  • Convention 2 (\(y_i = 1 \iff i \in V_S\) means Gem \(i\) is SMASHED):
    • Implication \(y_i = 1 \implies y_j = 1\) for \(i \mid j\) compiles to directed arc \(i \xrightarrow{+\infty} j\).
    • Positive gem \(a_i > 0\): wire \(i \xrightarrow{a_i} T\) (paid if \(i \in V_S\) is smashed).
    • Negative gem \(a_i < 0\): wire \(S \xrightarrow{|a_i|} i\) (forfeited if \(i \in V_T\) is kept).

Notice that Convention 2 is the exact graph transpose (with \(S\) and \(T\) swapped) of Convention 1, so both networks have the exact same minimum cut capacity! Moreover, by transitivity, we only need \(\infty\)-edges between \(i\) and \(i \cdot p\) for primes \(p\), reducing the edge count from \(O(N \log N)\) to \(O(N \log \log N)\).

4.2 Archetype 2: Interval-Triangle Inclusion DAGs ([SHOI2017] 寿司餐厅)

In #071 Luogu P3749 — [SHOI2017] 寿司餐厅 (Sushi Restaurant), \(N \le 100\) sushi plates sit in a row with type codes \(a_1, \dots, a_N \in [1, 1000]\).

  • Choosing a contiguous interval of plates \([l, r]\) (\(1 \le l \le r \le N\)) grants a signed satisfaction score \(d_{l,r} \in \mathbb{Z}\), and automatically includes all sub-intervals \([l', r'] \subseteq [l, r]\).
  • Eating \(c \ge 1\) plates of sushi type \(x\) incurs a total cost of \(m x^2 + c x\) (where \(m \in \{0, 1\}\) is a constant).

How do we compile this seemingly nonlinear, \(O(N^4)\)-dependency problem into a fast closure network?

  1. Transitive Pyramid Compression (\(O(N^4) \to 2\) Edges per Interval): There are \(\binom{N+1}{2} \approx 5050\) intervals \([l, r]\). If we wired \([l, r] \xrightarrow{\infty} [l', r']\) for every sub-interval \([l', r'] \subseteq [l, r]\), we would create \(\approx \frac{N^4}{24} > 4 \cdot 10^6\) edges. Instead, observe that for any interval \([l, r]\) of length \(\ge 2\) (\(l < r\)), every proper sub-interval \([l', r'] \subsetneq [l, r]\) is contained in either \([l + 1, r]\) or \([l, r - 1]\)! By induction on interval length \(L = r - l + 1\):

    \[x_{l, r} = 1 \implies x_{l', r'} = 1 \; (\forall [l', r'] \subseteq [l, r]) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \begin{cases} [l, r] \xrightarrow{+\infty} [l + 1, r], \text{ and} \\ [l, r] \xrightarrow{+\infty} [l, r - 1]. \end{cases}\]

    Just two \(\infty\)-arcs per interval compress the entire inclusion poset into \(2\binom{N}{2} \approx 10^4\) directed edges!

  2. Decoupling the Cost \(m x^2 + c x\) into Singleton Intervals \([i, i]\) and Type Hubs \(\text{Type}_x\):

    • Linear term \(c \cdot x\) (\(a_i\) per plate): Plate \(i\) is eaten if and only if the length-\(1\) singleton interval \([i, i]\) is selected! Thus we simply absorb the per-plate cost \(-a_i\) directly into the singleton node’s net weight: \(w([i, i]) = d_{i, i} - a_i\).

    • Fixed activation tax \(m \cdot x^2\) (paid once if \(c \ge 1\)): Create one auxiliary vertex \(\text{Type}_x\) of weight \(-m x^2\) (wired as \(\text{Type}_x \xrightarrow{m x^2} T\)) for each distinct sushi code \(x\), and wire every singleton interval \([i, i]\) to its type hub:

      \[[i, i] \xrightarrow{+\infty} \text{Type}_{a_i}.\]

    If any plate \(i\) of type \(x\) is eaten (\([i, i] \in V_S\)), the \(\infty\)-arc forces \(\text{Type}_x \in V_S\), severing \(\text{Type}_x \xrightarrow{m x^2} T\) exactly once regardless of how many plates \(c\) of type \(x\) are eaten!

4.3 Archetype 3: Hall-Shifted Bipartite Closures (CF 103 E — Buying Sets)

In #072 Codeforces 103 E — Buying Sets, we are given \(N \le 300\) subsets \(A_1, \dots, A_N \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) with purchase costs \(w_i \in [-10^6, 10^6]\) (note that \(w_i < 0\) means buying set \(A_i\) yields a net profit of \(-w_i > 0\)). The problem guarantees a crucial structural property:

  • Hall Guarantee: For every \(k \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) and every subcollection of \(k\) sets \(I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) (\(|I| = k\)), their union \(N(I) = \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i\) has size at least \(k\): \(|N(I)| \ge |I|\).
  • Task: Choose a subcollection \(I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) whose union has size exactly \(|I|\) (\(|N(I)| = |I|\)), minimizing the total cost \(\sum_{i \in I} w_i\) (or maximizing the net profit \(\sum_{i \in I} (-w_i)\)).

Why is the equality constraint \(|N(I)| = |I|\) tractable here when exact-cardinality union problems are normally NP-hard? Because the Hall guarantee \(|N(I)| \ge |I|\) means that no subset \(I\) can ever have \(|N(I)| < |I|\)! Every subset \(I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) satisfies:

\[|N(I)| - |I| \ge 0, \qquad \text{with equality } |N(I)| - |I| = 0 \iff |N(I)| = |I|!\]

This unlocks two dual compilations into Picard’s Maximum-Weight Closure:

Method 1: Uniform Pedestal Shift +M (ゲタを履かせる on \(2N\) Vertices)

Model the natural bipartite closure where choosing set \(i \in I\) forces choosing all elements \(j \in A_i\) (\(i \xrightarrow{+\infty} j\)). To forbid any closed selection \((I, J)\) (\(N(I) \subseteq J\)) with \(|J| > |I|\), choose a huge constant pedestal \(M = 10^9 > N \max_i |w_i|\):

  • Shift every set \(i\)’s closure weight by \(+M\): \(W(\text{Set}_i) = M - w_i > 0\) (wired as \(S \xrightarrow{M - w_i} \text{Set}_i\)).
  • Assign every element \(j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) a closure weight of \(-M < 0\) (wired as \(\text{Elem}_j \xrightarrow{M} T\)).

For any closed selection of sets \(I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) and elements \(J \supseteq N(I)\), the total closure weight is:

\[W(I, J) = \sum_{i \in I} (M - w_i) - \sum_{j \in J} M = -\underbrace{\big(|J| - |I|\big)}_{\ge |N(I)| - |I| \ge 0} M + \sum_{i \in I} (-w_i).\]

Because \(|J| \ge |N(I)| \ge |I|\) by Hall’s condition:

  • If \(|J| > |I|\), then \(|J| - |I| \ge 1\), so \(W(I, J) \le -M + N \max |w_i| < 0 = W(\emptyset, \emptyset)\)!
  • Therefore, any maximum-weight closure \((I^*, J^*)\) must have \(|J^*| = |N(I^*)| = |I^*|\), where the \(-M(|J^*| - |I^*|)\) penalty vanishes to \(0\) and \(W(I^*, J^*) = \sum_{i \in I^*} (-w_i)\)!

Method 2: Hall Perfect-Matching Bijection (\(N\)-Vertex Closure)

By Hall’s Marriage Theorem (Chapter 8), \(|N(I)| \ge |I|\) for all \(I\) implies the existence of a perfect matching \(M: \{1, \dots, N\} \to \{1, \dots, N\}\) with \(M(i) \in A_i\) for each \(i\).

If we first find \(M\) via Hopcroft–Karp / Dinic and identify each element \(e = M(j)\) with its matched set \(j\), then for any subset of sets \(I \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\), the matched partners \(\{M(i) : i \in I\}\) already contribute \(|I|\) distinct elements to \(N(I)\). Hence \(|N(I)| = |I|\) holds if and only if \(N(I)\) contains no elements matched outside \(I\):

\[|N(I)| = |I| \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \forall i \in I, \; \forall e \in A_i, \quad M^{-1}(e) \in I!\]

This collapses the problem to an ordinary unweighted-pedestal Maximum-Weight Closure on just \(N \le 300\) vertices with weights \(-w_i\) and dependency arcs \(i \xrightarrow{+\infty} M^{-1}(e)\) for all \(e \in A_i\)!

Purchasing instrument \(j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) costs \(c_j > 0\), and once purchased, instrument \(j\) can be shared by any number of experiments at no extra cost. NoteHint 1 — Formulating Shared Instrument Costs as Implications

Why does greedy selection fail? Because an expensive instrument \(j\) might be required by three different experiments \(i_1, i_2, i_3\), none of which can afford \(c_j\) alone, but whose combined revenue \(p_{i_1} + p_{i_2} + p_{i_3} > c_j\) is profitable. NoteHint 2 — Optimistic Baseline & Bipartite Closure Edges

Pre-claim all experiment revenues \(W^+ = \sum_{i=1}^M p_i\). Then every unchosen experiment \(i\) (\(x_i = 0\)) forfeits \(p_i(1 - x_i)\), every purchased instrument \(j\) (\(y_j = 1\)) pays \(c_j y_j\), and each requirement \(j \in I_i\) enforces \(\infty \cdot x_i(1 - y_j)\). Wire \(S \xrightarrow{p_i} \text{Exp}_i \xrightarrow{\infty} \text{Inst}_j \xrightarrow{c_j} T\).

NoteHint 3 — Reconstructing the Chosen Set & Zero-Gain Tie-Breaking

After running Dinic to termination, do not check which individual edges \((S \to \text{Exp}_i)\) or \((\text{Inst}_j \to T)\) are saturated (Section 2.3)! Every experiment and instrument visited by this BFS belongs to the canonical minimal closure \(C_{\min}^* = S_{\min} \setminus \{S\}\).

// Complete Model Compilation & Residual Closure Extraction for #065 (Luogu P2762)
// Assumes standard Dinic<long long> with `dinic.add_edge(u, v, cap)`, `dinic.max_flow()`,
// and `dinic.get_S_min()` returning vector<bool> of vertices reachable from S via c_f > 0.
void solve_space_flight(istream& in, ostream& out) {
    int M, N;
    if (!(in >> M >> N)) return;
    string line;
    getline(in, line); // consume trailing newline after N

    int S = 0, T = M + N + 1;
    const long long INF = 1e15;
    Dinic<long long> dinic(T + 1, S, T);
    long long W_plus = 0;

    for (int i = 1; i <= M; ++i) {
        getline(in, line);
        stringstream ss(line);
        long long p;
        ss >> p;
        W_plus += p;
        dinic.add_edge(S, i, p);
        int j;
        while (ss >> j) {
            dinic.add_edge(i, M + j, INF);
        }
    }
    for (int j = 1; j <= N; ++j) {
        long long c;
        in >> c;
        dinic.add_edge(M + j, T, c);
    }

    long long min_cut = dinic.max_flow();
    vector<bool> vis_S = dinic.get_S_min();

    bool first = true;
    for (int i = 1; i <= M; ++i) {
        if (vis_S[i]) {
            if (!first) out << ' ';
            out << i;
            first = false;
        }
    }
    out << '\n';

    first = true;
    for (int j = 1; j <= N; ++j) {
        if (vis_S[M + j]) {
            if (!first) out << ' ';
            out << j;
            first = false;
        }
    }
    out << '\n' << (W_plus - min_cut) << '\n';
}

Problem #066 — POJ 2987: Firing (Minimum-Cardinality Maximum-Weight Closure)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 09 — Stepping Stone 2 (SS-2) | Judge URL: POJ 2987
  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 5000\) employees, \(0 \le M \le 60000\) direct-subordinate relations, signed firing benefits \(w_i \in [-10^7, 10^7]\); Time Limit \(5.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: A company is downsizing. Firing employee \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) yields a signed net benefit \(w_i \in \mathbb{Z}\) (positive if employee \(i\)’s salary exceeds their productivity, negative if they are a net contributor) and forces you to also fire all direct and indirect subordinates of \(i\). Find a valid subset of employees \(C \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) to fire that maximizes the total net benefit \(\sum_{i \in C} w_i\); if multiple subsets achieve the maximum benefit, output the minimum number of fired employees \(|C|\) along with the maximum benefit.
NoteHint 1 — Why Subordinate Chains Form a Standard Closure

Firing \(u\) forces firing \(v\) (\(x_u = 1 \implies x_v = 1\)). Notice that even though the problem statement says “direct and indirect subordinates”, you do not need to compute the transitive closure of the hierarchy! Wiring \(u \xrightarrow{+\infty} v\) for each of the \(M\) direct-subordinate edges automatically enforces indirect subordinates by cut transitivity.

NoteHint 2 — Method A: Algebraic Lexicographic Weight Scaling (\(w'_i = B \cdot w_i - 1\))

How do you maximize \(\sum_{i \in C} w_i\) as the primary objective and minimize \(|C| = \sum_{i \in C} 1\) as the secondary tie-breaker? Multiply every weight \(w_i\) by a base \(B = N + 1\) and subtract \(1\):

\[w'_i = (N + 1) w_i - 1.\]

Then for any closed set \(C\), \(\sum_{i \in C} w'_i = (N + 1) \sum_{i \in C} w_i - |C|\). Since \(0 \le |C| \le N < N + 1\), maximizing \(\sum_{i \in C} w'_i\) lexicographically maximizes \(\sum_{i \in C} w_i\) first and minimizes \(|C|\) second!

NoteHint 3 — Method B: Why Canonical \(S_{\min}\) Solves It Without Scaling!

Recall Corollary 2.1 (Section 2.3): in the unscaled Picard network \(\mathcal{N}(G_{\text{dep}}, w)\), the residual reachability set \(C_{\min}^* = S_{\min} \setminus \{S\}\) is a subset of every maximum-weight closure \(C^*\) (\(C_{\min}^* \subseteq C^*\)). Therefore, \(|C_{\min}^*|\) is automatically the unique minimum cardinality among all maximum-weight closures, with zero capacity inflation!

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Each employee \(i\) has an arbitrary signed weight \(w_i \in \mathbb{Z}\), and firing \(u\) requires firing \(v\) for \((u \to v) \in E_{\text{dep}}\). This is a general-DAG Maximum-Weight Closure with a minimum-cardinality tie-breaker.

  1. void solve_firing(int N, int M, const vector<long long>& w,
                  const vector<pair<int, int>>& edges, ostream& out) {
    int S = 0, T = N + 1;
    const long long INF = 1e16;
    Dinic<long long> dinic(T + 1, S, T);
    long long W_plus = 0;

    for (int i = 1; i <= N; ++i) {
        if (w[i - 1] > 0) {
            W_plus += w[i - 1];
            dinic.add_edge(S, i, w[i - 1]);
        } else if (w[i - 1] < 0) {
            dinic.add_edge(i, T, -w[i - 1]);
        }
    }
    for (auto [u, v] : edges) {
        dinic.add_edge(u, v, INF);
    }

    long long max_profit = W_plus - dinic.max_flow();
    vector<bool> vis_S = dinic.get_S_min();
    int fired_cnt = 0;
    for (int i = 1; i <= N; ++i) {
        if (vis_S[i]) ++fired_cnt;
    }
    out << fired_cnt << ' ' << max_profit << '\n';
}

Problem #067 — Codeforces 1082 G: Petya and Graph

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 09 — Intermediate 1 (Int-1) | Judge URL: Codeforces 1082 G

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 10^3\) vertices, \(0 \le M \le 10^3\) undirected edges; vertex weights \(a_v \in [1, 10^9]\), edge weights \(w_e \in [1, 10^9]\); Time Limit \(2.0\text{ s}\).

  • Problem Statement: You are given an undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) with positive vertex weights \(a_v\) and positive edge weights \(w_e\). The weight of an induced subgraph on a vertex subset \(C \subseteq V\) is defined as the sum of weights of all edges having both endpoints in \(C\), minus the sum of weights of all vertices in \(C\):

    \[f(C) = \sum_{\substack{e = (u, v) \in E \\ u \in C \land v \in C}} w_e - \sum_{v \in C} a_v.\]

    Find the maximum possible weight \(f(C)\) over all subsets \(C \subseteq V\) (including \(C = \emptyset\), where \(f(\emptyset) = 0\)).

NoteHint 1 — Turning Induced Edges into Project Nodes

An edge \(e = (u, v) \in E\) contributes \(+w_e > 0\) to \(f(C)\) if and only if both \(u \in C\) and \(v \in C\). View each edge \(e \in E\) as a project of profit \(+w_e\) and each vertex \(v \in V\) as a machine of cost \(-a_v\)!

NoteHint 2 — Algebraic Linearization of \(w_e x_u x_v\)

For each edge \(e = (u, v)\), introduce an auxiliary binary indicator \(z_e \in \{0, 1\}\) (“claim edge \(e\)’s reward”) subject to the closure implications \(z_e = 1 \implies x_u = 1\) and \(z_e = 1 \implies x_v = 1\). At the optimum, \(z_e = x_u x_v\) holds automatically because \(w_e > 0\).

NoteHint 3 — Bipartite Closure Topology & 64-Bit Overflow Check

Wire \(S \xrightarrow{w_e} e\) for all \(M\) edges, \(e \xrightarrow{+\infty} u\) and \(e \xrightarrow{+\infty} v\) for each \(e = (u, v)\), and \(v \xrightarrow{a_v} T\) for all \(N\) vertices. Since \(\sum_{e=1}^M w_e \le 10^3 \times 10^9 = 10^{12}\), use long long with INF = 1e16.

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Even though the input is already a graph \(G = (V, E)\), the problem is not asking for a minimum cut of \(G\) itself! Let \(x_v = [v \in C] \in \{0, 1\}\) for \(v \in V\) and \(z_e \in \{0, 1\}\) for \(e = (u, v) \in E\):

\[\begin{aligned} \max_{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^V} \left(\sum_{e=(u,v) \in E} w_e x_u x_v - \sum_{v \in V} a_v x_v\right) &= \max_{\substack{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^V, \, \mathbf{z} \in \{0,1\}^E \\ z_e \le x_u, \, z_e \le x_v}} \left(\sum_{e \in E} w_e z_e - \sum_{v \in V} a_v x_v\right) \\ &= \sum_{e \in E} w_e - \min_{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}} \left(\sum_{e \in E} w_e(1 - z_e) + \sum_{v \in V} a_v x_v + \sum_{e=(u,v)} \infty\big(z_e(1 - x_u) + z_e(1 - x_v)\big)\right). \end{aligned}\]

// Complete Two-Phase Causal Pruning + Closure Solver for #070 (Luogu P2805)
long long solve_pvz(int N, int M, const vector<long long>& score,
                    const vector<vector<int>>& attacks) {
    int V = N * M;
    vector<vector<int>> adj_pre(V);
    vector<int> in_deg(V, 0);

    auto add_pre = [&](int protector, int target) {
        adj_pre[protector].push_back(target);
        in_deg[target]++;
    };

    for (int r = 0; r < N; ++r) {
        for (int c = 0; c < M; ++c) {
            int u = r * M + c;
            if (c + 1 < M) {
                add_pre(r * M + (c + 1), u); // must eat (r, c+1) before (r, c)
            }
            for (int target : attacks[u]) {
                add_pre(u, target); // u protects target -> must eat u before target
            }
        }
    }

    // Phase 1: Kahn's Topological BFS on G_pre to prune cycles and their descendants
    vector<bool> valid(V, false);
    queue<int> q;
    for (int u = 0; u < V; ++u) {
        if (in_deg[u] == 0) q.push(u);
    }
    while (!q.empty()) {
        int u = q.front();
        q.pop();
        valid[u] = true;
        for (int nxt : adj_pre[u]) {
            if (--in_deg[nxt] == 0) q.push(nxt);
        }
    }

    // Phase 2: Build Picard Closure Network ONLY on Kahn-validated vertices
    int S = V, T = V + 1;
    const long long INF = 1e15;
    Dinic<long long> dinic(V + 2, S, T);
    long long W_plus = 0;

    for (int u = 0; u < V; ++u) {
        if (!valid[u]) continue;
        if (score[u] > 0) {
            W_plus += score[u];
            dinic.add_edge(S, u, score[u]);
        } else if (score[u] < 0) {
            dinic.add_edge(u, T, -score[u]);
        }
        for (int nxt : adj_pre[u]) {
            if (valid[nxt]) {
                // Eating nxt requires eating u -> closure edge nxt -> u of cap INF
                dinic.add_edge(nxt, u, INF);
            }
        }
    }
    return W_plus - dinic.max_flow();
}

// Complete Model Compilation for #071 (Luogu P3749 — [SHOI2017] Sushi Restaurant)
long long solve_sushi_restaurant(int N, int m, const vector<int>& a,
                                 const vector<vector<long long>>& d) {
    vector<vector<int>> id(N + 1, vector<int>(N + 1, 0));
    int node_cnt = 0;
    for (int l = 1; l <= N; ++l)
        for (int r = l; r <= N; ++r)
            id[l][r] = ++node_cnt;

    int max_a = *max_element(a.begin(), a.end());
    vector<int> type_id(max_a + 1, 0);
    for (int x : a) {
        if (!type_id[x]) type_id[x] = ++node_cnt;
    }

    int S = 0, T = node_cnt + 1;
    const long long INF = 1e15;
    Dinic<long long> dinic(T + 1, S, T);
    long long W_plus = 0;

    for (int l = 1; l <= N; ++l) {
        for (int r = l; r <= N; ++r) {
            long long w = d[l][r] - (l == r ? a[l - 1] : 0);
            if (w > 0) {
                W_plus += w;
                dinic.add_edge(S, id[l][r], w);
            } else if (w < 0) {
                dinic.add_edge(id[l][r], T, -w);
            }
            if (l < r) {
                dinic.add_edge(id[l][r], id[l + 1][r], INF);
                dinic.add_edge(id[l][r], id[l][r - 1], INF);
            } else {
                dinic.add_edge(id[l][l], type_id[a[l - 1]], INF);
            }
        }
    }
    if (m == 1) {
        for (int x = 1; x <= max_a; ++x) {
            if (type_id[x]) {
                dinic.add_edge(type_id[x], T, 1LL * x * x);
            }
        }
    }
    return W_plus - dinic.max_flow();
}

Key Takeaways

  • Minimum Cut as a Pseudo-Boolean Compiler: Identifying \(x_v = [v \in V_S] \in \{0, 1\}\) (\(x_S = 1, x_T = 0\)) compiles every directed edge \((u \xrightarrow{c} v)\) into the quadratic cut monomial \(c \cdot x_u(1 - x_v)\).

  • Picard’s Maximum-Weight Closure Theorem (1976): Compiling \(x_u = 1 \implies x_v = 1\) as \(u \xrightarrow{+\infty} v\) establishes an exact bijection between closed subsets \(C \subseteq U\) and finite-capacity \((S, T)\)-cuts, with \(S_{\min} \setminus \{S\}\) constructively recovering the unique minimum-cardinality optimal closure.
  • Causal Cycle Pruning (FM-09): When prerequisites represent sequential physical unlocking (Semantics B), Kahn’s topological BFS on \(G_{\text{pre}}\) must precede Dinic to eliminate both deadlocked cycles and their acyclic causal descendants.

Bridge to Chapter 10: From One-Sided Closure to All-\(S\) / All-\(T\) Synergy Hubs and General Submodular Potentials

In this chapter, every project reward \(+w_u\) lived on a single side of the cut (\(V_S\)), and every dependency \(x_u = 1 \implies x_v = 1\) pointed in one direction.

What happens when a problem grants bonuses on both sides of the partition simultaneously—for instance, in [国家集训队] 文理分科 (Luogu P4313) or CF 311 E — Biologist, where a group of students \(A \subseteq U\) earns a synergy bonus \(B_A > 0\) if all of them choose Arts (\(x_i = 1\) for all \(i \in A\), an All-\(S\) clause \(\prod_{i \in A} x_i\)) AND another bonus \(B'_A > 0\) if all of them choose Science (\(x_i = 0\) for all \(i \in A\), an All-\(T\) clause \(\prod_{i \in A} (1 - x_i)\))? And what if two variables \(x_i, x_j \in \{0, 1\}\) interact via a general \(2 \times 2\) payoff matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}\) without creating auxiliary vertices?

In Chapter 10, we generalize Picard’s closure network to High-Arity All-\(S\) / All-\(T\) Synergy Hubs, derive Hu Botao’s Symmetric Vertex-Edge Merging (合并点边法), and lays the foundation for the Kolmogorov–Zabih Submodularity Theorem (\(A + D \le B + C\)) and Bipartite Variable Inversion (二部グラフの片側反転).
