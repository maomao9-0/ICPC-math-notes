Chapter 8 — DAG Path Covers, Dilworth’s Poset Duality, and Hall’s Theorem Flow Elimination

From Split-Graph Chain Decompositions and Transitive Antichain Recovery to \(O(N \log N)\) Segment-Tree and \(O(N 2^N)\) SOS-DP Flow Elimination

Prerequisites & Learning Objectives

NotePrerequisites

  • Chapter 5 & Chapter 6 (Bipartite Matching, König’s Theorem, and Dulmage–Mendelsohn Decomposition): Hopcroft–Karp \(O(E\sqrt{V})\) matching, König’s minimum vertex cover extraction \(C^* = (L \setminus S_{\min}) \cup (R \cap S_{\min})\), and essential/avoidable vertex classification.

1. Motivation: From Chain Decompositions to \(O(N \log N)\) Flow Elimination

Chapters 5 through 7 developed Direction A of bipartite modelling: taking a non-graph problem (grid rooks, L-corner brick tilings, prime-sum conflicts), discovering a hidden bipartition \(V = L \sqcup R\), and running Hopcroft–Karp or Dinic in \(O(E\sqrt{V})\) time.

In competitive programming at the ICPC World Finals and AtCoder Grand/Regular Contest level, two structural barriers break standard bipartite matching:

  1. Directional Sequentiality (Trajectories & Posets): What if an agent does not merely pair one \(u \in L\) with one \(v \in R\), but traverses an arbitrarily long directed chain \(v_1 \to v_2 \to \dots \to v_k\) in a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) or Partially Ordered Set (Poset)? How do we minimize the number of trajectories needed to visit every vertex, or dualize that trajectory cover into the largest subset of mutually incomparable objects (Dilworth’s Theorem)?
  2. Scale Explosion (\(N, M \ge 2 \cdot 10^5\) or \(D \ge 10^6\)): What if the bipartite graph is easy to spot—say, \(N = 2 \cdot 10^5\) people matching to \(M = 2 \cdot 10^5\) chairs along contiguous intervals, or \(N = 18\) workers matching to \(D = 3.6 \cdot 10^6\) workdays—so that even constructing the edges or running \(O(E\sqrt{V})\) Dinic exceeds the time and memory limits by three orders of magnitude?

This chapter resolves both barriers through a unified algebraic lens:

  • In Section 2, we compile sequential chains on a DAG into a bipartite split graph \(G_{\text{split}} = (V_{\text{out}} \sqcup V_{\text{in}}, E_{\text{split}})\) where every matched edge merges two chains and reduces the path count by \(1\). We then prove the Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma, showing how taking the transitive closure turns König’s vertex cover into a constructive Maximum Antichain engine.
  • In Sections 3 and 4, we reverse the direction of reduction (Direction B: Flow Elimination). Instead of turning an algebraic problem into a flow network, we use Hall’s Marriage Theorem and Ore’s Defect Formula to convert a massive bipartite flow network back into a combinatorial optimization problem over subsets \(S \subseteq L\), which we then crush using Segment Trees, Monotone Queues, and Sum-Over-Subsets (SOS) DP.

2. Minimum Disjoint DAG Path Cover vs. General Overlapping Path Cover (Dilworth’s Theorem)

2.1 Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Reach for DAG Path Covers or Poset Duality (Dilworth / Mirsky) whenever a problem exhibits one of the following signatures:

  • Sequential Reuse / Trajectory Stitching (Min Chain / Path Cover): You must cover all vertices (or treasures/tasks) of a directed acyclic process using the minimum number of agents, hands, pegs, or monotone trajectories.
    • Vertex-Disjoint Rule (each vertex may be entered and exited by at most one trajectory) \(\implies\) Minimum Disjoint Path Cover (MDPC) on the raw DAG \(G\).
    • Vertex-Overlapping Rule (trajectories may share intermediate vertices, or the underlying reachability relation is transitive) \(\implies\) Transitive Closure + Dilworth Chain Cover on \(\text{TC}(G)\).
  • Pairwise Incomparable Selection (Max Antichain): Select the largest (or maximum-weight) subset \(A \subseteq V\) such that no two selected elements satisfy \(u \prec v\) (e.g., no chosen string is a substring of another in CF 590 E — Birthday, or no two shrines can reach each other in [CTSC2008] 祭祀).
  • Why Naive Greedy Longest-Chain Peeling Fails: It is tempting to repeatedly extract the longest directed path in a DAG and delete it. This fails catastrophically! Consider the 6-vertex DAG with spine \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 4\) and two side branches \(2 \to 5\) and \(6 \to 3\):
    • Greedy Longest Path: Picks the 4-vertex spine \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 4\) first, leaving vertices \(\{5\}\) and \(\{6\}\) completely isolated. Greedy uses \(3\) paths: \((1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 4)\), \((5)\), and \((6)\).
    • True Optimum: Reroutes at the middle edge \(2 \to 3\) to form \(2\) paths of length \(3\): \((1 \to 2 \to 5)\) and \((6 \to 3 \to 4)\), covering all \(6\) vertices! Bipartite matching augmenting paths exist precisely to undo greedy edge commitments like \(2 \to 3\).

1. The Dual Quartet on a Finite Poset \(\mathcal{P} = (V, \prec)\)

Before writing down indicator variables, always verify which of the four poset quantities the problem asks for—two of them require zero flow (\(O(V + E)\) topological DP), while the other two require bipartite matching:

2. Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation of Disjoint Path Cover

Let \(G = (V, E)\) be a DAG with \(|V| = N\). A vertex-disjoint path cover \(\mathcal{P} = \{P_1, \dots, P_k\}\) partitions \(V\) into \(k\) directed paths. Let \(x_{uv} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether directed edge \((u \to v) \in E\) is traversed by one of the paths in \(\mathcal{P}\).

What algebraic constraints on \(x_{uv}\) characterize a valid vertex-disjoint path cover?

  1. Out-degree at most \(1\): Each vertex \(u \in V\) has at most one successor in its path:

    \[\sum_{v : (u, v) \in E} x_{uv} \le 1 \qquad \forall u \in V.\]

  2. In-degree at most \(1\): Each vertex \(v \in V\) has at most one predecessor in its path:

    \[\sum_{u : (u, v) \in E} x_{uv} \le 1 \qquad \forall v \in V.\]

  3. Cycle-Freeness via Acyclicity: In a general directed graph, degree-\(\le 1\) subgraphs can contain directed cycles. Because \(G\) is a DAG, no directed cycles exist in \(E\), so every edge subset \(M = \{(u, v) \in E : x_{uv} = 1\}\) satisfying (1) and (2) is a disjoint union of directed paths!

  4. Path Counting Identity: Initially, when \(M = \emptyset\) (\(x_{uv} = 0\)), every vertex \(v \in V\) is a trivial path of length \(0\), giving \(N\) paths. Each selected edge \((u, v) \in M\) concatenates the path ending at \(u\) with the path starting at \(v\), reducing the total number of paths by exactly \(1\):

    \[\text{Number of Paths}(M) = |V| - \sum_{(u, v) \in E} x_{uv} = |V| - |M|.\]

3. Step 3: Mechanical Graph Compilation (\(G_{\text{split}}\))

Look at constraints (1) and (2): each edge variable \(x_{uv} \in \{0, 1\}\) consumes at most \(1\) unit of “exit capacity” at \(u\) and at most \(1\) unit of “entry capacity” at \(v\). Since \(u\) can simultaneously have an incoming path edge \((w \to u)\) and an outgoing path edge \((u \to v)\), we must decouple \(u\)’s role as a path tail from its role as a path head:

  • Split each vertex \(v \in V\) into two bipartite nodes: an outgoing port \(v_{\text{out}} \in V_{\text{out}}\) (left side \(L\)) and an incoming port \(v_{\text{in}} \in V_{\text{in}}\) (right side \(R\)).
  • For every directed edge \((u \to v) \in E\), add a bipartite edge \((u_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}}) \in E_{\text{split}}\).
  • Constraints (1) and (2) state that \(M \subseteq E_{\text{split}}\) is a bipartite matching in \(G_{\text{split}}(G) = (V_{\text{out}} \sqcup V_{\text{in}}, E_{\text{split}})\)! Minimizing \(|V| - |M|\) maximizes \(|M|\), yielding the fundamental formula:

\[\boxed{\text{MDPC}(G) = |V| - \nu\big(G_{\text{split}}(G)\big).}\]

  • Explicit Path Reconstruction: After finding a maximum matching \(M^*\) in \(G_{\text{split}}(G)\), a vertex \(v \in V\) is the start of a path iff its incoming port \(v_{\text{in}} \in V_{\text{in}}\) is unmatched. From each unmatched \(v_{\text{in}}\), repeatedly follow the unique matched partner \(v_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{M^*} w_{\text{in}}\) until reaching an unmatched outgoing port \(z_{\text{out}}\).

  • Weighted / Cost Extension: Suppose starting a new path at \(v\) costs \(s_v\) and traversing \((u \to v)\) costs \(c_{uv}\). Then choosing edge \((u \to v) \in M\) replaces the start cost \(s_v\) at \(v\) with the transition cost \(c_{uv}\), changing the total cost by \(c_{uv} - s_v\):

    \[\text{Total Cost}(M) = \sum_{v \in V} s_v + \sum_{(u, v) \in M} (c_{uv} - s_v),\]

    which compiles immediately to Minimum-Weight Bipartite Matching (MCMF) on \(G_{\text{split}}(G)\)!

2.3 Dilworth’s Theorem, Overlapping Path Covers & The Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma

What if trajectories are allowed to share vertices (Minimum Overlapping Path Cover), or we want to find the Maximum-Weight Antichain of a poset \(\mathcal{P} = (V, \prec)\) where each element \(v \in V\) has positive weight \(w_v > 0\)?

1. Why Transitive Closure Eliminates Vertex Sharing (Path Jumping)

Suppose two trajectories \(P_1 = (a \to v \to b)\) and \(P_2 = (c \to v \to d)\) share an intermediate vertex \(v\). Because \(P_1\) already covers \(v\), \(P_2\) does not need to physically step on \(v\)—it only uses \(v\) as a stepping stone to get from \(c\) to \(d\). If we replace the raw DAG \(G\) with its transitive closure \(G^{\text{tc}} = \text{TC}(G)\) (adding a direct shortcut edge \(u \to w\) whenever \(u \leadsto_G w\) via a path of length \(\ge 1\)), then \(P_2\) can jump directly across \(v\) via the shortcut edge \(c \to d \in E(G^{\text{tc}})\)!

By uncrossing shared vertices one by one, any collection of \(k\) potentially overlapping paths covering \(V\) in \(G\) converts into \(k\) strictly vertex-disjoint chains covering \(V\) in \(G^{\text{tc}}\), and vice versa (replacing each transitive jump \(c \to d\) by its underlying path \(c \leadsto d\) in \(G\)). Hence:

\[\boxed{\text{MinOverlappingPathCover}(G) = \text{MDPC}\big(\text{TC}(G)\big) = |V| - \nu\Big(G_{\text{split}}\big(\text{TC}(G)\big)\Big).}\]

2. Resolving the Double-Cover Paradox: The Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma

Now let us connect \(\text{MDPC}(\text{TC}(G))\) to Dilworth’s Theorem and Constructive Antichain Recovery. In \(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P}) = (V_{\text{out}} \sqcup V_{\text{in}}, E_{\text{split}})\) where \((u_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}}) \in E_{\text{split}} \iff u \prec v\), assign weight \(w_v > 0\) to both \(v_{\text{out}} \in V_{\text{out}}\) and \(v_{\text{in}} \in V_{\text{in}}\).

By König’s Theorem (Chapter 6), the maximum matching size (or minimum \(s\)-\(t\) cut in the weighted split network) equals the minimum vertex cover \(\tau_w\big(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\big)\). Suppose \(C^* \subseteq V_{\text{out}} \sqcup V_{\text{in}}\) is a minimum vertex cover of \(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\).

Wait: each poset element \(v \in V\) was split into two vertices \(v_{\text{out}}\) and \(v_{\text{in}}\)! For \(A^* \subseteq V\) to be a valid antichain in \(\mathcal{P}\), we can only pick \(v \in A^*\) if neither \(v_{\text{out}}\) nor \(v_{\text{in}}\) is needed to cover any comparability edge—that is, we must define:

\[A^* := \big\{\, v \in V \;\big|\; v_{\text{out}} \notin C^* \;\land\; v_{\text{in}} \notin C^* \,\big\}.\]

Why does \(A^*\) have size \(|V| - |C^*|\) (or weight \(\sum_{v \in V} w_v - \tau_w(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P}))\))? What if \(C^*\) selects both \(v_{\text{out}} \in C^*\) and \(v_{\text{in}} \in C^*\) for some element \(v \in V\), paying twice for \(v\) while deleting only one element from \(V\)? The following fundamental lemma proves that transitivity of \(\prec\) makes double-covering impossible!

ImportantLemma 2.1 (The Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma)

Let \(\mathcal{P} = (V, \prec)\) be a strict poset (irreflexive and transitive), and let \(C^* \subseteq V_{\text{out}} \sqcup V_{\text{in}}\) be any minimal (or canonical minimum-cut) vertex cover of \(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\). Then no element \(v \in V\) has both \(v_{\text{out}} \in C^*\) and \(v_{\text{in}} \in C^*\):

\[v_{\text{out}} \in C^* \implies v_{\text{in}} \notin C^* \qquad \forall v \in V.\]

Consequently, the sets \(C^*_{\text{out}} = \{v \in V : v_{\text{out}} \in C^*\}\) and \(C^*_{\text{in}} = \{v \in V : v_{\text{in}} \in C^*\}\) are disjoint subsets of \(V\), and the complement

\[\boxed{A^* = \big\{\, v \in V \;\big|\; v_{\text{out}} \notin C^* \;\land\; v_{\text{in}} \notin C^* \,\big\} = \big\{\, v \in V \;\big|\; v_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min} \;\land\; v_{\text{in}} \notin S_{\min} \,\big\}}\]

is a Maximum-Weight Antichain of weight \(\sum_{v \in A^*} w_v = \sum_{v \in V} w_v - \tau_w\big(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\big)\) (and cardinality \(|A^*| = |V| - \nu(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P}))\) when \(w_v = 1\)).

Proof: Suppose for contradiction that for some \(v \in V\), both \(v_{\text{out}} \in C^*\) and \(v_{\text{in}} \in C^*\) (and in the canonical min-cut \(C^* = (V_{\text{out}} \setminus S_{\min}) \cup (V_{\text{in}} \cap S_{\min})\) or any minimal cover, neither can be dropped without uncovering an edge):

  1. Because \(v_{\text{out}} \in C^*\) is essential to the cover (or in the canonical min-cut \(v_{\text{out}} \notin S_{\min}\)), there exists at least one outgoing edge \((v_{\text{out}}, y_{\text{in}}) \in E_{\text{split}}\) (\(v \prec y\)) whose right endpoint is not in \(C^*\): \(y_{\text{in}} \notin C^*\) (in the canonical min-cut, \(v_{\text{in}} \in S_{\min}\) means some \(x_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min}\) has an \(\infty\)-edge \((x_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}})\) to \(v_{\text{in}}\), so \(x_{\text{out}} \notin C^*\)).
  2. Symmetrically, because \(v_{\text{in}} \in C^*\), there exists at least one incoming edge \((x_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}}) \in E_{\text{split}}\) (\(x \prec v\)) whose left endpoint is not in \(C^*\): \(x_{\text{out}} \notin C^*\).
  3. By transitivity of the poset relation \(\prec\), \(x \prec v\) and \(v \prec y\) force \(x \prec y\), which means the shortcut edge \((x_{\text{out}}, y_{\text{in}})\) must exist in \(E_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\)!
  4. Yet \(x_{\text{out}} \notin C^*\) and \(y_{\text{in}} \notin C^*\), meaning the edge \((x_{\text{out}}, y_{\text{in}})\) has neither endpoint in \(C^*\)—contradicting that \(C^*\) is a valid vertex cover! (In the canonical min-cut, \(x_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min}\) and \((x_{\text{out}}, y_{\text{in}}) \in E_{\text{split}}\) with capacity \(\infty\) forces \(y_{\text{in}} \in S_{\min}\), so \(y_{\text{in}} \in C^*\) for every \(y \succ v\), meaning removing \(v_{\text{out}}\) from \(C^*\) leaves zero uncovered edges and strictly decreases the cut cost by \(w_v > 0\)!)

Finally, for any two distinct elements \(u, v \in A^*\), neither \(u_{\text{out}}\) nor \(v_{\text{in}}\) belongs to \(C^*\). Since \(C^*\) covers all edges of \(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\), \((u_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}}) \notin E_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\), so \(u \not\prec v\) and \(v \not\prec u\). Thus \(A^*\) is a valid antichain of weight \(\sum_{v \in V} w_v - \sum_{v \in C^*_{\text{out}} \sqcup C^*_{\text{in}}} w_v = \sum_{v \in V} w_v - \tau_w\big(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\big)\). Since every chain can contain at most one element of any antichain, no antichain can exceed the minimum chain cover weight, proving optimality. \(\blacksquare\)

2.4 Step 4: Broken-Gadget Counterexample FM-08 (Missing Transitive Closure & OR vs. AND Bug)

WarningFailure Mode FM-08: Omitting Transitive Closure & Using OR Instead of AND in Antichain Extraction

Two fatal bugs account for over 90% of Wrong Answers on Dilworth and DAG path cover problems:

  1. Bug 1 — Omitting Transitive Closure Before Matching (X-Bottleneck Counterexample): Consider the 5-vertex X-shaped DAG \(G\) with edges \(1 \to 2\), \(4 \to 2\), \(2 \to 3\), and \(2 \to 5\) (vertex \(2\) is a central hub; there are no direct edges from \(\{1, 4\}\) to \(\{3, 5\}\)).
    • True Overlapping Path Cover / Max Antichain: \(2\) chains (\(1 \to 2 \to 3\) and \(4 \to 2 \to 5\), sharing hub \(2\)), and the maximum antichain has size \(2\) (e.g., \(\{1, 4\}\) or \(\{3, 5\}\)).
    • What Happens If You Forget \(\text{TC}(G)\): In the raw split graph \(G_{\text{split}}(G)\), \(E_{\text{split}} = \{(1_{\text{out}}, 2_{\text{in}}), (4_{\text{out}}, 2_{\text{in}}), (2_{\text{out}}, 3_{\text{in}}), (2_{\text{out}}, 5_{\text{in}})\}\). Because \(2_{\text{in}}\) can match at most one of \(\{1_{\text{out}}, 4_{\text{out}}\}\) and \(2_{\text{out}}\) can match at most one of \(\{3_{\text{in}}, 5_{\text{in}}\}\), the maximum matching size is \(\nu = 2\), falsely reporting \(5 - 2 = \mathbf{3}\) chains! Worse still, the minimum vertex cover of \(G_{\text{split}}(G)\) is \(C^* = \{2_{\text{out}}, 2_{\text{in}}\}\) (size \(2\)), which violates Lemma 2.1 by picking both halves of vertex \(2\), and outputs \(A^* = V \setminus \{2\} = \{1, 3, 4, 5\}\) of size \(4\) (not even an antichain, since \(1 \leadsto 3\))!
    • How \(\text{TC}(G)\) Fixes It: Adding transitive shortcut edges \(1 \to 3, 1 \to 5, 4 \to 3, 4 \to 5\) allows matching \((1_{\text{out}}, 2_{\text{in}})\), \((2_{\text{out}}, 3_{\text{in}})\), and the shortcut \((4_{\text{out}}, 5_{\text{in}})\), raising \(\nu\) to \(3\) (\(5 - 3 = 2\) chains) and forcing \(C^* = \{2_{\text{in}}, 3_{\text{in}}, 5_{\text{in}}\}\) so \(A^* = \{1, 4\}\) has size \(2\).
  2. Bug 2 — Extracting \(A^*\) with OR (\(\notin C^*_{\text{out}} \lor \notin C^*_{\text{in}}\)) Instead of AND (H-06): On a simple 2-vertex chain \(1 \to 2\), \(M^* = \{(1_{\text{out}}, 2_{\text{in}})\}\) and \(C^* = \{2_{\text{in}}\}\). Here \(1_{\text{out}} \notin C^*, 1_{\text{in}} \notin C^*\) (both out), while for vertex \(2\), \(2_{\text{out}} \notin C^*\) is true even though \(2_{\text{in}} \in C^*\)! Using OR (\(\neg C^*_{\text{out}} \lor \neg C^*_{\text{in}}\)) selects \(\{1, 2\}\) (comparable!), whereas AND (\(\neg C^*_{\text{out}} \land \neg C^*_{\text{in}}\)) selects the true antichain \(\{1\}\).

Reference Implementation Pattern: Constructive Dilworth Maximum Antichain (CF 590 E, [CTSC2008] 祭祀)

// Given a transitively closed, irreflexive poset P on 0..N-1 (reach[u][v] == true iff u < v)
// and residual reachability vis_S[] from Source S after running Dinic on S -> u_out -> v_in -> T:
//   u_out = u (0 .. N-1), u_in = u + N (N .. 2N-1)
vector<int> extract_max_antichain(int N, const vector<bool>& vis_S) {
    vector<int> antichain;
    for (int v = 0; v < N; ++v) {
        // v_out not in C*  <==>  vis_S[v] == true
        // v_in  not in C*  <==>  vis_S[v + N] == false
        if (vis_S[v] && !vis_S[v + N]) {
            antichain.push_back(v);
        }
    }
    return antichain; // Guaranteed size == N - MaxFlow (or weight == Sum(w) - MinCut)
}

2.5 Extension: Partial \(k\)-Chain Covers, Greene–Kleitman Duality (1976) & MCMF Compilation

What if a problem gives us a budget of at most \(k\) chains (or \(k\) paths on a DAG) where \(k < \text{width}(\mathcal{P})\), and asks for the maximum number (or maximum total weight) of vertices we can cover using at most \(k\) chains?

NoteTheorem 2.2 (Greene–Kleitman Theorem, 1976 — \(k\)-Dilworth Duality)

In any finite poset \(\mathcal{P} = (V, \prec)\) and for any integer \(k \ge 1\), the maximum cardinality of a \(k\)-chain family (a subset \(U \subseteq V\) that can be partitioned into at most \(k\) chains) equals:

\[\max_{\substack{C_1, \dots, C_k \text{ chains}}} \left|\bigcup_{i=1}^k C_i\right| = \min_{\substack{A_1 \sqcup \dots \sqcup A_q = V \\ \text{antichain partition}}} \sum_{j=1}^q \min\big(|A_j|, \; k\big) = \min_{U \subseteq V} \Big(|V \setminus U| + k \cdot \text{width}(\mathcal{P}[U])\Big).\]

When each vertex \(v \in V\) of a DAG \(G = (V, E)\) offers a reward \(w_v \ge 0\) upon its first visit by one of at most \(k\) paths, we compile this directly to Min-Cost Max-Flow (MCMF) of flow value \(k\):

  • Split each vertex \(v \in V\) into \(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}\) with:
    • A reward edge \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{\text{cap } 1, \text{ cost } -w_v} v_{\text{out}}\) (for vertex-disjoint paths).
    • If paths are allowed to overlap (share vertices without collecting \(w_v\) twice), add a parallel bypass edge \(v_{\text{in}} \xrightarrow{\text{cap } \infty, \text{ cost } 0} v_{\text{out}}\).
  • Wire \(S_{\text{sub}} \xrightarrow{\text{cap } \infty, \text{ cost } 0} v_{\text{in}}\), \(v_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\text{cap } \infty, \text{ cost } 0} T\), and for each \((u \to v) \in E\), wire \(u_{\text{out}} \xrightarrow{\text{cap } \infty, \text{ cost } 0} v_{\text{in}}\).
  • Throttle total trajectories to \(\le k\) via \(S \xrightarrow{\text{cap } k, \text{ cost } 0} S_{\text{sub}}\) and a slack bypass \(S_{\text{sub}} \xrightarrow{\text{cap } \infty, \text{ cost } 0} T\). The negated min-cost of pushing \(k\) units from \(S\) to \(T\) is the exact Greene–Kleitman maximum \(k\)-chain weight!

3. Hall’s Marriage Theorem, Ore’s Defect Formula, and Supply–Demand Hall

3.1 Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (When to Eliminate Flow via Hall’s Theorem)

When a bipartite matching or capacitated supply–demand problem has \(N, M \ge 2 \cdot 10^5\) (or \(D \sim 3.6 \cdot 10^6\) days in ARC 106 E), explicit Dinic or Hopcroft–Karp requires \(O(E\sqrt{V})\) time and TLEs! Reach for Hall’s Marriage Theorem or Ore’s Defect Formula (Direction B: Flow Elimination) whenever:

  1. Geometric Neighbourhood Convexity: Each \(u \in L\) connects to a contiguous interval \([l_u, r_u] \subseteq R\) (POI 2009 LYZ-Ice Skates), a prefix–suffix union \([1, l_u] \cup [r_u, M]\) (ARC 076 F — Exhausted?), or a circular arc of radius \(d\) (CF 981 F — Round Marriage).
  2. Extreme Cardinality Asymmetry (\(|L| \le 18, |R| \ge 10^5\), or vice versa): One side of the bipartite graph has \(\le 20\) vertices (CF 1009 G with \(|\Sigma| = 6\), ARC 106 E with \(N \le 18\)), allowing \(O(N 2^N)\) Sum-Over-Subsets (SOS) DP over all \(2^{|L|}\) subsets \(S \subseteq L\).
  3. Hyperedge-to-Vertex Tree Realization (AGC 029 F): Given \(N - 1\) subsets \(E_1, \dots, E_{N-1} \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\), choosing one representative endpoint \(u_i \in E_i\) per hyperedge to form a spanning tree is equivalent to Hall’s condition \(\left|\bigcup_{i \in S} E_i\right| \ge |S| + 1\) for all \(\emptyset \neq S \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}\).

3.2 Step 2: Deriving Ore’s Defect Formula & Supply–Demand Hall from König / Min-Cut

Let \(G = (L \sqcup R, E)\) be a bipartite graph where each left vertex \(u \in L\) has supply \(a_u \ge 0\) and each right vertex \(v \in R\) has demand capacity \(b_v \ge 0\) (in unweighted matching, \(a_u = b_v = 1\)). Consider the standard \(S\)-\(T\) flow network \(\mathcal{N}\) with capacities:

\[c(S, u) = a_u \quad (\forall u \in L), \qquad c(u, v) = \infty \quad (\forall (u, v) \in E), \qquad c(v, T) = b_v \quad (\forall v \in R).\]

Let \((V_S, V_T)\) be any finite-capacity \(S\)-\(T\) cut, with \(S_L := L \cap V_S\) and \(S_R := R \cap V_S\).

  • Infinite-Edge Constraint: For the cut capacity \(\text{cap}(V_S, V_T)\) to be finite, no edge \((u, v) \in E\) with \(u \in S_L\) and \(v \in R \setminus S_R\) may be severed. Thus every neighbor of \(S_L\) must also lie in \(S_R\):

    \[N(S_L) \subseteq S_R, \qquad \text{where } N(S_L) := \bigcup_{u \in S_L} N(u).\]

  • Optimal Right-Side Choice: For any fixed subset \(S_L \subseteq L\), the cut severs \(S \to u\) for \(u \in L \setminus S_L\) and \(v \to T\) for \(v \in S_R\). Since \(b_v \ge 0\), minimizing \(\sum_{v \in S_R} b_v\) over all \(S_R \supseteq N(S_L)\) sets \(S_R = N(S_L)\) exactly!

  • Cut Capacity as Supply Minus Defect: Substituting \(S_R = N(S_L)\) into the cut capacity gives:

    \[\text{Cut}(S_L) = \sum_{u \in L \setminus S_L} a_u + \sum_{v \in N(S_L)} b_v = \sum_{u \in L} a_u - \underbrace{\left(\sum_{u \in S_L} a_u - \sum_{v \in N(S_L)} b_v\right)}_{\text{Defect } \text{def}(S_L)}.\]

Minimizing \(\text{Cut}(S_L)\) over all \(S_L \subseteq L\) maximizes the defect \(\text{def}(S_L)\). By the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem (and König’s Theorem when \(a_u = b_v = 1\)), we immediately obtain the three fundamental pillars of Hall–Ore Duality:

ImportantTheorem 3.1 (Ore’s Defect Formula, Hall’s Marriage Theorem & Complement Zeta Identity)

For any bipartite graph \(G = (L \sqcup R, E)\) with supplies \(a_u \ge 0\) (\(u \in L\)) and capacities \(b_v \ge 0\) (\(v \in R\)):

  1. Ore’s Defect Formula (1955): The maximum \(a\)-\(b\) flow (or maximum matching \(\nu(G)\) when \(a_u = b_v = 1\)) equals total supply minus the maximum subset defect:

    \[\boxed{\nu_{a, b}(G) = \sum_{u \in L} a_u - \max_{S \subseteq L} \left(\sum_{u \in S} a_u - \sum_{v \in N(S)} b_v\right), \qquad \nu(G) = |L| - \max_{S \subseteq L} \big(|S| - |N(S)|\big).}\]

    (Note: Choosing \(S = \emptyset\) always yields \(\text{def}(\emptyset) = 0\), so the maximum defect is always \(\ge 0\).)

  2. Hall’s Marriage Theorem (1935) / Supply–Demand Feasibility: A matching saturating all left supplies \(\sum_{u \in L} a_u\) exists if and only if \(\max_{S \subseteq L} \text{def}(S) \le 0\), i.e.:

    \[\boxed{\forall S \subseteq L, \qquad \sum_{v \in N(S)} b_v \ge \sum_{u \in S} a_u.}\]

  3. Set-Complement Rewrite for Fast Zeta Transform (ARC 106 E, CF 1009 G): A right vertex \(v \in R\) does not belong to \(N(S)\) if and only if its entire neighborhood \(N(v)\) lies inside the complement \(L \setminus S\). Defining the upward subset-sum \(g[T] := \sum_{v \in R : N(v) \subseteq T} b_v\) for each mask \(T \subseteq L\), we have:

    \[\boxed{\sum_{v \in N(S)} b_v = \sum_{v \in R} b_v - g[L \setminus S], \qquad \text{where } g[T] = \sum_{M \subseteq T} \left(\sum_{v \in R : N(v) = M} b_v\right).}\]

4. Direction B: Eliminating Flow via Hall’s Theorem + Segment Trees / Bitmasks (ARC 076 F, CF 981 F, ARC 106 E, AGC 029 F)

Theorem 3.1 replaces a graph flow algorithm with a maximization over \(2^{|L|}\) subsets \(S \subseteq L\). When \(|L| \le 20\), we evaluate all \(2^{|L|}\) subsets directly via SOS DP (Section 4.3). But what when \(|L|, |R| = 2 \cdot 10^5\)? How can we maximize \(\text{def}(S)\) over \(2^{200,000}\) subsets in \(O(N \log N)\) time?

4.1 Step 3 & Step 4: The Connected-Component Additivity Lemma (Feasibility vs. Exact Defect Trap!)

Suppose every left item \(u \in L\) connects to a contiguous interval \(N(u) = [l_u, r_u] \subseteq R\) (where \(R = \{1, 2, \dots, M\}\)). Instead of iterating over subsets \(S \subseteq L\), reverse the viewpoint and iterate over candidate right-side footprints \(I \subseteq R\):

  • For any fixed target footprint \(I \subseteq R\), which subset \(S \subseteq L\) satisfies \(N(S) \subseteq I\) and maximizes \(\sum_{u \in S} a_u\)? Since \(a_u \ge 0\), we should greedily include every \(u \in L\) whose interval \([l_u, r_u]\) falls completely inside \(I\)! This is the maximal saturation set:

    \[S^*(I) := \big\{\, u \in L \;\big|\; [l_u, r_u] \subseteq I \,\big\}.\]

  • Defining the footprint defect \(\text{def}(I) := \sum_{u \in S^*(I)} a_u - \sum_{v \in I} b_v\), Ore’s Defect Formula becomes \(\max_{S \subseteq L} \text{def}(S) = \max_{I \subseteq R} \text{def}(I)\).

Now decompose an arbitrary subset \(I \subseteq R\) into its maximal contiguous interval components \(I = I_1 \sqcup I_2 \sqcup \dots \sqcup I_k\), separated by at least one gap element \(g \notin I\) between \(I_j\) and \(I_{j+1}\).

ImportantLemma 4.1 (Connected-Component Additivity of Interval Defect)

If every \(u \in L\) has a contiguous interval neighborhood \([l_u, r_u] \subseteq R\), then for any subset \(I = I_1 \sqcup I_2 \sqcup \dots \sqcup I_k\) decomposed into maximal contiguous intervals separated by gaps:

  1. No interval \([l_u, r_u]\) can bridge across a gap \(g \notin I\), so \([l_u, r_u] \subseteq I \iff \exists! j \in \{1, \dots, k\}\) such that \([l_u, r_u] \subseteq I_j\).

  2. Therefore, \(S^*(I) = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^k S^*(I_j)\), and the footprint defect is strictly additive across connected components:

    \[\boxed{\text{def}(I_1 \sqcup I_2 \sqcup \dots \sqcup I_k) = \sum_{j=1}^k \text{def}(I_j), \qquad \text{where } \text{def}([l, r]) = \sum_{u : l \le l_u \le r_u \le r} a_u - \sum_{v=l}^r b_v.}\]

CautionCritical Distinction: Feasibility Testing (<= 0) vs. Exact Maximum Defect (> 0)

From the additivity identity \(\text{def}(I_1 \sqcup \dots \sqcup I_k) = \sum_{j=1}^k \text{def}(I_j)\), two sharply different algorithmic rules follow depending on whether you are testing feasibility or computing the exact maximum defect:

  1. Rule A — Feasibility Testing (\(\max_I \text{def}(I) \le 0\), e.g., POI 2009 LYZ, CF 981 F Binary Search): A sum of numbers is \(\le 0\) for all combinations if and only if every individual summand is \(\le 0\)! Therefore:

    \[\forall I \subseteq R, \; \text{def}(I) \le 0 \quad \iff \quad \forall \text{ single contiguous interval } [l, r] \subseteq R, \; \text{def}([l, r]) \le 0.\]

    Checking the \(O(M^2)\) single intervals \([l, r]\) with a Segment Tree or Monotone Queue is 100% necessary and sufficient for Hall feasibility!

  2. Rule B — Exact Maximum Defect (\(\max_I \text{def}(I) > 0\)) on Interior Intervals: When the graph is not fully matchable (\(\max_{[l,r]} \text{def}([l, r]) > 0\)) and you need the exact maximum matching size \(\nu(G) = \sum a_u - \max_I \text{def}(I)\), maximizing over single intervals \([l, r]\) is FALSE if two disjoint intervals \(I_1 = [l_1, r_1]\) and \(I_2 = [l_2, r_2]\) (\(l_2 \ge r_1 + 2\)) both have positive defect!

    • Broken Toy Counterexample: Let \(R = \{1, 2, 3\}\) with \(b_1 = b_2 = b_3 = 1\). Let \(L\) have \(2\) items with interval \([1, 1]\) and \(2\) items with interval \([3, 3]\) (\(\sum a_u = 4\)).

      • Single intervals give: \(\text{def}([1, 1]) = 2 - 1 = 1\), \(\text{def}([3, 3]) = 2 - 1 = 1\), \(\text{def}([1, 3]) = 4 - 3 = 1\), and all other single intervals have defect \(\le 0\). Thus \(\max_{[l, r]} \text{def}([l, r]) = 1\), which falsely predicts \(\nu(G) = 4 - 1 = \mathbf{3}\)!
      • In reality, the disconnected footprint \(I = [1, 1] \sqcup [3, 3] = \{1, 3\}\) has \(\text{def}(\{1, 3\}) = \text{def}([1, 1]) + \text{def}([3, 3]) = 1 + 1 = \mathbf{2}\), giving the true matching size \(\nu(G) = 4 - 2 = \mathbf{2}\).

    • Fix for Interior Intervals: To compute \(\max_I \text{def}(I)\) when \(N(u) = [l_u, r_u]\), accumulate positive-defect disjoint intervals separated by \(\ge 1\) gap via DP:

      \[dp[r] = \max\Big(dp[r - 1], \;\max_{1 \le l \le r} \big(dp[l - 2] + \text{def}([l, r])\big)\Big).\]

  3. Rule C — Why Prefix–Suffix Unions (ARC 076 F, \(N(u) = [1, L_u] \cup [R_u, M]\)) Need Only ONE Pair \((L, R)\): In ARC 076 F — Exhausted?, each person \(u\) connects to a prefix union suffix \(N(u) = [1, L_u] \cup [R_u, M]\), which means its complement \(\overline{N(u)} = (L_u, R_u)\) is a single open interval! For any \(I \subseteq R\), \(N(u) \subseteq I \iff I^c \subseteq (L_u, R_u)\). Replacing \(I^c\) by its contiguous bounding hull \([\min I^c, \max I^c] = (L, R)\) only enlarges \(I^c\) (shrinking \(|I|\) and increasing the defect!) while preserving \(I^c \subseteq (L_u, R_u)\) for every saturated \(u\). Thus the worst-case footprint \(I\) is always a single prefix–suffix union \([1, L] \cup [R, M]\) (\(0 \le L < R \le M + 1\)), so a single Lazy Segment Tree sweep over \((L, R)\) computes the exact positive defect!

4.2 Five Canonical Hall Flow-Elimination Engines

Engine 1: Dynamic Point-Shift Interval Hall \(\leftrightarrow\) Maximum Subarray Sum (POI 2009 LYZ-Ice Skates, #058 / S2)

Suppose skaters of foot size \(i \in \{1, \dots, N - d\}\) have demand \(a_i\) and can wear any skate size in \([i, i + d]\), where each skate size \(j \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) has supply \(k\). For any contiguous interval of foot sizes \([l, r]\), their union of allowed skate sizes is \([l, r + d]\), which has total capacity \((r - l + 1 + d) \cdot k\). By Lemma 4.1 (Rule A), Hall feasibility holds iff for all \(1 \le l \le r \le N - d\):

\[\sum_{i=l}^r a_i \le (r - l + 1 + d) \cdot k \quad \iff \quad \sum_{i=l}^r (a_i - k) \le d \cdot k.\]

Maintain the array \(c_i := a_i - k\) in a Segment Tree supporting point updates and Maximum Contiguous Subarray Sum (max_sub). All skaters can be matched iff tree[1].max_sub <= d * k in \(O(\log N)\) time per query!

Engine 2: Prefix–Suffix Hall Defect via 2D Dominance Sweep + Lazy Segment Tree (ARC 076 F — Exhausted?)

Given \(N\) people with allowed chairs \(N(i) = [1, L_i] \cup [R_i, M]\) (\(N, M \le 2 \cdot 10^5\)), by Rule C above, every non-trivial candidate footprint has the form \(I = [1, L] \cup [R, M]\) with \(0 \le L < R \le M + 1\) and size \(|I| = L + (M - R + 1)\). Person \(i\) is forced into \(S^*(I)\) iff \(L_i \le L\) and \(R_i \ge R\). Thus Ore’s Defect Formula gives:

\[\text{Min Unseated People} = \max\Big(0, \; N - M, \; \max_{0 \le L < R \le M + 1} \big(\#\big\{i : L_i \le L \;\land\; R_i \ge R\big\} - L - (M - R + 1)\big)\Big).\]

Sort people by \(L_i\) ascending and sweep \(L\) from \(0\) to \(M\). Maintain a Lazy Segment Tree over \(R \in [1, M + 1]\) initialized with \(T[R] = R - M - 1\). Whenever a person with \((L_i, R_i)\) is activated at \(L = L_i\), add \(+1\) to the range \(R \in [1, R_i]\); then query \(\max_{R \in [L + 1, M + 1]} T[R] - L\) in \(O((N + M) \log M)\) total time!

Engine 3: Circular Hall Unrolling (CF 981 F — Round Marriage)

Given \(N \le 2 \cdot 10^5\) brides at sorted positions \(a_1 \le \dots \le a_N\) and \(N\) grooms at sorted positions \(b_1 \le \dots \le b_N\) on a circle of circumference \(L\), binary search the maximum matching distance \(D \in [0, L / 2]\).

To eliminate wrap-around on the circle, unroll the arrays: copy \(a\) twice to length \(2N\) (\(a_{i+N} = a_i + L\), shifted by \(+L\)) and copy \(b\) four times to length \(4N\) (\(b_{j+kN} = b_j + kL\)). Each bride \(i \in [1, 2N]\) then connects to a contiguous interval of grooms \([l_i, r_i] \subseteq [1, 4N]\) computable by two pointers. By Lemma 4.1 (Rule A), distance \(D\) is feasible iff every contiguous bride interval \([i, j]\) (\(1 \le i \le j < i + N\)) satisfies:

\[r_j - l_i + 1 \ge j - i + 1 \quad \iff \quad (r_j - j) \ge (l_i - i) \qquad \forall 1 \le i \le j < i + N,\]

which is verified in \(O(N)\) linear time per binary search step by maintaining the running prefix maximum of \((l_i - i)\)!

Engine 4: Capacitated Small-Side SOS DP (ARC 106 E) vs. Primal Flow-State DP (CF 1519 F)

  • When \(|L| \le 18\) and \(|R| \ge 10^6\) (ARC 106 E — Medals, #062): Apply Theorem 3.1(3). Group the millions of right vertices \(v \in R\) by their exact \(N\)-bit neighborhood mask \(M_v \in [0, 2^N)\), accumulate \(g[T] = \sum_{M \subseteq T} \text{cnt}[M]\) via the \(O(N 2^N)\) Fast Zeta Transform (SOS DP), and check \(|R| - g[L \setminus S] \ge K|S|\) for all \(S \subseteq L\).
  • When \(N, M \le 6\) and \(a_i, b_j \le 4\) (CF 1519 F — Chests and Keys): If we must pay costs on edges to satisfy Hall’s condition across all \(2^6 = 64\) subsets simultaneously, tracking the boolean satisfaction mask of \(64\) subsets would take \(2^{64}\) states (impossible!). Instead, flip back from Dual (Hall) to Primal (Flow): track the remaining supply tuple \((rem_1, \dots, rem_6) \in [0, 4]^6\) across the \(M \le 6\) locks, which has at most \(5^6 = 15,625\) states!

Engine 5: Constructive Hall Tree Realization (AGC 029 F) & Hall–Kotzig Leaf-Peeling

Given \(N - 1\) vertex subsets \(E_1, \dots, E_{N-1} \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\) (AGC 029 F — Construction of a tree), when can we choose one edge \(e_i \subseteq E_i\) per subset to form a tree on \(\{1, \dots, N\}\)?

  1. Rooted Parent–Child Orientation: Root the target tree at vertex \(r = 1\). In any rooted tree, every non-root vertex \(v \in \{2, \dots, N\}\) has a unique parent edge, establishing a bijection (perfect matching) \(M\) between the \(N - 1\) hyperedges \(\{E_1, \dots, E_{N-1}\}\) and the \(N - 1\) non-root vertices \(V \setminus \{1\}\) such that \(M(E_i) \in E_i\).
  2. Connectivity via Alternating BFS: Run Hopcroft–Karp to find a perfect matching \(M\) between \(\{E_1, \dots, E_{N-1}\}\) and \(V \setminus \{1\}\) (if none exists, output -1). However, a perfect matching alone could form a disconnected cycle (e.g., \(E_1 = \{2, 3\}, E_2 = \{2, 3\}\) matched to \(2\) and \(3\), isolated from root \(1\)!). To orient edges away from root \(1\) without cycles, run a BFS starting from \(r = 1\): whenever vertex \(u\) is visited, for every unvisited hyperedge \(E_i\) containing \(u\), set \(u\) as the parent endpoint of \(E_i\), and push its matched child vertex \(v = M(E_i)\) into the BFS queue!
  3. Why One Matching Suffices: If the BFS visits all \(N\) vertices, we have constructed a valid tree! If the BFS stalls after visiting \(V_{\text{vis}} \subsetneq V\) and \(E_{\text{vis}} \subsetneq \{E_1, \dots, E_{N-1}\}\) (\(|V_{\text{vis}}| = |E_{\text{vis}}| + 1\)), then the unvisited hyperedges \(S = \mathcal{E} \setminus E_{\text{vis}}\) contain zero vertices in \(V_{\text{vis}}\), so \(\bigcup_{E_i \in S} E_i \subseteq V \setminus V_{\text{vis}}\) has size \(N - |V_{\text{vis}}| = (N - 1) - |E_{\text{vis}}| = |S| < |S| + 1\), proving that no valid tree exists for any matching!
// Constructive Hall Tree Realization (AGC 029 F) after finding perfect matching
// match_v[i] in {1 .. N-1} (non-root vertices) for each hyperedge i in {0 .. N-2}.
// inc_edges[u] lists all hyperedges i containing vertex u (0-indexed, root = 0).
vector<pair<int, int>> reconstruct_hall_tree(int N, const vector<int>& match_v,
                                             const vector<vector<int>>& inc_edges) {
    vector<bool> vis_v(N, false), vis_e(N - 1, false);
    vector<pair<int, int>> tree_edges(N - 1);
    queue<int> q;
    vis_v[0] = true;
    q.push(0);
    int visited_count = 1;
    while (!q.empty()) {
        int u = q.front();
        q.pop();
        for (int e_idx : inc_edges[u]) {
            if (!vis_e[e_idx]) {
                vis_e[e_idx] = true;
                int v = match_v[e_idx]; // Unique matched child vertex of hyperedge e_idx
                tree_edges[e_idx] = {u, v};
                if (!vis_v[v]) {
                    vis_v[v] = true;
                    ++visited_count;
                    q.push(v);
                }
            }
        }
    }
    return visited_count == N ? tree_edges : vector<pair<int, int>>{};
}

Problem #057 — [SCOI2010] 部落战争 (Tribal War) & Minimum DAG Path Cover

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Stepping Stone 1 (SS-1) | Judge URL: Luogu P2172 (Companion: Luogu P2764 — 最小路径覆盖问题)
  • Constraints: \(M, N \le 50\) (\(M\) rows, \(N\) columns), jump parameters \(1 \le R, C \le 50\), Time Limit \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: You are given an \(M \times N\) grid where . denotes an enemy base that must be conquered and x denotes an impassable lake. Each army starts at any . cell and makes a sequence of jumps. Every jump must move strictly downward (\(\Delta r > 0\)) using one of the four knight-like displacement vectors \((+R, +C)\), \((+R, -C)\), \((+C, +R)\), or \((+C, -R)\). Each . cell must be visited by exact/at least one army, and no two armies may ever step on the same . cell. Find the minimum number of armies required to conquer all . cells.
NoteHint 1 — Why Is This a DAG and Not a General Graph?

Notice that every valid jump strictly increases the row coordinate (\(\Delta r \in \{+R, +C\} > 0\)). Because an army can never return to a previous or same row, the directed movement graph \(G = (V, E)\) on the . cells is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)!

NoteHint 2 — Translating Disjoint Trajectories to Node Degrees

Since no two armies may share a . cell, the trajectories partition the . cells into vertex-disjoint directed paths. How does choosing a jump edge \((u \to v)\) affect the total number of armies needed?

NoteHint 3 — Bipartite Split Graph Compilation

Split each . cell \(u\) into an outgoing port \(u_{\text{out}} \in L\) and an incoming port \(u_{\text{in}} \in R\). Wire \((u_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}})\) for every valid downward jump \(u \to v\) between two . cells, and compute \((\text{number of '.' cells}) - \text{MaxMatching}(L, R)\).

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #057 (Luogu P2172 & P2764)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

In general knight-move grid problems (Chapter 7), moves can go in all \(8\) directions, requiring a parity coloring. Here, moves only go strictly downward (\(\Delta r > 0\)), which immediately guarantees acyclicity (DAG). Furthermore, the rule “every . cell is visited, and no two armies share a . cell” asks for the Minimum Vertex-Disjoint Path Cover (MDPC) of this DAG.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(V\) be the set of all . cells (\(|V| = K \le 2500\)), and let \(E\) be the set of valid downward jumps \((u \to v)\) between . cells. Let \(x_{uv} \in \{0, 1\}\) indicate whether an army jumps directly from \(u\) to \(v\). Because at most one army leaves \(u\) and at most one army enters \(v\):

\[\sum_{v : (u, v) \in E} x_{uv} \le 1 \quad (\forall u \in V), \qquad \sum_{u : (u, v) \in E} x_{uv} \le 1 \quad (\forall v \in V).\]

Starting from \(K\) armies of length \(0\) (one per . cell), each selected jump \(x_{uv} = 1\) merges the army ending at \(u\) with the army starting at \(v\), reducing the army count by \(1\):

\[\min \text{Armies} = K - \max_{x} \sum_{(u, v) \in E} x_{uv}.\]

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  • Create bipartite graph \(G_{\text{split}} = (V_{\text{out}} \sqcup V_{\text{in}}, E_{\text{split}})\) with \(|V_{\text{out}}| = |V_{\text{in}}| = K\).
  • For each . cell \(u = (r, c)\), iterate over the displacement vectors \((\Delta r, \Delta c) \in \{(R, C), (R, -C), (C, R), (C, -R)\}\). If \(v = (r + \Delta r, c + \Delta c)\) is in bounds and is also ., add edge \((u_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}})\).
  • Run Hopcroft–Karp / Dinic on \(G_{\text{split}}\) and return \(K - \nu(G_{\text{split}})\).
  • (Path Reconstruction for Luogu P2764): Track next_v[u] \(= v\) if \((u_{\text{out}}, v_{\text{in}}) \in M^*\) and has_prev[v] = true. Every \(u\) with !has_prev[u] is the start of a path; walk u = next_v[u] to print each trajectory.

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

  • Toy Trace: Suppose \(3 \times 3\) grid with \(R = 1, C = 1\), and . cells at \(u_1 = (1, 2)\), \(u_2 = (2, 1)\), \(u_3 = (2, 3)\), \(u_4 = (3, 2)\). The jumps are \(u_1 \to u_2\), \(u_1 \to u_3\), \(u_2 \to u_4\), \(u_3 \to u_4\). In \(G_{\text{split}}\), we can match \((u_{1,\text{out}}, u_{2,\text{in}})\) and \((u_{2,\text{out}}, u_{4,\text{in}})\), giving \(\nu = 2\) (since \(u_1\) cannot match to both \(u_2\) and \(u_3\), and \(u_4\) cannot receive from both \(u_2\) and \(u_3\)). Thus \(K - \nu = 4 - 2 = 2\) armies: \((u_1 \to u_2 \to u_4)\) and \((u_3)\).
  • Failure-Mode Guard: When \(R = C\), the \(4\) displacement vectors \((R, \pm C)\) and \((C, \pm R)\) contain duplicates! Always deduplicate the move vector list (e.g., via sort + unique) before adding edges. Complexity: \(O(K\sqrt{K}) \le 2500 \times 50 \approx 1.25 \cdot 10^5\) operations.
// Core Graph Compilation for #057 [SCOI2010] Tribal War
int solve_tribal_war(int M, int N, int R, int C, const vector<string>& grid) {
    vector<pair<int, int>> dirs = {{R, C}, {R, -C}, {C, R}, {C, -R}};
    sort(dirs.begin(), dirs.end());
    dirs.erase(unique(dirs.begin(), dirs.end()), dirs.end());

    vector<vector<int>> id(M, vector<int>(N, -1));
    int K = 0;
    for (int r = 0; r < M; ++r)
        for (int c = 0; c < N; ++c)
            if (grid[r][c] == '.') id[r][c] = K++;

    int S = 2 * K, T = 2 * K + 1;
    Dinic dinic(2 * K + 2, S, T);
    for (int u = 0; u < K; ++u) {
        dinic.add_edge(S, u, 1);
        dinic.add_edge(u + K, T, 1);
    }
    for (int r = 0; r < M; ++r) {
        for (int c = 0; c < N; ++c) {
            if (id[r][c] == -1) continue;
            for (auto [dr, dc] : dirs) {
                int nr = r + dr, nc = c + dc;
                if (nr >= 0 && nr < M && nc >= 0 && nc < N && id[nr][nc] != -1) {
                    dinic.add_edge(id[r][c], id[nr][nc] + K, 1);
                }
            }
        }
    }
    return K - dinic.max_flow();
}

Problem #058 — 魔术球问题 (Magic Ball — Incremental DAG Path Cover) & Dynamic Interval Hall (POI 2009 LYZ)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Stepping Stone 2 (SS-2) | Judge URL: Luogu P2765 (Companion: POI 2009 / Luogu P3488 — LYZ-Ice Skates)
  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 55\) pegs (for P2765, \(M \le 1600\) balls); for companion P3488, \(N, M \le 2 \cdot 10^5\), Time Limit \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: You have \(N \le 55\) pegs. You place numbered balls \(1, 2, 3, \dots, M\) sequentially onto the pegs. A ball \(v\) can either be placed on an empty peg, or placed on top of an existing ball \(u\) (\(u < v\)) if and only if \(u + v\) is a perfect square (\(u + v = k^2\)). Find the maximum number of balls \(M\) that can be placed on the \(N\) pegs, and print the sequence of balls on each peg.
NoteHint 1 — Representing Each Peg as a DAG Path

Because balls are placed in increasing order \(1, 2, \dots, M\), a ball \(v\) can only sit directly on top of a smaller ball \(u < v\) with \(u + v = k^2\). Thus the sequence of balls on a single peg is a directed path in the DAG \(G_M\) with vertices \(\{1, \dots, M\}\) and edges \(u \to v \iff (u < v \land u + v = k^2)\)!

NoteHint 2 — Incremental Residual Augmentation

We want the largest \(M\) such that \(\text{MDPC}(G_M) = M - \nu(G_{\text{split}}(G_M)) \le N\). Instead of binary searching \(M\) and rebuilding the graph from scratch, what happens to the residual network when we insert ball \(v = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) one by one?

NoteHint 3 — Stopping Condition & Peg Trajectory Recovery

When inserting ball \(v\), add \(S \to v_{\text{out}}\), \(v_{\text{in}} \to T\), and \(u_{\text{out}} \to v_{\text{in}}\) for all \(1 \le u < v\) with \(u + v = k^2\), then run dinic.max_flow() on the existing residual network! If \(\Delta\text{flow} = 1\), the required peg count stays unchanged; if \(\Delta\text{flow} = 0\), the required peg count increases by \(1\). Stop as soon as \(v - \text{total\_flow} == N + 1\) (so \(M = v - 1\)).

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #058 (Luogu P2765 & Companion P3488)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

Each peg holds a strictly increasing chain \(u_1 < u_2 < \dots < u_len\) where consecutive elements sum to a square, and each ball \(1 \dots M\) sits on exactly one peg. Thus \(M\) balls fit on \(N\) pegs iff the DAG \(G_M\) has Minimum Disjoint Path Cover \(\text{MDPC}(G_M) \le N\). Because adding vertex \(v\) only adds incoming DAG edges from \(u < v\), \(\text{MDPC}(G_v) = v - \nu(G_{\text{split}}(G_v))\) is monotonically non-decreasing in \(v\) (incrementing by either \(0\) or \(+1\) at each step)!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

For each \(v = 1, 2, \dots\), let \(\nu_v\) be the maximum matching size in \(G_{\text{split}}(G_v)\). The minimum number of pegs needed for \(\{1, \dots, v\}\) is \(P(v) = v - \nu_v\). We seek \(M = \max \{v : v - \nu_v \le N\}\). (Note: By Hall/Dilworth structure on square-sum graphs, the exact threshold turns out to be \(M(N) = \lfloor (N + 1)^2 / 2 \rfloor - 1 \le 1567\) for \(N = 55\), so incremental Dinic finishes in \(< 5\text{ ms}\)!)

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  • Pre-allocate Dinic with \(S = 0, T = 1\), and dynamic node pairs \(v_{\text{out}} = 2v\), \(v_{\text{in}} = 2v + 1\) up to \(v_{\max} = 1600\).
  • For \(v = 1, 2, \dots\):
    • Add \(S \xrightarrow{1} 2v\), \(2v + 1 \xrightarrow{1} T\), and \(2u \xrightarrow{1} 2v + 1\) for every \(1 \le u < v\) where \(u + v = k^2\).
    • Augment the existing residual flow: flow += dinic.max_flow().
    • If \(v - \text{flow} > N\), Roll back / ignore vertex \(v\), set \(M = v - 1\), and reconstruct the \(N\) paths from \(\{1, \dots, M\}\).

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

  • Toy Trace (\(N = 2\) pegs):
    • \(v = 1\): no \(u < 1\), \(\nu_1 = 0 \implies 1 - 0 = 1\) peg ([1]).
    • \(v = 2\): \(1+2=3 \neq k^2\), \(\nu_2 = 0 \implies 2 - 0 = 2\) pegs ([1], [2]).
    • \(v = 3\): \(1+3=4=2^2 \implies\) match \((1_{\text{out}}, 3_{\text{in}})\), \(\nu_3 = 1 \implies 3 - 1 = 2\) pegs ([1, 3], [2]).
    • \(v = 4\): no \(u \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) gives a new augmenting path (\(1+4=5, 2+4=6, 3+4=7\)), so \(\nu_4 = 1 \implies 4 - 1 = 3 > 2\) pegs! Stop and output \(M = 3\).
  • Failure-Mode Guard: When \(v = M + 1\) triggers the stop condition \(v - \text{flow} == N + 1\), vertex \(v\) has zero matched edges, so when reconstructing the \(N\) paths, simply restrict your loop to \(u \in \{1, \dots, M\}\) so you do not print \(v = M + 1\) as an \((N+1)\)-th peg!

Problem #059 — [TJOI2015] 组合数学 (Treasure Grid Weighted Dilworth)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Intermediate 1 (Int-1) | Judge URL: Luogu P3974
  • Constraints: \(T \le 50\) test cases, \(1 \le N, M \le 1000\), cell treasure counts \(0 \le a_{i,j} \le 10^6\), Time Limit \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: You are given an \(N \times M\) grid where cell \((i, j)\) contains \(a_{i,j}\) treasures. In each pass, you start at the top-left corner \((1, 1)\) and walk to the bottom-right corner \((N, M)\) moving only Right \((i, j+1)\) or Down \((i+1, j)\). Whenever you step on a cell \((i, j)\) that still has treasures, you may pick up at most \(1\) treasure from that cell during that pass. Find the minimum number of passes required to collect all treasures in the grid.
NoteHint 1 — Why Flow on \(10^6\) Vertices TLEs & The Poset Viewpoint

With \(N, M = 1000\), the grid has \(N M = 10^6\) cells and \(T = 50\) test cases—explicit Min-Flow or MCMF will TLE! Instead, view each cell \((i, j)\) as an element of a poset with multiplicity \(a_{i,j}\), where \((i_1, j_1) \preceq (i_2, j_2) \iff (i_1 \le i_2 \land j_1 \le j_2)\). What is a Right-Down path in this poset?

NoteHint 2 — Applying Weighted Dilworth’s Theorem

A Right-Down path is a chain in the 2D coordinate dominance poset! By Weighted Dilworth’s Theorem, the minimum number of chains needed to cover all cell multiplicities \(a_{i,j}\) equals the Maximum-Weight Antichain of the poset. What does an antichain look like geometrically on an \(N \times M\) grid?

NoteHint 3 — Strict Up-Right / Bottom-Left Antichain DP

Two cells \((i_1, j_1)\) and \((i_2, j_2)\) are incomparable iff one lies strictly down-and-left (or up-and-right) of the other: \((i_1 > i_2 \land j_1 < j_2)\). Sweep \(i = 1 \dots N\) and \(j = M \dots 1\) with:

\[dp[i][j] = \max\big(dp[i - 1][j], \; dp[i][j + 1], \; dp[i - 1][j + 1] + a_{i,j}\big).\]

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #059 (Luogu P3974)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

\(N, M \le 1000\) (\(10^6\) vertices) immediately rules out any graph flow algorithm. However, the trajectories are monotone Right-Down chains on \(\mathbb{Z}^2\), where \((r_1, c_1)\) can reach \((r_2, c_2)\) iff \(r_1 \le r_2\) and \(c_1 \le c_2\). Since coordinate dominance is already reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive, the grid is a poset \(\mathcal{P}\), and covering node weights \(a_{i,j}\) with the minimum number of chains is the exact dual of Maximum-Weight Antichain (Weighted Dilworth’s Theorem)!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Replace each cell \((i, j)\) of weight \(a_{i,j}\) with a chain of \(a_{i,j}\) unit clones (or apply weighted Dilworth directly). Any antichain \(A \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\} \times \{1, \dots, M\}\) can be ordered by strictly increasing row indices \(i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k\), which forces strictly decreasing column indices \(j_1 > j_2 > \dots > j_k\):

\[\text{Min Passes} = \max_{\substack{1 \le i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k \le N \\ M \ge j_1 > j_2 > \dots > j_k \ge 1}} \sum_{t=1}^k a_{i_t, j_t}.\]

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Compilation to 2D DP)

Let \(dp[i][j]\) be the maximum weight of an antichain contained in the top-right subgrid \([1 \dots i] \times [j \dots M]\):

  • Skip cell \((i, j)\): Transition from \(dp[i - 1][j]\) (move down without picking) or \(dp[i][j + 1]\) (move left without picking).
  • Pick cell \((i, j)\): Every previously picked cell \((i', j')\) in the top-right subgrid must satisfy \(i' \le i - 1\) and \(j' \ge j + 1\), so its best sum is \(dp[i - 1][j + 1] + a_{i,j}\).

Combining both cases gives the \(O(NM)\) recurrence:

\[dp[i][j] = \max\Big(\max\big(dp[i - 1][j], \; dp[i][j + 1]\big), \; dp[i - 1][j + 1] + a_{i,j}\Big).\]

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

  • Toy Trace: Consider the \(2 \times 2\) grid \(a = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\).
    • \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 1)\) are incomparable (\(1 < 2\) and \(2 > 1\)), forming an antichain of weight \(a_{1,2} + a_{2,1} = 5 + 4 = 9\).
    • No single chain can visit both \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 1)\), so at least \(9\) passes are needed, while \((1, 1) \to \dots \to (2, 2)\) easily absorbs \(a_{1,1} = 1\) and \(a_{2,2} = 2\) along those \(9\) passes! Our DP computes \(dp[1][2] = 5\), and \(dp[2][1] = \max(dp[1][1], dp[2][2], dp[1][2] + 4) = \max(1, 2, 5 + 4) = 9\).
  • Failure-Mode Guard: Never add \(+a_{i,j}\) to \(dp[i-1][j]\) or \(dp[i][j+1]\)! Cells \((i-1, j)\) and \((i, j)\) lie in the same column (\(j = j\)) and are comparable! Only the strict diagonal predecessor \((i - 1, j + 1)\) is incomparable with \((i, j)\).
// O(N * M) Weighted Dilworth Antichain DP for #059 [TJOI2015] Treasure Grid
long long solve_treasure_grid(int N, int M, const vector<vector<long long>>& a) {
    vector<vector<long long>> dp(N + 2, vector<long long>(M + 2, 0));
    for (int i = 1; i <= N; ++i) {
        for (int j = M; j >= 1; --j) {
            dp[i][j] = max({dp[i - 1][j], dp[i][j + 1], dp[i - 1][j + 1] + a[i][j]});
        }
    }
    return dp[N][1];
}

Problem #060 — CF 1009 G: Allowed Letters (Lexicographically Smallest Matching via Bitmask Hall)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Intermediate 2 (Int-2) | Judge URL: Codeforces 1009G
  • Constraints: String length \(1 \le N \le 10^5\), alphabet \(\Sigma = \{\text{'a'}, \text{'b'}, \text{'c'}, \text{'d'}, \text{'e'}, \text{'f'}\}\) (\(|\Sigma| = 6\)), Time Limit \(2.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: You are given a string \(S\) of length \(N \le 10^5\) consisting of letters 'a' through 'f'. You may rearrange the characters of \(S\) in any order, subject to \(m\) positional restrictions: at index \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), only a subset of characters \(M_i \subseteq \{0, \dots, 5\}\) is allowed (if no restriction is given for index \(i\), all \(6\) letters are allowed, so \(M_i = 63\)). Output the lexicographically smallest valid string, or Impossible if no valid rearrangement exists.
NoteHint 1 — Standard Lexicographical Greedy + Feasibility Oracle

To construct the lexicographically smallest string, iterate through positions \(i = 1, 2, \dots, N\) from left to right. At position \(i\), try placing the smallest allowed character \(c \in \{0, \dots, 5\}\) (\(c \in M_i\) and \(\text{char\_cnt}[c] > 0\)) such that the remaining \(N - i\) characters can still be matched to the remaining \(N - i\) positions!

NoteHint 2 — Compressing \(10^5\) Positions into \(|\Sigma| = 6\) Character Subsets via Hall

Running Dinic at every position \(i = 1 \dots 10^5\) would take \(O(N^2)\) and TLE! Instead, look at the bipartite graph between the \(6\) character types (total supply \(\text{char\_cnt}[0 \dots 5]\)) and the \(2^6 = 64\) position-mask types (supply \(\text{mask\_cnt}[0 \dots 63]\)). What does Hall’s Marriage Theorem say when applied to subsets \(A \subseteq \{0, \dots, 5\}\)?

NoteHint 3 — Complement / Subset Inequality Across All \(2^6 = 64\) Masks

For any subset of positions whose allowed masks \(m\) fall entirely inside a character subset \(A \subseteq \{0, \dots, 5\}\) (\(m \subseteq A\)), those positions can only receive characters from \(A\)! By Hall’s Theorem (Theorem 3.1), a valid completion exists iff for all \(64\) masks \(A \in [0, 64)\):

\[f[A] := \sum_{m \subseteq A} \text{mask\_cnt}[m] \le \sum_{c \in A} \text{char\_cnt}[c].\]

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #060 (CF 1009 G)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

We have \(N = 10^5\) positions on the left and \(|\Sigma| = 6\) character types on the right. The extreme asymmetry (\(6\) vs. \(10^5\)) is the textbook diagnostic signal for Bitmask Hall Flow Elimination: checking all \(2^6 = 64\) subsets of \(\Sigma\) takes \(64\) operations per query, completely eliminating any flow network!

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

At any point during the left-to-right sweep, let \(\text{char\_cnt}[c]\) be the remaining supply of letter \(c \in \{0, \dots, 5\}\), and let \(\text{mask\_cnt}[m]\) be the number of remaining positions \(j > i\) with allowed mask \(M_j = m\). Applying Hall’s Theorem (Theorem 3.1(3)) to every subset of characters \(A \subseteq \Sigma\): the positions that can only be matched to characters in \(A\) are precisely those with \(M_j \subseteq A\). Their total count cannot exceed the available supply of characters in \(A\):

\[\forall A \in [0, 64), \qquad f[A] := \sum_{m \subseteq A} \text{mask\_cnt}[m] \;\le\; \text{char\_sum}[A] := \sum_{c \in A} \text{char\_cnt}[c].\]

(Equivalently, \(\sum_{c \in \bar{A}} \text{char\_cnt}[c] \le \sum_{m \cap \bar{A} \neq \emptyset} \text{mask\_cnt}[m]\) since total remaining characters equal total remaining positions!)

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Compilation)

  1. Count initial frequencies \(\text{char\_cnt}[0 \dots 5]\) and \(\text{mask\_cnt}[0 \dots 63]\), and initialize \(f[A] = \sum_{m \subseteq A} \text{mask\_cnt}[m]\) for \(A \in [0, 64)\).
  2. For each position \(i = 1, \dots, N\):
    • First remove position \(i\) from the remaining pool: decrement \(f[A]\) for all \(A\) with \((M_i \ \& \ A) == M_i\).
    • Try \(c = 0, 1, \dots, 5\) in increasing order: if \(((M_i \gg c) \ \& \ 1)\) and \(\text{char\_cnt}[c] > 0\), tentatively decrement \(\text{char\_cnt}[c]\) and check if \(f[A] \le \text{char\_sum}[A]\) holds for all \(A \in [0, 64)\) (equivalently, whether every \(A\) containing bit \(c\) had slack \(\text{char\_sum}[A] - f[A] \ge 1\) before decrementing \(\text{char\_cnt}[c]\)!).
    • If valid, append 'a' + c to the answer and move to \(i + 1\); otherwise restore \(\text{char\_cnt}[c]\). If no \(c\) works, output Impossible.

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

  • Toy Trace: \(S = \text{"ab"}\), Position 1 allows \(\{\text{'a'}, \text{'b'}\} (m = 3)\), Position 2 allows \(\{\text{'a'}\} (m = 1)\).
    • At \(i = 1\), removing \(M_1 = 3\) leaves Position 2 with \(M_2 = 1\), so \(f[\{a\}] = 1\).
    • If we try \(c = \text{'a'}\) at \(i = 1\), remaining \(\text{char\_cnt}[\text{'a'}]\) drops to \(0\), violating \(f[\{a\}] = 1 \le \text{char\_sum}[\{a\}] = 0\)!
    • So \(i = 1\) skips 'a' and picks 'b', leaving 'a' for Position 2 ("ba").
  • Failure-Mode Guard: Do not forget to check if the initial state at \(i = 1\) has any valid character \(c\); if the loop over \(c \in \{0 \dots 5\}\) finds no match at \(i = 1\) (or any \(i\)), return "Impossible" immediately. Complexity: \(O(N \cdot |\Sigma| 2^{|\Sigma|}) \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^7\) bitwise operations (\(\sim 40\text{ ms}\)).
// O(N * 6 * 64) Bitmask Hall Lexicographical Greedy for #060 (CF 1009 G)
string solve_allowed_letters(const string& s, const vector<int>& mask_at) {
    int N = s.size();
    array<int, 6> char_cnt{};
    for (char ch : s) char_cnt[ch - 'a']++;

    array<int, 64> f{};
    for (int m : mask_at) {
        for (int A = 0; A < 64; ++A)
            if ((A & m) == m) f[A]++;
    }
    string ans;
    ans.reserve(N);
    for (int i = 0; i < N; ++i) {
        int m = mask_at[i];
        for (int A = 0; A < 64; ++A)
            if ((A & m) == m) f[A]--;

        int chosen = -1;
        for (int c = 0; c < 6; ++c) {
            if (!((m >> c) & 1) || char_cnt[c] == 0) continue;
            char_cnt[c]--;
            bool ok = true;
            for (int A = 0; A < 64; ++A) {
                int char_sum = 0;
                for (int b = 0; b < 6; ++b)
                    if ((A >> b) & 1) char_sum += char_cnt[b];
                if (f[A] > char_sum) { ok = false; break; }
            }
            if (ok) { chosen = c; break; }
            char_cnt[c]++;
        }
        if (chosen == -1) return "Impossible";
        ans.push_back(char('a' + chosen));
    }
    return ans;
}

Problem #061 — [CTSC2008] 祭祀 (Sacrifice — Three-Part Constructive Dilworth Antichain)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Advanced 1 (Adv-1) | Judge URL: Luogu P4298
  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 100\) vertices, \(1 \le M \le 1000\) directed edges (DAG), Time Limit \(1.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: Given a directed acyclic graph \(G = (V, E)\) with \(N \le 100\) river shrines:
    1. Find the maximum number \(K\) of shrines that can be selected simultaneously such that no directed path exists between any two selected shrines.
    2. Output a binary string of length \(N\) representing one explicit valid selection of size \(K\).
    3. Output a second binary string of length \(N\) where the \(u\)-th bit is 1 iff shrine \(u\) can belong to at least one valid selection of size \(K\).
NoteHint 1 — Subproblem (1): Why You Must Run Floyd–Warshall First (FM-08)

“No directed path exists between any two selected shrines” means no \(u\) can reach \(v\) via a path of any length \(\ge 1\). Before building the bipartite split graph \(G_{\text{split}}\), you must compute the transitive closure \(\text{reach}[u][v]\) via Floyd–Warshall! Then \(K = N - \nu\big(G_{\text{split}}(\text{TC}(G))\big)\).

NoteHint 2 — Subproblem (2): Constructive Antichain via the Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma

By Lemma 2.1 (The Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma), in the canonical minimum vertex cover \(C^* = (V_{\text{out}} \setminus S_{\min}) \cup (V_{\text{in}} \cap S_{\min})\) of \(G_{\text{split}}(\text{TC}(G))\), no shrine \(u\) has both \(u_{\text{out}} \in C^*\) and \(u_{\text{in}} \in C^*\). Thus \(A^* = \{u \in V : u_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min} \land u_{\text{in}} \notin S_{\min}\}\) is guaranteed to be a valid antichain of size exactly \(K\)!

NoteHint 3 — Subproblem (3): Testing If Shrine \(u\) Can Belong to Some Maximum Antichain

If we force shrine \(u\) into our antichain, which other shrines are immediately forbidden? Every shrine \(w\) that is comparable with \(u\) (\(\text{reach}[u][w] \lor \text{reach}[w][u] \lor w == u\)) must be deleted! Delete \(u\) and all shrines comparable to \(u\), and recompute the maximum antichain size \(K_{-u}\) on the remaining induced poset: \(u\) can belong to a size-\(K\) antichain iff \(1 + K_{-u} == K\)!

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #061 (Luogu P4298 [CTSC2008] 祭祀)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

This is the definitive benchmark problem for Constructive Dilworth’s Theorem: it asks not only for the poset width \(K\) (Subproblem 1), but also for an explicit Maximum Antichain (Subproblem 2) and the union of all Maximum Antichains (Subproblem 3). Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(u \prec v \iff (u \neq v \land u \leadsto_G v)\).

  • Subproblem 1: \(K = \text{width}(\mathcal{P}) = N - \nu\big(G_{\text{split}}(\mathcal{P})\big)\).
  • Subproblem 2: By Lemma 2.1, \(A^* = \{u \in V : u_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min} \land u_{\text{in}} \notin S_{\min}\}\) satisfies \(|A^*| = N - |C^*| = K\).

    • Transitive closure adds \(0 \to 3\).

    • Residual BFS from \(S\) reaches \(1_{\text{out}}, 2_{\text{out}}, 3_{\text{out}}, 3_{\text{in}}\), so \(u_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min} \land u_{\text{in}} \notin S_{\min}\) selects \(A^* = \{1, 2\}\) (size \(2\)).
    • For Subproblem 3, forcing \(u = 0\) removes \(\{0, 1, 2, 3\}\) (\(|V_0| = 0 \neq K - 1\)), so only \(\{1, 2\}\) can belong to a size-\(2\) antichain.

// Complete 3-Part Solver for #061 [CTSC2008] Sacrifice (Luogu P4298)
void solve_sacrifice(int N, vector<vector<int>> reach) {
    for (int i = 0; i < N; ++i) reach[i][i] = 0;
    for (int k = 0; k < N; ++k)
        for (int i = 0; i < N; ++i)
            if (reach[i][k])
                for (int j = 0; j < N; ++j)
                    reach[i][j] |= reach[k][j];

    auto build_and_solve = [&](const vector<bool>& alive, vector<bool>& vis_S) {
        int S = 2 * N, T = 2 * N + 1, cnt_alive = 0;
        Dinic dinic(2 * N + 2, S, T);
        for (int u = 0; u < N; ++u) {
            if (!alive[u]) continue;
            ++cnt_alive;
            dinic.add_edge(S, u, 1);
            dinic.add_edge(u + N, T, 1);
            for (int v = 0; v < N; ++v)
                if (alive[v] && reach[u][v])
                    dinic.add_edge(u, v + N, 1); // Inf or 1 (both valid since unit side caps)
        }
        int width = cnt_alive - dinic.max_flow();
        vis_S = dinic.get_S_min(); // Residual BFS from S with c_f > 0
        return width;
    };

    vector<bool> all_alive(N, true), vis_S;
    int K = build_and_solve(all_alive, vis_S);
    string part2(N, '0'), part3(N, '0');
    for (int u = 0; u < N; ++u)
        if (vis_S[u] && !vis_S[u + N]) part2[u] = '1';

    for (int u = 0; u < N; ++u) {
        vector<bool> alive_u(N, false), dummy_vis;
        for (int w = 0; w < N; ++w)
            if (w != u && !reach[u][w] && !reach[w][u]) alive_u[w] = true;
        if (build_and_solve(alive_u, dummy_vis) == K - 1) part3[u] = '1';
    }
    // Print K, part2, part3
}

Problem #062 — AtCoder ARC 106 E: Medals (Binary Search + SOS DP Hall Elimination)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Advanced 2 (Adv-2) | Judge URL: AtCoder ARC 106 E (Companions: ARC 076 F — Exhausted? & CF 981 F — Round Marriage)
  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 18\) employees, \(1 \le K \le 10^5\) medals per employee, shift lengths \(1 \le A_i \le 10^5\), Time Limit \(2.5\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: \(N \le 18\) employees each need \(K \le 10^5\) medals. Employee \(i\) works for \(A_i\) consecutive days, then rests for \(A_i\) consecutive days, then works for \(A_i\) days, and so on (so on day \(t \ge 1\), employee \(i\) is at work iff \(\lfloor (t - 1) / A_i \rfloor\) is even). On each day, you may award at most \(1\) medal to one employee who is at work that day. Find the minimum number of days \(D\) required so that all \(N\) employees can receive \(K\) medals each.
NoteHint 1 — Upper Bound on \(D\) & Binary Search Monotonicity

Because each employee works at least half of all days (specifically, out of every \(2 A_i\) days they work \(A_i\) days), within \(D_{\max} = 2 N K \le 3.6 \cdot 10^6\) days, at least \(N K\) work-days exist even if we served employees one by one! Moreover, if \(D\) days suffice, then \(D + 1\) days also suffice—so we can binary search on \(D \in [N K, 2 N K]\)!

NoteHint 2 — Hall’s Marriage Theorem on \(N \le 18\) Employees vs. \(D\) Days

For a candidate deadline \(D\), we have a bipartite supply–demand graph between \(L = \{0, \dots, N-1\}\) (each needing \(K\) medals) and \(R = \{1, \dots, D\}\) (each day supplying \(1\) medal). By Hall’s Theorem (Theorem 3.1(2)), a valid assignment exists iff for every subset \(S \subseteq L\) (\(2^N\) subsets), the number of days in \([1, D]\) where at least one employee in \(S\) works is \(\ge K \cdot |S|\).

NoteHint 3 — Counting Disjoint Days via Complement Fast Zeta Transform (SOS DP)

For each day \(t \in [1, 2NK]\), precompute the \(N\)-bit mask mask[t] of employees working on day \(t\). Inside the binary search for \(D\), how many days \(t \in [1, D]\) have mask[t] intersecting \(S\) (\(\text{mask}[t] \cap S \neq \emptyset\))? By Theorem 3.1(3), it equals \(D\) minus the number of days where \(\text{mask}[t] \subseteq L \setminus S\), which is computed for all \(2^N\) masks simultaneously in \(O(N 2^N)\) via SOS DP (Fast Zeta Transform)!

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #062 (ARC 106 E)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

\(N \le 18\) on the left and \(D \le 3.6 \cdot 10^6\) on the right: explicit Dinic on \(3.6 \cdot 10^6\) vertices is impossible, while \(2^{18} = 262,144\) is tiny! This is the canonical trigger for Engine 4 (Section 4.2): Binary Search + Complement SOS DP Hall Elimination.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

Let \(m_t \in [0, 2^N)\) be the bitmask of employees working on day \(t \in \{1, \dots, D\}\). For any subset \(S \subseteq \{0, \dots, N-1\}\), the neighborhood size \(|N(S)|\) in \([1, D]\) is:

\[|N(S)| = \#\big\{t \in [1, D] : m_t \cap S \neq \emptyset\big\} = D - \#\big\{t \in [1, D] : m_t \subseteq \bar{S}\big\} = D - g[\bar{S}],\]

where \(\bar{S} = (2^N - 1) \oplus S\) and \(g[T] := \sum_{M \subseteq T} \text{cnt}_D[M]\) is the upward subset-sum of frequency array \(\text{cnt}_D[M] = \#\{t \in [1, D] : m_t = M\}\). By Hall’s Theorem, \(D\) is feasible iff:

\[\forall S \in [1, 2^N), \qquad D - g\big[(2^N - 1) \oplus S\big] \ge K \cdot \text{popcount}(S).\]

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Compilation)

  1. Precompute mask[t] for \(t = 1, \dots, 2NK\) once outside the binary search in \(O(N \cdot NK)\) time by toggling bit \(i\) every \(A_i\) days (avoiding slow % division in the inner loop!).
  2. Binary search \(D \in [NK, 2NK]\) (\(\approx 22\) iterations):
    • Zero-fill \(g[0 \dots 2^N - 1]\), and for \(t = 1 \dots D\), increment \(g[\text{mask}[t]]\).
    • Run Fast Zeta Transform: for \(i = 0 \dots N-1\), for \(T = 0 \dots 2^N - 1\), if \((T \gg i) \ \& \ 1\), do \(g[T] += g[T \oplus (1 \ll i)]\).
    • Check \(D - g[(2^N - 1) \oplus S] \ge K \cdot \text{popcount}(S)\) for all \(S\).

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

  • Toy Trace: \(N = 2, K = 1, A = [1, 2]\).
    • Day 1: both work (\(m_1 = 3\)). Day 2: only emp 1 (\(A_1=2\)) works (\(m_2 = 2\)).
    • At \(D = 2\): \(\text{cnt}[3] = 1, \text{cnt}[2] = 1\). For \(S = \{0, 1\} = 3\), \(\bar{S} = 0\), \(g[0] = 0 \implies 2 - 0 \ge 2\) (OK). For \(S = \{0\} = 1\), \(\bar{S} = 2\), \(g[2] = \text{cnt}[0] + \text{cnt}[2] = 1 \implies 2 - 1 = 1 \ge 1\) (OK). So \(D = 2\) is feasible!
  • Failure-Mode Guard: Never recompute mask[t] using (t - 1) / A[i] % 2 inside the binary search! Precomputing mask[1 .. 2NK] before the binary search cuts the runtime from TLE (\(> 3\text{ s}\)) to \(250\text{ ms}\). Complexity: \(O(N^2 K + N 2^N \log(NK))\).
// O(N^2 K + N 2^N log(NK)) SOS DP Hall Solver for #062 (ARC 106 E — Medals)
int solve_medals(int N, int K, const vector<int>& A) {
    int MAXD = 2 * N * K;
    vector<int> mask(MAXD + 1, 0);
    for (int i = 0; i < N; ++i) {
        for (int start = 1; start <= MAXD; start += 2 * A[i]) {
            int end_day = min(MAXD, start + A[i] - 1);
            for (int t = start; t <= end_day; ++t) mask[t] |= (1 << i);
        }
    }
    int full_mask = (1 << N) - 1;
    vector<int> g(1 << N);
    auto check = [&](int D) {
        fill(g.begin(), g.end(), 0);
        for (int t = 1; t <= D; ++t) g[mask[t]]++;
        for (int i = 0; i < N; ++i)
            for (int T = 0; T <= full_mask; ++T)
                if ((T >> i) & 1) g[T] += g[T ^ (1 << i)];
        for (int S = 1; S <= full_mask; ++S)
            if (D - g[full_mask ^ S] < K * __builtin_popcount(S)) return false;
        return true;
    };
    int lo = N * K, hi = MAXD;
    while (lo < hi) {
        int mid = lo + (hi - lo) / 2;
        if (check(mid)) hi = mid;
        else lo = mid + 1;
    }
    return lo;
}

Problem #064 — CF 1089 B: Guests (Tutte’s 2-for-1 Twin Gadget & General Graph Blossom Matching)

  • Canonical Slot: Ch 08 — Mastery 2 (Mas-2) | Judge URL: Codeforces 1089B
  • Constraints: \(T\) test cases, \(1 \le N, M \le 150\) (\(N\) guests, \(M\) bedrooms), \(\sum (N + M) \le 600\), Time Limit \(2.0\text{ s}\).
  • Problem Statement: A hotel has \(N \le 150\) guests and \(M \le 150\) two-bed bedrooms. You are given the bipartite preference matrix between guests and bedrooms. Two guests can be paired into a bedroom \(r\) if and only if both guests like bedroom \(r\) (each bedroom can hold at most one pair of guests, and each guest can be assigned to at most one bedroom). Maximize the total number of guest pairs formed.
NoteHint 1 — Why Bipartite Flow Cannot Enforce ‘2 Guests or 0 Guests’

If we simply give each bedroom \(r\) a sink capacity of \(c(r, T) = 2\) in a bipartite flow network, the max-flow solver will happily send \(1\) unit of flow into bedroom \(r\) (assigning a single unpaired guest to \(r\)) and count it toward the total flow! We need bedroom \(r\) to contribute \(+1\) to the objective only when \(2\) guests are matched to it, and \(0\) net gain when \(0\) or \(1\) guest is matched to it!

NoteHint 2 — Tutte’s 2-for-1 Twin Gadget (r_1 — r_2 Internal Edge)

Split each bedroom \(r \in \{1, \dots, M\}\) into two bed vertices \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), and connect them with an internal edge \((r_1, r_2)\)! Connect every guest \(g\) who likes room \(r\) to both \(r_1\) and \(r_2\). Now analyze how many matched edges are Incident to \(\{r_1, r_2\}\) in any maximum matching of this general graph!

NoteHint 3 — Counting the Net Matching Gain & Edmonds’ Blossom Algorithm

In the general graph on \(N + 2M\) vertices:

  • If \(0\) guests match to \(\{r_1, r_2\}\), the internal edge \((r_1, r_2)\) matches with itself: \(1\) matched edge.
  • If \(1\) guest \(g\) matches to \(r_1\), then \((r_1, r_2)\) is broken and \(r_2\) is left unmatched: still \(1\) matched edge (\(0\) net gain!).
  • If \(2\) guests \(g_a, g_b\) match to \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), we get \(2\) matched edges (\(+1\) net gain over the baseline!).

Therefore, \(\text{MaxGeneralMatching}(G) - M\) equals the exact maximum number of guest pairs!

TipFull 4-Step Compilation & Solution — #064 (CF 1089 B — Guests)

Step 1: Non-Graph Diagnostic Signal (Recognition)

At first sight, “guests and bedrooms” looks bipartite. However, the objective pairs two guests \(g_a, g_b \in L\) through a shared bedroom \(r \in R\). This is a capacitated parity / 2-for-1 packing problem (\(N, M \le 150\)), which crosses the boundary from Bipartite Matching into General Graph Matching (Edmonds’ Blossom Algorithm) via Tutte’s Twin-Clique Gadget.

Step 2: Intermediate Algebraic Representation

For each bedroom \(r \in \{1, \dots, M\}\), let \(k_r \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) be the number of guests assigned to room \(r\). We want to maximize \(\sum_{r=1}^M \lfloor k_r / 2 \rfloor\). Notice the algebraic identity for \(k_r \in \{0, 1, 2\}\):

\[1 + \left\lfloor \frac{k_r}{2} \right\rfloor = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k_r = 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } k_r = 1, \\ 2 & \text{if } k_r = 2. \end{cases}\]

How can a vertex pair \(\{r_1, r_2\}\) contribute \(1\) to the matching size when \(k_r \in \{0, 1\}\) and \(2\) when \(k_r = 2\)? By placing an edge \((r_1, r_2)\) between them! When \(k_r = 0\), \((r_1, r_2)\) is matched (\(+1\)); when \(k_r = 1\), one guest matches to \(r_1\) and \((r_1, r_2)\) is unmatched (\(+1\)); when \(k_r = 2\), two guests match to \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) (\(+2\)). Summing over all \(M\) bedrooms yields:

\[\nu(G_{\text{twin}}) = \sum_{r=1}^M \left(1 + \left\lfloor \frac{k_r}{2} \right\rfloor\right) = M + \sum_{r=1}^M \left\lfloor \frac{k_r}{2} \right\rfloor \implies \max \text{Pairs} = \nu(G_{\text{twin}}) - M.\]

Step 3: Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Mechanical Graph Compilation)

  • Create a general undirected graph \(G_{\text{twin}}\) with \(V = N + 2M \le 450\) vertices:
    • Guests are \(0, \dots, N - 1\).
    • Bedroom \(r \in \{0, \dots, M - 1\}\) has twin vertices \(r_1 = N + 2r\) and \(r_2 = N + 2r + 1\), connected by internal edge \((r_1, r_2)\).
    • If guest \(g\) likes bedroom \(r\), add undirected edges \((g, r_1)\) and \((g, r_2)\).
  • Run Edmonds’ Blossom Algorithm in \(O(V^3)\) time to compute \(\nu(G_{\text{twin}})\), and output \(\nu(G_{\text{twin}}) - M\).

Step 4: Hand-Checkable Toy Trace & Failure-Mode Guard

  • Toy Trace: \(N = 3\) guests \(\{g_1, g_2, g_3\}\), \(M = 2\) rooms \(\{r, s\}\). \(g_1, g_2\) like \(r\); \(g_3\) likes \(s\).
    • In Room \(r\): \(g_1\) matches \(r_1\), \(g_2\) matches \(r_2\) (\(2\) edges).
    • In Room \(s\): even if \(g_3\) matches \(s_1\) (or \((s_1, s_2)\) stays matched), Room \(s\) contributes only \(1\) edge!
    • Total matching \(\nu(G_{\text{twin}}) = 2 + 1 = 3\), and \(\nu - M = 3 - 2 = \mathbf{1}\) guest pair.
  • Failure-Mode Guard: Notice that \((g, r_1, r_2)\) forms a 3-cycle (odd cycle / blossom)! Running a bipartite matching solver on \(G_{\text{twin}}\) is completely invalid—you must contract odd cycles via Edmonds’ Blossom algorithm (or compute \(\text{rank}(A_{\text{Tutte}}) / 2 - M\) modulo \(998244353\)).
// Graph Compilation for #064 (CF 1089 B — Guests) using Blossom Solver
int solve_guests(int N, int M, const vector<string>& likes) {
    int V = N + 2 * M;
    BlossomMatcher blossom(V); // Standard O(V^3) Edmonds Blossom template
    for (int r = 0; r < M; ++r) {
        int r1 = N + 2 * r, r2 = N + 2 * r + 1;
        blossom.add_edge(r1, r2); // Internal twin edge (baseline +1 per room)
        for (int g = 0; g < N; ++g) {
            if (likes[g][r] == '1') {
                blossom.add_edge(g, r1);
                blossom.add_edge(g, r2);
            }
        }
    }
    return blossom.max_matching() - M;
}

8. Chapter Revision Summary & Multilingual Bibliography

Key Takeaways

  • DAG Disjoint vs. Overlapping Path Covers: On a DAG \(G = (V, E)\), \(\text{MDPC}(G) = |V| - \nu(G_{\text{split}}(G))\). Allowing paths to share vertices is equivalent to taking the transitive closure \(\text{TC}(G)\) first.
  • Dilworth’s Theorem & The Transitivity Non-Overlap Lemma: On a strict poset \(\mathcal{P} = (V, \prec)\), no element \(v \in V\) has both \(v_{\text{out}} \in C^*\) and \(v_{\text{in}} \in C^*\). Thus \(A^* = \{v \in V : v_{\text{out}} \in S_{\min} \land v_{\text{in}} \notin S_{\min}\}\) constructively recovers a Maximum (Weight) Antichain.
  • Hall–Ore Duality & Flow Elimination: By Min-Cut duality, \(\nu_{a,b}(G) = \sum_{u \in L} a_u - \max_{S \subseteq L} \big(\sum_{u \in S} a_u - \sum_{v \in N(S)} b_v\big)\). When neighborhoods are contiguous intervals or \(|L| \le 18\), Segment Trees and SOS DP completely eliminate explicit flow solvers.
