09 — Transforms on Subsets and Divisors

This chapter is about changing coordinates. A convolution that appears quadratic often becomes pointwise multiplication after the correct transform. The central habit is to identify the relation among indices: subset inclusion, divisibility, or the Boolean group under XOR. The loops are short; the hard part is proving which operation the statement is actually combining.

Unless a section says otherwise, array values live in a commutative ring. The zeta/Möbius stages themselves use only addition and subtraction; a convolution also needs the pointwise products in the transformed coordinates to make sense.

Prerequisites, notation, and learning objectives

Read sets and induction, divisor sums, and modular units. You should learn to derive the transform from the index relation, prove the inverse, distinguish union from disjoint union, and choose exact stage orders for divisor transforms.

The universe is \(\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\), subsets are masks \(0,\ldots,2^n-1\), and \(|S|\) is the number of set bits. A poset is a set with a reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive relation; subset inclusion and positive divisibility are examples. A zeta transform sums over predecessors in that relation. Complexities count ring operations and storage in ring elements, not bit costs for arbitrarily large integers.

1. Subset zeta and Möbius transforms

For a function \(f[S]\) on subsets of an \(n\)-element universe, the subset zeta transform changes coordinates from “exactly set \(T\)” to “some subset of \(S\)”:

\[F[S]=\sum_{T\subseteq S}f[T].\]

For example, if \(f[T]\) counts objects whose feature set is exactly \(T\), then \(F[S]\) counts objects using only features in \(S\). Process one bit at a time, adding the state without the current bit into the state with it; invert by subtracting in the same schedule. After processing bits \(0,\ldots,i\), the current value has summed over all ways of deleting those processed bits. This induction invariant explains the transform—not merely its loop pattern.

More precisely, after a set \(B\) of bits is processed, the value at \(S\) is \(\sum_{T\subseteq S,\ S\setminus T\subseteq B}f[T]\). The next bit chooses whether it is deleted, partitioning the allowed terms into two disjoint cases. For two bits and input \(f[\varnothing],f[\{0\}],f[\{1\}],f[\{0,1\}] =(1,2,3,4)\), processing bit 0 gives \((1,3,3,7)\), then bit 1 gives \((1,3,4,10)\). Inversion by subtraction recovers the original four entries.

Both take \(O(n2^{n})\) time and \(O(1)\) extra memory. The inverse works because the Möbius function of the Boolean lattice is \(\mu(T,S)=(-1)^{|S\setminus T|}\). The bits may be processed in any order because their stages commute. Keep one bit as the outer stage, however, and invert each butterfly \((a,b)\mapsto(a,a+b)\) with \((a,b)\mapsto(a,b-a)\); changing which member of the pair receives the update changes the transform.

The superset variants switch the condition and, for masks s without bit b, update f[s] += f[s | (1<<b)]. Recognition signals include “sum over all submasks/supermasks”, compatibility masks, and inclusion–exclusion over missing features.

OR and AND convolution

For \(h[S]=\sum_{A\cup B=S}f[A]g[B]\), subset-zeta both arrays, multiply pointwise, then subset-Möbius. At a fixed \(S\), the product is

\[\left(\sum_{A\subseteq S}f[A]\right)\left(\sum_{B\subseteq S}g[B]\right),\]

which counts all pairs whose union is contained in \(S\). Möbius inversion turns “contained in” back into “equal to,” leaving exactly \(A\cup B=S\). For \(A\cap B=S\), use superset-zeta instead. Complexity is \(O(n2^n)\).

2. XOR convolution and FWT

XOR masks form the group \((\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^{n}\): every mask is its own inverse. Its characters are the plus/minus functions \(\chi_k(x)=(-1)^{\langle k,x\rangle}\). They turn a shift by XOR into a multiplicative sign, which is why

\[\widehat f[k]=\sum_x f[x]\chi_k(x),\qquad \widehat{f *_\oplus g}[k]=\widehat f[k]\widehat g[k].\]

The Walsh–Hadamard butterfly evaluates these characters recursively.

Here \(\langle k,x\rangle=\sum_{b=0}^{n-1}k_bx_b\pmod2\) is the parity of common set bits. A character turns the group operation into multiplication: \(\chi_k(a\mathbin\oplus b)=\chi_k(a)\chi_k(b)\), since bit parities add. Consequently, if \(h[s]=\sum_{a\oplus b=s}f[a]g[b]\), then

\[\widehat h[k]=\sum_{a,b}f[a]g[b]\chi_k(a\oplus b) =\left(\sum_a f[a]\chi_k(a)\right) \left(\sum_b g[b]\chi_k(b)\right).\]

For inversion, consider \(\sum_k\chi_k(x)\chi_k(y)\). If \(x=y\), all \(2^n\) terms are one. If \(x\ne y\), pick a differing bit; pair each \(k\) with the mask toggling that bit. Their signs are opposite and cancel. Applying the transform twice therefore gives \(2^nf\). Separating frequency masks by one bit gives the butterfly \((a,b)\mapsto(a+b,a-b)\), and \(n\) stages cost \(O(n2^n)\).

For an array of length \(N=2^n\), XOR inversion divides by \(N\). Thus the coefficient ring must make \(N\) invertible; over a field this is equivalent to characteristic not two. Under an odd prime modulus multiply by \(N^{-1}\). With integers, transform, multiply using a wide type, then divide exactly on inversion. OR/AND FWT uses triangular butterflies and has no normalization.

In characteristic two, the length-two transform sends both \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) to \((1,1)\), so it loses information. There is no inverse scaling that repairs that collision. Over integers, exact final division is valid, but intermediate transformed products can be much larger than final coefficients.

Worked example. With \(f[0]=1,f[1]=2\) and \(g[0]=3,g[1]=4\), the XOR convolution is \([1·3+2·4, 1·4+2·3]=[11,10]\). Their transforms are \([3,-1]\) and \([7,-1]\); pointwise product \([21,1]\), inverse butterfly gives \([11,10]\).

3. Subset convolution

The disjoint-union convolution is

\[h[S]=\sum_{T\subseteq S}f[T]g[S\setminus T].\]

Plain enumeration costs \(O(3^{n})\). Split each array by rank: \(f_k[S]=f[S]\) if \(|S|=k\), else zero. Zeta-transform every rank, form

\[H_k[S]=\sum_{i=0}^{k} F_i[S]G_{k-i}[S],\]

Möbius-transform each rank, and take \(h[S]=H_{|S|}[S]\). After inversion, the rank-\(k\) entry contains pairs with \(A\cup B=S\) and \(|A|+|B|=k\). Selecting \(k=|S|\) forces \(|A\cap B|=0\) because \(|A|+|B|=|A\cup B|+|A\cap B|\). This is why overlapping operands disappear. Time is \(O(n^{2}2^{n})\) and memory is \(O(n2^{n})\).

Use ranks \(0\le k\le n\) and inner indices \(0\le i\le k\) for the product; ranks above \(n\) cannot affect a requested rank \(|S|\le n\). However, before Möbius inversion do not clear entries merely because \(k>|S|\): they contain overlapping pairs which must cancel at larger masks. For example, two copies of singleton \(\{0\}\) contribute rank two at mask \(\{0\}\) and also at \(\{0,1\}\) after zeta. Inversion subtracts the former from the latter. Prematurely deleting the former leaves a false disjoint contribution.

For \(S=\{0,1\}\), the four summands are

\[ f[\varnothing]g[S]+f[\{0\}]g[\{1\}] +f[\{1\}]g[\{0\}]+f[S]g[\varnothing]. \]

The rank index is what distinguishes these disjoint splits from OR convolution, which would also admit overlapping pairs such as \((S,S)\).

Use it when the transition partitions a set into two labeled, disjoint parts and \(n\) is around 20. If one side can be enumerated sparsely, \(O(3^{n})\) may be smaller and easier.

At \(n=20\), one rank array of 32-bit entries occupies \(21\cdot2^{20}\cdot4=88{,}080{,}384\) bytes, or 84 MiB. Three such arrays need 252 MiB before other storage. Check the actual number of live arrays and memory limit; the asymptotic space bound alone does not promise feasibility.

4. Divisor-poset transforms

On positive integers ordered by divisibility, the same idea replaces “subset” by “divisor”:

\[F[x]=\sum_{d\mid x}f[d],\qquad G[x]=\sum_{x\mid m}f[m].\]

For arrays up to \(N\), divisor zeta uses \(f[ip]\mathrel{+}=f[i]\) for every prime \(p\) and \(ip\le N\). Traverse \(i\) upward in the forward stage and downward while subtracting in the inverse, so chains \(i,ip,ip^2,\ldots\) accumulate and undo correctly. Multiple zeta uses the reversed update \(f[i]\mathrel{+}=f[ip]\): traverse \(i\) downward in the forward stage and upward while subtracting in the inverse. A linear-sieve list of primes makes the structural analogy with subset bits explicit.

In each prime stage, the exact range is \(1\le i\le\lfloor N/p\rfloor\); prime stages cover every prime \(p\le N\). For the \(p=2\) chain \(1,2,4\), values \((a,b,c)\) become \((a,a+b,a+b+c)\) when the updates run upward. To undo them, subtract the entry at 2 from that at 4 first, then the entry at 1 from that at 2. Forward descending order would produce \((a,a+b,b+c)\), omitting \(a\) from index 4.

After processing a prime set \(P\), divisor zeta at \(x\) sums \(f[d]\) over exactly those divisors \(d\mid x\) for which every prime factor of \(x/d\) lies in \(P\). Each prime stage adds all choices of that prime’s exponent once, by its chain. Unique prime factorization makes the stages commute; inverse stages must undo the chain dependence within each prime.

For LCM convolution, “both inputs divide \(x\)” is the transformed condition, so divisor zeta, pointwise multiply, then invert. For GCD convolution, the natural condition is “both inputs are multiples of \(x\),” so use sums over multiples. The update directions above matter because an in-place stage must first accumulate a whole prime-power chain and later undo it in the reverse dependency order. Complexity is \(O(N\log\log N)\) with prime-stage transforms (or \(O(N\log N)\) with direct divisor loops).

Preconditions: indices start at 1; define deliberately whether zero exists. \(\gcd(0,x)\) and \(\operatorname{lcm}(0,x)\) make zero a special element, so most contest implementations handle it separately.

The LCM output here is restricted to \(1,\ldots,N\). Pairs whose LCM exceeds \(N\) contribute nowhere in that output. For any retained \(x\), both operands divide \(x\) and hence already lie in the retained range, so the divisor-zeta identity is unaffected by discarding those large outputs. For GCD convolution, all positive-input pairs have a GCD inside the range.

5. Pitfalls and decision guide

  • A | B, A & B, and A ^ B require three different transforms; visual similarity is not algebraic equivalence.
  • Möbius inversion must be performed in the same ring as the sums. Under a modulus, normalize subtraction.
  • \(O(n^{2} 2^{n})\) subset convolution becomes memory-bound first. Use contiguous rank-major storage.
  • SOS DP may need only one transformed array, not a convolution. Do not pay for an inverse you do not need.
  • For counting reachable XORs, a linear basis can beat FWT; FWT is appropriate when multiplicities/distributions matter for every XOR value.

6. Solved exercises

A. Union is not disjoint union

Let \(f[\{0\}]=g[\{0\}]=1\) and all other entries vanish on a two-element universe. Find the OR and disjoint-union convolutions.

TipHint

There is only one ordered pair with nonzero weight; inspect its intersection.

NoteSolution

OR convolution has value one at \(\{0\}\), since the union of the singleton with itself is that singleton. Subset convolution is zero everywhere because the pair overlaps. In the ranked method it has rank two and union size one, so it is rejected only when selecting the final rank \(|S|\) after inversion.

B. Count AND-zero subsequences

For input masks \((1,2,3)\) on two bits, count nonempty subsequences with AND zero. Derive the formula from transformed counts, not by guessing alternating signs.

TipHint

Let \(c[S]\) count inputs containing all bits of \(S\).

NoteSolution

In mask order 0,1,2,3, \(c=(3,2,2,1)\). A subsequence whose AND contains \(S\) chooses any nonempty subset of those occurrences, so \(F[S]=2^{c[S]}-1\) is the superset zeta of the exact-AND distribution. Möbius inversion at zero gives \(7-3-3+1=2\). They are the occurrences \((1,2)\) and \((1,2,3)\). In general, compute \(c\) in \(O(n2^n)\), then sum \(\sum_S(-1)^{|S|}(2^{c[S]}-1)\). Repeated values remain distinct occurrences.

C. Recover exact gcd counts

Both operands are independently chosen from \(\{1,2,3,4\}\). Find the numbers of ordered pairs with gcd 1, 2, 3, and 4.

TipHint

Pairs with both operands divisible by \(d\) number \(\lfloor4/d\rfloor^2\).

NoteSolution

Let \(h[d]\) count exact gcd \(d\). Then \(\lfloor4/d\rfloor^2=\sum_{d\mid m}h[m]\). Descending subtraction gives \(h[4]=1\), \(h[3]=1\), \(h[2]=4-h[4]=3\), and \(h[1]=16-h[2]-h[3]-h[4]=11\). The values sum to all 16 pairs. This is multiple-zeta inversion; pointwise squaring counts independent ordered choices and would need correction for unordered pairs or distinct indices.

7. Practice, in order

Labels: Foundation isolates one idea; Advanced combines it; Mastery requires recognition; Capstone is suitable for team practice. All statements below are available in English.

  1. Foundation — Compatible Numbers, CF 165E (English). Superset/subset SOS DP. Official tutorial.
    • Prerequisite: bitmasks.
    • Why: the canonical “find a disjoint present mask” transform.
      Hints
    1. A number compatible with \(x\) is a submask of the complement of \(x\).
    2. Store any present witness, then propagate witnesses from submasks.
    3. Propagate any present witness from submasks. This uses the same dependency order as zeta but is not an invertible additive transform; no inverse is needed.
  2. Advanced — Jzzhu and Numbers, CF 449D (English). Superset zeta plus inclusion–exclusion. Official tutorial.
    • Prerequisite: counting subsequences, Möbius inversion.
    • Why: converts AND-zero into counts of elements containing each mask.
      Hints
    1. Count subsequences whose AND contains \(S\).
    2. If \(c[S]\) input values contain \(S\), there are \(2^{c[S]}-1\) nonempty choices.
    3. Möbius-invert, or take the alternating sum by popcount.
  3. Advanced — Nim Counting, AtCoder ABC212 H (English). XOR FWT. Official editorial.
    • Prerequisite: modular arithmetic, generating counts by length.
    • Why: forces character-space reasoning rather than treating FWT as a black box.
      Hints
    1. Winning/losing is determined by the XOR of chosen pile sizes.
    2. Transform the one-choice frequency distribution.
    3. Sum transformed powers for lengths 1 through \(N\), invert, and subtract the XOR-zero count from all \(K+\cdots+K^N\) sequences. At transformed value one, the geometric sum is \(N\); do not divide by a vanishing difference.
  4. Mastery — Sum the Fibonacci, CF 914G (English). Ranked subset, XOR, and AND convolutions together. Official tutorial.
    • Prerequisite: ranked subset convolution and XOR/AND transforms.
    • Why: the explicit disjointness condition changes OR into subset convolution.
      Hints
    1. Build frequency array \(f\). The \(a,b\) pair must satisfy \(s_a\mathbin\&s_b=0\); obtain its union distribution by ranked subset convolution \(f*f\).
    2. Obtain the \(d,e\) XOR distribution separately. Multiply these distributions and the single-element distribution pointwise by the corresponding Fibonacci values.
    3. AND-convolve the three weighted distributions and sum outputs at powers of two. The statement counts ordered five-tuples of indices, allowing repeated indices: no symmetry division belongs in these convolutions.

8. Reference sheet

Index relation Transform or method Time Extra algebra
\(A\cup B=S\) Subset zeta / product / inverse \(O(n2^n)\) None beyond ring operations
\(A\cap B=S\) Superset zeta / product / inverse \(O(n2^n)\) None beyond ring operations
\(A\oplus B=S\) Walsh–Hadamard \(O(n2^n)\) \(2^n\) unit, or exact integer division
\(A\sqcup B=S\) Ranked subset convolution \(O(n^22^n)\) Keep overlap ranks until inversion
\(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=s\) Divisor zeta \(O(N\log\log N)\) Positive indices; output truncated at \(N\)
\(\gcd(a,b)=s\) Multiple zeta \(O(N\log\log N)\) Positive indices

The common proof strategy is to transform “exactly this result” into a sum over “results below/above this index,” factor the independent choices, and invert. XOR instead uses multiplicative signs of a group. Check which proof applies before calling a library routine.
