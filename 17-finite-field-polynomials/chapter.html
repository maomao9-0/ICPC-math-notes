17 — Finite-Field Polynomial Algebra

When a bitstring is secretly a polynomial

A contest problem: you may repeatedly XOR a binary string with a shifted copy of itself, and you want the smallest number of steps to reach a target. Try to attack that as a string problem and you drown in cases.

Read the string as a polynomial over \(\mathbb F_2\) — bit \(i\) is the coefficient of \(x^i\) — and the shift-XOR is multiplication by \(1 + x^{\text{shift}}\). The question becomes: what is the smallest \(L\) with \(g \mid 1 + x^L\)? And that is answered by the factor structure of \(g\): the multiplicative order of \(x\) in each irreducible factor, combined across factors. (This is exactly problem 5 below.)

That is the shift this chapter asks you to make. So far polynomials have been coefficient arrays you convolve fast. Here the useful representation is the factorization, because it turns questions about repetition, periodicity, and roots into questions about irreducible factors and their multiplicities. Three recurring signals:

  • repeating a linear process becomes taking powers in a polynomial quotient;
  • finding all roots becomes extracting linear factors;
  • a period or order question becomes the order of \(x\) modulo each irreducible.

Prerequisites: fields and units, polynomial arithmetic, and linear maps for Berlekamp’s method. Recall that in a field every nonzero element can be divided by — the property the whole chapter leans on.

After this chapter you should be able to explain how irreducibles create extension fields, separate multiplicity from degree, derive the Frobenius factor-degree tests, and tell certification apart from mere reconstruction.

The plan is a pipeline, and it is worth keeping the shape in mind: strip repeated factors, then group the remaining factors by degree, then split equal-degree groups, then certify. Each stage exists because the next one assumes something the previous stage guarantees.

Euclidean algebra and quotient fields

Everything below is built from one fact: polynomials over a field support long division with remainder, so they behave like integers — gcds, Bézout, and modular arithmetic all carry over.

Fix a prime \(p\). The field \(\mathbb F_p\) consists of residues modulo \(p\). A polynomial is monic when its leading coefficient is one. A positive-degree polynomial is irreducible if it has no factorization into two positive-degree polynomials over the coefficient field. Constants are units, not irreducible factors.

For nonzero \(B\), long division gives unique \(Q,R\) with

\[A=BQ+R,\qquad R=0\text{ or }\deg R<\deg B.\]

The leading term can be cancelled because the leading coefficient of \(B\) is invertible. Repeated division produces a monic gcd and a Bezout identity \(UA+VB=\gcd(A,B)\), just as for integers.

For a monic modulus \(M\) of degree \(d\ge1\), the quotient \(\mathbb F_p[x]/(M)\) identifies polynomials whose difference is divisible by \(M\). Every residue has one representative of degree below \(d\). There are \(p^d\) representatives: each of its \(d\) coefficients has \(p\) choices.

NoteWhen is the quotient a field?

The residue of \(A\) is invertible exactly when \(\gcd(A,M)=1\). Bezout supplies an inverse; conversely, an inverse supplies a Bezout identity modulo \(M\). The quotient is therefore a field exactly when \(M\) is irreducible. If \(M=BC\) with both degrees positive, the nonzero residues \(B,C\) multiply to zero, which is impossible in a field.

Example: four elements from a quadratic. Over \(\mathbb F_2\), \(M=x^2+x+1\) has neither 0 nor 1 as a root. A reducible quadratic has a linear factor, so \(M\) is irreducible. Set \(\alpha=x\bmod M\). Then \(\alpha^2=\alpha+1\) and

\[\alpha(\alpha+1)=\alpha^2+\alpha=1.\]

The field consists of \(0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1\), and \(\alpha\) has multiplicative order three.

Failed shortcuts. Having no field roots certifies irreducibility only in degrees two and three: \((x^2+x+1)^2\) is a reducible quartic without an \(\mathbb F_2\) root. Composite coefficient moduli introduce another failure: modulo 4, the leading coefficient 2 cannot be inverted, since \(2\cdot2=0\).

Stage 1: remove multiplicities

Why first? Because the next stage represents the quotient ring as a product of fields, and a repeated factor makes that false. Squeeze out repetition before anything else.

Write a monic input as \(f=\prod_i h_i^{e_i}\) with distinct monic irreducibles \(h_i\). It is square-free when every multiplicity \(e_i\) is one. Repeated factors prevent its quotient from being a product of fields.

The formal derivative is defined algebraically by \((x^k)'=kx^{k-1}\). For \(f=h^eu\) with \(h\nmid u\),

\[f'=e h^{e-1}h'u+h^eu'.\]

An irreducible over \(\mathbb F_p\) has nonzero derivative: otherwise it would be a \(p\)th power. If \(p\nmid e\), the exponent of \(h\) in \(\gcd(f,f')\) is exactly \(e-1\); if \(p\mid e\), that exponent is \(e\). Thus the gcd detects repetition but does not always lower each multiplicity by one.

A square-free decomposition can use the following invariant-driven procedure:

  1. Set \(c=\gcd(f,f')\), \(w=f/c\), and \(i=1\).
  2. While \(w\ne1\), set \(y=\gcd(w,c)\) and \(z=w/y\). Emit nonconstant \(z\) with multiplicity \(i\). Replace \(w\) by \(y\), replace \(c\) by \(c/y\), and increase \(i\).
  3. Any remaining \(c\ne1\) is a \(p\)th power. Take its formal \(p\)th root, recurse, and multiply the returned multiplicities by \(p\).

For a factor with \(p\nmid e\), \(w\) initially contains one copy; successive iterations remove copies from \(c\) until the factor is emitted at \(i=e\). Factors whose multiplicities are divisible by \(p\) remain entirely in \(c\). This explains both the loop and the residual recursion.

Taking a \(p\)th root over \(\mathbb F_p\) divides exponents by \(p\) and leaves coefficients unchanged, since

\[\left(\sum_j a_jx^j\right)^p=\sum_j a_j^px^{jp}=\sum_j a_jx^{jp}.\]

The intermediate binomial coefficients vanish modulo \(p\). Over an extension field the coefficients also require inverse Frobenius; the stated procedure uses the prime field.

Mixed example. In \(\mathbb F_2[x]\), take \(f=x^2(x+1)^3\). Then \(f'=x^2(x+1)^2\), so initially \(c=x^2(x+1)^2\) and \(w=x+1\). The loop emits \(x+1\) at multiplicity three and leaves \(c=x^2\). Its square root returns \(x\) at multiplicity two. Handling only the top-level case \(f'=0\) would miss this residual square.

Stage 2: Frobenius and degree decomposition

The key identity of this stage separates factors by degree using nothing but modular exponentiation and gcd — no candidate irreducibles are ever enumerated.

In characteristic \(p\), the map \(a\mapsto a^p\) preserves sums and products. It is called Frobenius. In a field of \(p^e\) elements every nonzero element satisfies \(a^{p^e-1}=1\), by the finite-group order theorem. Including zero gives \(a^{p^e}=a\).

Let \(h\) be irreducible of degree \(e\) and \(\alpha=x\bmod h\). Its Frobenius orbit has length exactly \(e\). To prove this, let the orbit length be \(t\). The polynomial

\[\prod_{j=0}^{t-1}(X-\alpha^{p^j})\]

has coefficients fixed by Frobenius. Those fixed elements are exactly \(\mathbb F_p\), since \(X^p-X\) has at most \(p\) roots and already has all \(p\) base-field elements as roots. Thus the orbit polynomial belongs to \(\mathbb F_p[X]\) and is divisible by the minimal polynomial \(h\), giving \(e\le t\). Meanwhile \(\alpha^{p^e}=\alpha\) gives \(t\le e\). Consequently \(t=e\).

Therefore \(h\) divides \(x^{p^d}-x\) exactly when \(e\mid d\). The derivative of \(x^{p^d}-x\) is \(-1\), so its factors do not repeat. We have proved

\[x^{p^d}-x=\prod_{\substack{h\text{ monic irreducible}\\\deg h\mid d}}h.\]

This turns factor degrees into gcd queries without enumerating candidate irreducibles. For square-free \(f\), maintain \(r=f\), \(H=x\bmod r\), and \(d=1\). While \(r\ne1\) and \(2d\le\deg r\):

  1. Replace \(H\) by \(H^p\bmod r\), giving \(H\equiv x^{p^d}\pmod r\).
  2. Compute \(b=\gcd(r,H-x)\). Emit nonconstant \(b\) as the product of degree-\(d\) factors.
  3. Divide \(r\) by \(b\), reduce \(H\) modulo the new \(r\) if necessary, and increase \(d\).

Before iteration \(d\), all factors of \(r\) have degree at least \(d\). The gcd therefore selects degree exactly \(d\): smaller divisor degrees have already been removed. At termination any nonconstant residual is irreducible, because two remaining factors would require total degree at least \(2d\). Emit it with its own degree.

Example. For

\[f=(x+1)(x^2+x+1)(x^3+x+1)\quad\text{over }\mathbb F_2,\]

the degree-one gcd returns \(x+1\). The degree-two gcd on the residual returns \(x^2+x+1\). The remaining cubic is irreducible; checking 0 and 1 also verifies this independently.

Stage 3: equal-degree splitting and Berlekamp

After stage 2 you hold blocks whose factors all share one degree, so no deterministic degree test can separate them further. The standard escape is randomness: pick a random element and let a gcd read off which hidden factors it “labels” the same way.

Suppose the square-free block \(b=h_1\cdots h_k\) has \(k\ge2\) factors of degree \(d\). Polynomial CRT gives

\[\mathbb F_p[x]/(b)\cong\prod_{j=1}^k\mathbb F_{p^d}.\]

A uniformly random polynomial \(a\) of degree below \(\deg b\) gives independent uniform field coordinates. First compute \(\gcd(a,b)\): a proper nontrivial gcd already splits the block. Retry if \(a=0\bmod b\). When the gcd is one, every coordinate is nonzero.

For odd \(p\), put \(q=p^d\). In a field with \(q\) elements, exactly half of the nonzero elements are squares, and \(a_j^{(q-1)/2}\) is respectively \(+1\) or \(-1\). Thus

\[g=\gcd\bigl(b,a^{(q-1)/2}-1\bigr)\]

collects factors receiving sign \(+1\). Conditional on nonzero coordinates, a useful split has probability \(1-2^{1-k}\ge1/2\). Retry when \(g=1\) or \(g=b\), otherwise recurse on the proper factors. This is Cantor–Zassenhaus: randomness labels hidden factors and gcd extracts a label class. The answer is exact; randomness affects running time.

For \(p=2\), use a fresh uniformly random residue \(a\) without conditioning it to be a unit, and use the trace

\[T(a)=a+a^2+a^{2^2}+\cdots+a^{2^{d-1}}\pmod b.\]

In each field coordinate, \(T(a)^2=T(a)\), so the result is a bit. The trace is a nonzero linear map: its defining polynomial has degree \(2^{d-1}<2^d\) and cannot vanish on the whole field. Its two values are therefore equally frequent. The gcd \(\gcd(b,T(a))\) selects trace-zero factors with the same split probability. Evaluate by repeated squaring and addition.

Berlekamp’s alternative. On representatives of degree below \(n=\deg b\), Frobenius is an \(\mathbb F_p\)-linear map \(Q\). In the CRT coordinates, the kernel of \(Q-I\) consists of arbitrary base-field constants on each factor. Its dimension is the number of factors. Find a kernel basis with your linear-algebra library. For basis vectors \(v\) and \(c\in\mathbb F_p\), gcds with \(v-c\) separate the factors: if two coordinates agreed on every basis vector they would agree on the whole kernel, contrary to the availability of independent coordinate labels. Enumerating \(p\) constants is useful for small fields; it is not independent of \(p\).

Stage 4: roots and certification

The distinct linear factors of nonzero \(f\) form \(\gcd(f,x^p-x)\). Split this square-free product and read roots from \(x-a\). Recover their multiplicities from the earlier decomposition.

Reconstruction alone does not certify a complete factorization: returning the input itself passes that check. Also certify that every returned factor is irreducible. A monic degree-\(d\) polynomial \(h\) is irreducible exactly when

\[x^{p^d}\equiv x\pmod h,\qquad \gcd\bigl(h,x^{p^{d/\ell}}-x\bigr)=1 \quad\text{for every prime }\ell\mid d.\]

The first condition makes \(h\) square-free with factor degrees dividing \(d\). Any proper divisor degree \(e\) divides \(d/\ell\) for some prime \(\ell\mid d/e\), contradicting a gcd condition. For degree one the gcd list is empty. Check these tests, multiply the factors with multiplicities, and restore the input’s leading scalar.

Contest contract and complexity

The input is a nonzero polynomial over a specified prime field. Normalize its leading coefficient. Nonzero constants have an empty factor list; zero has no finite factorization of this form.

Let \(M(n)\) bound multiplication and reduction modulo a degree-\(n\) polynomial, and \(G(n)\) bound gcd. Binary modular exponentiation to exponent \(E\) costs \(O(M(n)\log E)\). The distinct-degree procedure uses at most \(n\) Frobenius steps and gcds, hence costs

\[O\bigl(n(M(n)\log p+G(n))\bigr).\]

With quadratic multiplication and Euclid this is \(O(n^3\log p)\) field operations. This is a conservative baseline, not the fastest known factorization bound. At block degree \(m\), an odd-field splitting trial costs \(O(M(m)d\log p+G(m))\); a binary trace costs \(O(dM(m)+G(m))\). Proper splits terminate the recursion. Including zero-coordinate splitting, each trial has a constant success probability, so the expected retries are bounded. Berlekamp additionally requires a dense kernel computation, cubic in block degree, and the subsequent splitting gcds.

Keep polynomials trimmed and gcds monic. Compare small-degree results with trial division by every monic candidate of degree at most half the input degree. Distinguish a splitter retry from an input that violates the field contract.

Problem-solving checklist

  • Is the coefficient domain a field? Is the input zero or constant?
  • Do you need roots, factor degrees, irreducibles, or multiplicities?
  • Have you separated repetition before using polynomial CRT?
  • Does the Frobenius residue use the current modulus?
  • Does verification check irreducibility as well as reconstruction?

Exercises with hints and solutions

  1. Inverse. Invert \(x+1\) modulo \(x^2+x+1\) over \(\mathbb F_2\).

    Hint: multiply by \(x\). Solution: \((x+1)x=x^2+x\equiv1\), so the inverse is \(x\).

  2. Multiplicity. Recover the factor multiplicity in \((x+1)^6\) over \(\mathbb F_3\).

    Hint: its derivative is zero. Solution: take the formal cube root \((x+1)^2\). Its square-free decomposition returns multiplicity two; scaling by three recovers six.

  3. Certificate. Which Frobenius gcd tests suffice for a degree-six candidate?

    Hint: list proper divisors of six. Solution: after the \(p^6\) identity, possible factor degrees are \(1,2,3,6\). Gcd tests at \(p^3\) and \(p^2\) exclude 1,2,3. Only degree six remains.

  4. Order with repetition. Find the least positive \(L\) such that \((x+1)^3\mid x^L+1\) over \(\mathbb F_2\).

    Hint: write \(L=2^tu\), \(u\) odd. Solution: \(x^L+1=(x^u+1)^{2^t}\). The derivative of \(x^u+1\) is nonzero at 1, so its root there is simple. The multiplicity is exactly \(2^t\), giving least \(L=4\). Ignoring repetition incorrectly gives one.

  5. Contest application — Long Binary String, CF1698G. The official input length is at most 35; no enlarged constraint is assumed.

    Editorial guidance: remove the initial power of \(x\) from the input polynomial to obtain \(g\) with constant term one. A two-bit target requires \(g\mid1+x^L\). For each distinct irreducible \(h\), find the order of \(x\) modulo \(h\), which divides \(2^{\deg h}-1\). Take the lcm of these odd orders, then multiply by the smallest power of two at least the largest factor multiplicity. The initial zero prefix fixes the earliest possible first bit; the minimum gap maximizes the string lexicographically. Treat the zero input separately and prove that the multiplier fits the finite allowed string. Chapter 20 develops a small complete instance.
