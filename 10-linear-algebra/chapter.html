10 — Linear Algebra over Finite Fields

Linear algebra appears whenever constraints are linear, path choices form an XOR space, spanning trees must be counted, or a long recurrence must be compressed. The common idea is to replace many objects by coordinates in a vector space and then use reversible row operations. Always identify the scalar domain first: real numbers, \(\mathbb F_p\), and \(\mathbb F_2\) have different numerical and structural hazards.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Read proofs and invariants and fields and units. The recurrence sections also use polynomial division. After this chapter you should be able to count solutions from rank, prove an XOR representation count, translate a tree count into a determinant, and explain why a finite-dimensional transition produces a linear recurrence. Your library supplies elimination and polynomial arithmetic; the task here is to establish what those operations mean and when they are valid.

1. Vectors, coordinates, and reversible equations

Fix a field \(K\). A vector in \(K^c\) is a list of \(c\) field elements; addition and scalar multiplication act coordinate by coordinate. A linear combination of \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\) is \(\sum_i a_i v_i\) with \(a_i\in K\). Their span is the set of all such combinations. A list is independent if the only combination equal to zero has every coefficient zero. A basis is an independent list that spans the space in question. Its length is the dimension.

For example, \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\) form a basis of \(K^2\): the vector \((x,y)\) is uniquely \((x-y)(1,0)+y(1,1)\). The word “uniquely” matters. If two coefficient lists represented the same vector, subtracting them would produce a nonzero dependence. A basis is therefore a lossless coordinate system, much like compressing a state to exactly its independent parameters.

A matrix \(A\in K^{r\times c}\) describes the linear map \(x\mapsto Ax\). Its kernel is \(\{x:Ax=0\}\); its image is \(\{Ax:x\in K^c\}\). The image dimension is the rank. The equation \(Ax=b\) asks whether \(b\) belongs to the image and, if it does, for all its preimages.

Elimination and the rank–nullity count

Over a field, row operations preserve the solution set because they merely replace one equation by an equivalent linear combination of equations. Maintain a pivot row; for each column choose a nonzero entry, swap it to that row, normalize, and eliminate. These operations are reversible: undo the swap, multiply back by the nonzero scalar, or subtract the row previously added. After processing a column, each pivot column has one leading entry and all later rows are zero in previous pivot columns. This is the invariant that ensures no equation is lost and each pivot settles one new variable. A pivot in the coefficient matrix records one independent equation, so \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\) is the number of such pivots. For a system with \(r\) equations and \(c\) unknowns, a zero row with a nonzero augmented entry means inconsistency; otherwise the augmented matrix has the same rank as \(A\) and there are \(c-\operatorname{rank}(A)\) free variables. Over \(\mathbb F_q\), that means exactly \(q^{\,c-\operatorname{rank}(A)}\) solutions; over an infinite field it describes a solution affine space of that dimension. To justify the count, choose the \(c-\operatorname{rank}(A)\) nonpivot variables freely and solve pivot variables backwards; each choice has exactly one completion. Thus \(\dim\ker A=c-\operatorname{rank}(A)\), the rank–nullity identity. If \(x_0\) solves \(Ax=b\), every solution is \(x_0+z\) with \(z\in\ker A\), since \(A(x-x_0)=0\). Such a translated subspace is called an affine space.

NoteExample: constraints versus variables

Over \(\mathbb F_5\), the equations \(x+y+z=2\) and \(2x+2y+2z=4\) have rank one. Choose \(y,z\) arbitrarily and set \(x=2-y-z\): there are \(25\) solutions. Replacing the second right side by \(3\) makes the system inconsistent, not rank two: subtraction leaves \(0=4\) in \(\mathbb F_5\).

For example, over \(\mathbb F_5\), \(x+y=1\), \(2x+3y=0\) becomes \(y=3,x=3\) after subtracting twice the first row. The integer solution is irrelevant: the field is part of the question. Dense elimination costs \(O(rc\min(r,c))\). For determinant, do not normalize: each row swap negates the answer, multiply by the pivot, then eliminate below using the current entry divided by the pivot. Composite moduli are not fields: a nonzero pivot may be noninvertible. Use prime-factor methods, fraction-free elimination, or exact integer algorithms instead. For instance, \(2x=2\) modulo \(6\) has solutions \(x=1,4\); dividing by the nonzero residue \(2\) would discard one. Rank-based field counts do not apply to this ring.

What the determinant measures

For an \(n\times n\) matrix, define

\[\det A=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^n A_{i,\sigma(i)}.\]

Here \(S_n\) consists of permutations of \(1,\ldots,n\), and the sign is \((-1)\) to the number of inversions. A summand chooses one entry from each row and column. Swapping rows reverses the sign; adding a multiple of another row does not change the determinant because terms with repeated rows cancel. Triangular matrices have determinant equal to their diagonal product. These facts explain the elimination contract above. Over a field, \(\det A\ne0\) exactly when every column has a pivot, equivalently when \(Ax=b\) has a unique solution for every \(b\). The permutation definition also makes sense over any commutative ring, although division-based elimination may not. The determinant of the empty matrix is \(1\).

2. XOR basis as elimination over \(\mathbb F_2\)

A set of machine words is a vector set whose coordinates are bits, with XOR as vector addition. Keep at most one pivot per highest bit. Inserting a word means XORing away known highest bits; if nothing remains it was already a linear combination of earlier words, otherwise it adds one independent direction.

Insertion costs \(O(B)\). If rank is \(r\), the span has \(2^{r}\) distinct values. For representation counts from \(n\) input vectors, every reachable value has \(2^{n-r}\) representations. To obtain the zero-indexed \(k\)-th smallest distinct XOR, reduce to a basis in which every pivot bit occurs in only its own vector, order those vectors by increasing pivot bit, and XOR the vectors selected by the bits of \(k\). A merely triangular or arbitrarily ordered basis does not give numeric order.

Why the representation count is uniform: map a choice vector in \(\mathbb F_2^n\) to the XOR it selects. This map has rank \(r\), so its kernel has \(2^{n-r}\) elements; every nonempty fiber is a translate of that kernel. For words \(1,2,3\), the rank is \(2\) and each of \(0,1,2,3\) has two representations. Zero comes from the empty subset and from all three words.

For sorted enumeration require \(0\le k<2^r\). In a reduced basis, compare two coefficient masks at their highest different bit. That selects the highest pivot at which their numeric XORs differ; lower basis vectors cannot change it. Thus the order of coefficient masks equals numeric order. As a failure example, triangular vectors \(1,3\) in increasing pivot order enumerate \(0,1,3,2\), not sorted order; reducing \(3\) by \(1\) repairs them to \(1,2\).

Graph recognition: assign each vertex an XOR distance from a DFS root. Every non-tree edge yields a cycle XOR dist[u] ^ w ^ dist[v]. If \(s,t\) lie in the same undirected connected component, all XORs of walks from \(s\) to \(t\) are dist[s]^dist[t] plus the span of that component’s cycle XORs.

To prove this, XOR the edges of a walk with the fixed tree path; every vertex then has even incidence, so the remaining edges decompose into cycles. Each cycle is a combination of fundamental cycles from non-tree edges. Conversely, walk to any such cycle along the tree, traverse it, and return along the same tree edges; the detour contributes exactly the cycle XOR because its connector appears twice. This proves equality for walks in an undirected component. It does not prove equality for simple paths: a triangle attached at a single articulation to the \(s\)–\(t\) path can alter a walk’s XOR, but a simple path cannot visit the triangle and return through that articulation.

With \(m\) edges, \(n\) vertices, and \(B\) bits, build the component bases in \(O((n+m)B)\) time and \(O(n+B\cdot\text{components})\) auxiliary space. Do not reuse one component’s cycles for endpoints in another component.

3. Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theorem

For a weighted undirected graph, form the Laplacian

\[L_{ii}=\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij},\qquad L_{ij}=-w_{ij}\quad(i\ne j).\]

The diagonal is the total edge weight leaving a vertex; an off-diagonal entry is the negative edge weight connecting its two vertices (with parallel-edge weights added first). Delete any one row and the corresponding column. The determinant of the resulting \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) cofactor equals the total weight of spanning trees, where a tree’s weight is the product of its edge weights.

Why it works: orient edges arbitrarily and let \(B\) be the incidence matrix. Each column of \(B\) has \(+1\) at one endpoint and \(-1\) at the other; \(W\) is diagonal with the edge weights. Then \(L=BWB^{T}\) because one edge contributes its weight to both diagonal positions and its negative to the two off-diagonal positions. Delete the root row from \(B\) to obtain \(\bar B\). The determinant-of-product expansion, called Cauchy–Binet, gives

\[\det(\bar B W\bar B^T) =\sum_{\substack{S\subseteq E\\|S|=n-1}} \det(\bar B_{[:,S]})^2\prod_{e\in S}w_e.\]

This identity follows by expanding determinants: terms that choose a repeated intermediate column cancel in opposite-sign pairs, leaving subsets of distinct columns and their two determinant factors. A cyclic edge set has dependent incidence columns. For a disconnected set, summing the rows of a component not containing the deleted root gives zero, so its determinant also vanishes. For a tree, remove a nonroot leaf and expand along its single incident entry; induction down to the empty matrix gives determinant \(\pm1\). Exactly the spanning trees therefore survive, each with its edge-product weight. Directed arborescences require a directional convention. With \(L_{ii}=\sum_{i\to j,\ j\ne i}w_{ij}\) and, for \(i\ne j\), \(L_{ij}=-\sum_{\text{edges }i\to j}w_{ij}\), the root cofactor counts trees whose edges point toward the root. To count trees pointing away from the root, use the indegree convention \(L_{ii}=\sum_{j\to i,\ j\ne i}w_{ji}\), \(L_{ij}=-\sum_{\text{edges }j\to i}w_{ji}\). On the one-edge graph \(r\to v\), deleting the row and column of \(r\) gives determinant \(1\) only under the indegree convention, as required for the unique tree pointing away from \(r\). This two-vertex test catches a transposed convention.

Complexity is \(O(n^{3})\) with dense elimination. Parallel edge weights add. Self-loops do not contribute. Under a modulus, the determinant counts modulo that modulus.

For a triangle with unit edge weights, deleting one row and column leaves \(\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\), whose determinant is \(3\). These are exactly the three spanning trees obtained by deleting one edge.

For triangle edge weights \(a,b,c\), the same calculation gives \(ab+ac+bc\): with \((a,b,c)=(2,3,5)\) the weighted count is \(31\). A zero determinant modulo a prime can mean cancellation of a positive integer count modulo that prime; it is not a certificate that the original graph is disconnected.

4. Linear recurrences and characteristic polynomials

Let \(k\ge1\) and let the recurrence \(s_n=c_1s_{n-1}+\cdots+c_ks_{n-k}\) hold for every \(n\ge k\), with initial values \(s_0,\ldots,s_{k-1}\) in a commutative ring. Then the polynomial \(x^{k}-c_1x^{k-1}-\cdots-c_k\) annihilates the sequence: replace every \(x^k\) by the prescribed combination of lower powers. Reduce \(x^{N}\) modulo this polynomial; the remainder coefficients tell exactly how to combine \(s_0,\ldots,s_{k-1}\) to obtain \(s_N\). Binary exponentiation with polynomial reduction costs \(O(k^{2}\log N)\) (Kitamasa); NTT-based methods or Bostan–Mori improve large \(k\).

Here is why reduction computes sequence values, rather than merely a useful formal remainder. Define \(\Lambda(x^j)=s_j\) and extend by linearity. For every \(j\ge0\), the recurrence says \(\Lambda(x^j(x^k-c_1x^{k-1}-\cdots-c_k))=0\). If \(x^N=Q(x)C(x)+R(x)\) with \(\deg R<k\), applying \(\Lambda\) leaves \(s_N=\Lambda(R)\). Division by the monic \(C\) uses no inverse, so this argument works even modulo a composite number. Binary exponentiation maintains that its accumulated polynomial is congruent to the processed power of \(x\) modulo \(C\); multiply and reduce after each step. Memory is \(O(k)\) with naive products.

For Fibonacci, the characteristic polynomial is \(x^2-x-1\). Since \(x^5\equiv5x+3\pmod{x^2-x-1}\), every Fibonacci-type sequence obeys \(s_5=3s_0+5s_1\). This is what “the remainder coefficients tell how to combine initial terms” means concretely.

Discovering a recurrence: the discrepancy idea

For a field-valued sequence, Berlekamp–Massey maintains a shortest recurrence consistent with the prefix already read. Its discrepancy is the observed next term minus the term predicted by the current recurrence. Zero discrepancy extends the verified prefix. A nonzero discrepancy supplies a new independent constraint; subtract a suitably shifted saved recurrence whose discrepancy is nonzero, scaled by the ratio of discrepancies, to cancel it. The shift ensures earlier constraints remain satisfied. The saved state comes from the last increase of the necessary order. This explains both the field-division requirement and why the algorithm is more than guessing a recurrence by fitting a few coefficients.

Its library contract is \(O(T^2)\) field operations and \(O(T)\) space for a prefix of \(T\) terms (often bounded as \(O(TL)\) for linear complexity \(L\)). If an infinite sequence is known to have shortest recurrence order \(L\), valid from index \(L\), then \(2L\) initial terms suffice to identify it. One justification for uniqueness is that a sequence generated by an order-at-most-\(L\) recurrence has a rational generating function with denominator degree at most \(L\) and numerator degree below \(L\). Two such functions agreeing through degree \(2L-1\) have a cross-multiplied numerator of degree below \(2L\) with \(2L\) initial zero coefficients, hence are equal. The all-zero sequence has order zero. No finite prefix proves an arbitrary unknown future obeys the inferred rule: \(0,0,0,0,1,\ldots\) defeats an inference from the first four terms.

Matrices and Cayley–Hamilton

The characteristic polynomial is \(\chi_A(x)=\det(xI-A)\); the minimal polynomial is the monic polynomial of least degree with \(m_A(A)=0\). Cayley–Hamilton states \(\chi_A(A)=0\). To see the mechanism, use the cofactor identity \((xI-A)\operatorname{adj}(xI-A)=\chi_A(x)I\) and compare powers of \(x\). The coefficient equations telescope after multiplying by successive powers of \(A\), leaving \(\chi_A(A)=0\); this avoids an unjustified substitution into matrices whose coefficients might not commute.

Consequently, multiplying by \(u^TA^j\) and \(v\) shows \(u^TA^nv\) obeys a recurrence of order at most the matrix dimension. Some observations see fewer directions: for \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\) and \(u=v=(1,0)^T\), the observed sequence is \(2^n\), of order one although \(\chi_A=(x-2)(x-3)\).

5. Characteristic polynomial and black-box methods

For dense matrices over a field, a similarity transformation \(A\mapsto S^{-1}AS\) preserves \(\det(xI-A)\). Hessenberg reduction uses paired row/column operations to zero entries below the first subdiagonal in \(O(n^3)\) operations. Unlike ordinary row elimination, the inverse column operation is necessary to preserve the characteristic polynomial. For an upper Hessenberg matrix \(H\), let \(D_i=\det(xI_i-H_{1:i,1:i})\), with \(D_0=1\). Expanding its final column gives

\[D_i=(x-h_{ii})D_{i-1} -\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}h_{ki} \left(\prod_{j=k}^{i-1}h_{j+1,j}\right)D_{k-1}.\]

Each non-diagonal choice forces the intervening subdiagonal chain, leaving the leading minor of size \(k-1\); this is why there are no missing minors. Naive polynomial accumulation costs \(O(n^3)\) altogether. Determinant interpolation at \(n+1\) distinct field points instead costs \(O(n^4)\) with naive determinants and needs a field large enough to supply those points.

For a huge sparse matrix, dense elimination wastes work on zeroes. Wiedemann chooses random vectors \(u,v\), generates \(a_i=u^T A^i v\), and applies Berlekamp–Massey. With enough terms, this recovers the scalar sequence’s minimal polynomial exactly; random projection makes it equal to the matrix’s minimal polynomial with high probability, with a bound that depends on the field and invariant factors. Applications include sparse linear systems and determinants after random diagonal preconditioning.

Assumptions and failure modes:

  • It is randomized; a projection can miss invariant factors. Repeat independently.
  • A small fixed field may need an extension field and a proved projection strategy. Changing the prime changes the input problem; several primes help integer reconstruction only when that is the actual goal.
  • One sparse matrix-vector multiply must be fast; otherwise dense elimination is safer.
  • Recovering a determinant requires more than the scalar recurrence: use a proven preconditioning recipe and account for the preconditioner’s determinant.

Generating \(2n\) terms costs \(O(n\,\mathcal V+n^2)\) field operations, where \(\mathcal V\) is one matrix-vector multiplication cost and the \(n^2\) term allows naive BM. A returned annihilator can be tested on additional vectors, but such random checks have an error probability; a deterministic certificate requires checking its action on a spanning set. This optional section is a decision guide for black-box methods, not a replacement for a specified preconditioned solver.

6. Common pitfalls

  • Division modulo a composite number silently invalidates Gaussian elimination.
  • Floating-point partial pivoting helps numerical stability but does not repair an ill-conditioned model; “largest pivot” has no algebraic meaning in a finite field.
  • A determinant changes sign on every row swap.
  • A graph XOR basis uses cycle values, not raw edge weights.
  • Kirchhoff counts multigraph edges separately; collapsing them without adding weights changes the answer.
  • Berlekamp–Massey coefficient sign conventions differ among templates. Verify on Fibonacci before a contest.

Exercises and solution workshop

1. Concept check: a fiber count

Over \(\mathbb F_3\), how many triples satisfy \(x+y+z=1\) and \(x+2y=0\)? Hint: choose one coordinate freely.

NoteSolution

The second equation gives \(x=y\), and the first gives \(z=1+y\) modulo \(3\). There are three solutions, corresponding to \(y=0,1,2\). The two rows are independent, so this also checks \(3^{3-2}=3\). The solutions form a translate of the kernel, not a subspace: the zero vector is not among them.

2. Application: a partition objective

Given nonnegative \(B\)-bit words, divide them into two groups and maximize the sum of the two group XORs. Derive the reduction used in ABC141 F. Hint: let \(T\) be the XOR of all inputs and inspect one bit of \(X+(T\mathbin{\oplus}X)\).

NoteSolution

At a set bit of \(T\), exactly one group has the bit, giving a fixed contribution. At an unset bit, both groups agree and contribute twice that bit of \(X\). Hence the objective is \(T+2(X\mathbin{\&}\neg T)\), with the complement restricted to the \(B\) input bits. Mask every input by \(\neg T\) and maximize subset XOR by a basis, greedily improving the answer from high pivot to low. This costs \(O(nB)\) time and \(O(B)\) space. For \(3,6,5\), \(T=0\) and maximum subset XOR is \(6\), giving \(12\). Maximizing the unmasked \(X\) would optimize irrelevant fixed bits. If both groups must be nonempty and \(n\ge2\), an optimum with positive masked XOR is already proper; if the maximum is zero, all partitions attain \(T\) and any proper one suffices.

3. Synthesis: minimum-price spanning choices

Prove that sorting vectors by nonnegative cost and accepting a vector exactly when it raises rank produces a cheapest basis of their span. Hint: exchange one vector of an optimal basis when greedy accepts its next vector.

NoteSolution

Maintain an optimal basis containing the accepted greedy prefix. Express the next accepted vector \(v\) in that basis. At least one vector \(w\) with nonzero coefficient lies outside the prefix, since \(v\) is independent of the prefix. Replacing \(w\) by \(v\) preserves a basis: the expression solves for \(w\) in the new basis. Every vector processed before \(v\) already lies in the prefix span, so such a \(w\) cannot have been an earlier rejected vector. Its cost is at least the cost of \(v\). The exchange preserves optimality and extends the invariant. Sorting plus insertion costs \(O(n\log n+nB)\) for XOR vectors. For costs \(c_1=4,c_2=5,c_3=3\), accept \(3\) then \(1\), costing \(7\); raw numeric order would instead choose the more expensive pair \(1,2\).

4. Advanced: weighted graph versus modular certificate

A triangle has edge weights \(2,3,5\). Count its weighted trees modulo \(31\). Does the answer certify disconnection? Hint: enumerate the deleted edge.

NoteSolution

The three weights are \(2\cdot3\), \(2\cdot5\), and \(3\cdot5\), totaling \(31\), so the modular determinant is zero although the graph is connected. Exact nonnegative weights and residue-field computations answer different questions. This is why a determinant used as a randomized existence certificate needs its own error analysis; see algebraic graph methods.

Contest checklist and reference sheet

  • Identify the scalar field or ring before selecting a routine.
  • State what the columns, equations, or bit coordinates represent.
  • Count independent parameters; distinguish solutions from distinct images.
  • Check whether a graph statement concerns walks, paths, or trees.
  • Prove a recurrence exists before extrapolating a sampled prefix.
  • Verify determinant signs, coefficient orientation, and the zero-dimensional case.
