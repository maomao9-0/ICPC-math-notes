10 — Linear Algebra over Finite Fields

Linear algebra appears whenever constraints are linear, path choices form an XOR space, spanning trees must be counted, or a long recurrence must be compressed. Always identify the scalar domain first: real numbers, \(\mathbb F_p\), and \(\mathbb F_2\) have different numerical and structural hazards.

1. Gaussian elimination, rank, and determinants

Over a field, row operations preserve the solution set. Maintain a pivot row \(r\); for each column choose a nonzero entry, swap it to \(r\), normalize, and eliminate. Rank is the number of pivots. A zero row with a nonzero augmented entry means inconsistency; otherwise there are \(m-\operatorname{rank}\) free variables.

This is \(O(nm\min(n,m))\). For determinant, do not normalize: each row swap negates the answer, multiply by the pivot, then eliminate below using a[i][c]/pivot. Composite moduli are not fields: a nonzero pivot may be noninvertible. Use prime-factor methods, fraction-free elimination, or exact integer algorithms instead.

Worked example. Over \(\mathbb F_5\), \(x+y=1\), \(2x+3y=0\). Subtract twice the first row from the second: \(y=3\); hence \(x=3\). The integer solution is irrelevant—the field is part of the problem.

2. XOR basis as elimination over \(\mathbb F_2\)

A set of machine words is a vector set whose coordinates are bits. Keep at most one pivot per highest bit.

Insertion costs \(O(B)\). If rank is \(r\), the span has \(2^{r}\) distinct values. For representation counts from \(n\) input vectors, every reachable value has \(2^{n-r}\) representations. To obtain the k-th smallest XOR, first reduce to a canonical basis (eliminate pivot bits in both directions); a merely triangular basis is insufficient.

Graph recognition: assign each vertex an XOR distance from a DFS root. Every non-tree edge yields a cycle XOR dist[u] ^ w ^ dist[v]. All walk XORs from \(s\) to \(t\) are dist[s]^dist[t] plus the cycle space.

3. Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theorem

For a weighted undirected graph, form the Laplacian

\[L_{ii}=\sum_{ij}w_{ij},\qquad L_{ij}=-w_{ij}.\]

Delete any one row and the corresponding column. The determinant of the resulting \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) cofactor equals the total weight of spanning trees, where a tree’s weight is the product of its edge weights.

Why it works: orient edges arbitrarily and let \(B\) be the incidence matrix. Then \(L=BWB^{T}\). Cauchy–Binet expands a cofactor determinant over edge subsets; exactly the spanning trees have determinant squared equal to one. Directed arborescences use an in- or out-Laplacian—choose the convention from the desired root orientation and test it on two vertices.

Complexity is \(O(n^{3})\) with dense elimination. Parallel edge weights add. Self-loops do not contribute. Under a modulus, the determinant counts modulo that modulus.

4. Linear recurrences and characteristic polynomials

If \(s_n=c_1s_{n-1}+\cdots+c_ks_{n-k}\), then \(x^{k}-c_1x^{k-1}-\cdots-c_k\) annihilates the sequence. To find \(s_N\), reduce \(x^{N}\) modulo this polynomial, then combine the first \(k\) terms. Binary exponentiation with polynomial reduction costs \(O(k^{2}\log N)\) (Kitamasa); NTT-based methods or Bostan–Mori improve large \(k\).

Berlekamp–Massey recovers the shortest recurrence from about twice as many terms over a field. Preconditions: exact field arithmetic and enough terms; a short prefix can admit a spurious recurrence.

Cayley–Hamilton says a square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial. Thus every scalar sequence \(u^T A^n v\) has order at most the matrix dimension. This bridges matrix exponentiation, recurrences, and generating functions.

5. Characteristic polynomial and black-box methods

For dense matrices, Hessenberg reduction followed by the determinant recurrence computes the characteristic polynomial in \(O(n^{3})\). Determinant interpolation at \(n+1\) points is simpler but \(O(n^{4})\) with naive determinants.

For a huge sparse matrix, Wiedemann chooses random vectors \(u,v\), generates \(a_i=u^T A^i v\), and applies Berlekamp–Massey. The recovered minimal polynomial equals the relevant projection’s minimal polynomial with high probability. Applications include sparse linear systems and determinants after random diagonal preconditioning.

Assumptions and failure modes:

  • It is randomized; a projection can miss invariant factors. Repeat independently.
  • Field size should be comfortably large. Extension fields or several primes reduce failure probability.
  • One sparse matrix-vector multiply must be fast; otherwise dense elimination is safer.
  • Recovering a determinant requires more than the scalar recurrence: use a proven preconditioning recipe and account for the preconditioner’s determinant.

6. Common pitfalls

  • Division modulo a composite number silently invalidates Gaussian elimination.
  • Partial pivoting is essential over floating point, but “largest pivot” has no meaning in a finite field.
  • A determinant changes sign on every row swap.
  • A graph XOR basis uses cycle values, not raw edge weights.
  • Kirchhoff counts multigraph edges separately; collapsing them without adding weights changes the answer.
  • Berlekamp–Massey coefficient sign conventions differ among templates. Verify on Fibonacci before a contest.

7. Practice, in order

  1. Advanced — Xor Sum 3, AtCoder ABC141 F (English, 600). XOR basis with an objective transformation. Official editorial.
    Hints
    1. Bits set in the XOR of all numbers contribute a fixed amount.
    2. Mask those bits away from every vector.
    3. Maximize a subset XOR on the remaining bits and relate the two colors.
  2. Advanced — Spices, AtCoder ABC236 F (English, 500). Minimum-weight basis / linear matroid greedy. Official editorial.
    Hints
    1. Being able to create every mask means selecting a full-rank basis.
    2. Sort candidates by cost.
    3. Insert a vector exactly when it raises the XOR-basis rank (matroid greedy).
  3. Mastery — Xor-matic Number of the Graph, CF 724G (English, 2600). Cycle space and aggregate counting. Official tutorial.
    Hints
    1. DFS XOR distances make each non-tree edge a cycle vector.
    2. For a bit represented in the cycle basis, exactly half of span values contain it.
    3. Otherwise its contribution depends only on endpoint distance bits; sum per component.
