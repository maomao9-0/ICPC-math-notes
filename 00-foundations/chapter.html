00 — Mathematical Foundations and Proof

A bug that testing cannot find

Here is a situation every competitive programmer recognizes. You are solving a problem about an array, you notice a pattern in the samples, you code the pattern, and you get Wrong answer on test 7. The implementation is perfect. The pattern was false.

Now compare that with a different failure. You reduce the problem to “find the smallest \(x\) with \(Kx \equiv -S \pmod N\)”, you call your extended-Euclid routine, and you get Wrong answer on test 3 because a solution exists only when \(\gcd(K,N)\) divides \(S\) — a condition you never checked, because nobody told you it was there.

Both bugs have the same cause: a claim was used without being proved, and the claim was false or incomplete. No amount of stress testing reliably finds these, because the wrong claim is usually right on small cases; that is precisely why it looked convincing.

This chapter is about the small set of habits that catch such bugs before you write code: saying exactly what you are claiming, knowing which direction of an “if and only if” you actually need, proving that a loop makes progress, and counting a set of objects in two different ways to check yourself. None of it requires a mathematics degree. All of it is the working language of every later chapter.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

You need arithmetic with integers and fractions, and nothing else. The notation reference collects conventions used throughout the book.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • read a quantified statement (“for every … there exists …”) and say precisely what a counterexample to it would look like;
  • pick a proof technique — direct, contrapositive, contradiction, induction — for a claim, and know why each one is valid;
  • state an invariant for a process and use it to prove that a target state is unreachable, or a potential function to prove that a loop ends;
  • derive Bézout’s identity from Euclid’s algorithm and use it to decide when \(ax + by = c\) has integer solutions and what all of them are;
  • justify a counting formula by exhibiting a bijection or by counting one set in two ways.

If all of this is familiar, skim the section headings and jump to the exercises; they are designed to locate gaps rather than to gatekeep.

The vocabulary, in one page

Mathematical writing is terse mostly because the same handful of symbols appear everywhere. Here they are, with the reading you should hear in your head.

Sets. \(\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Q\), and \(\mathbb R\) are the integers, the rationals (fractions \(p/q\) of integers with \(q \ne 0\)), and the reals. \(\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}\) is the nonnegative integers. A set is an unordered collection with no repeats — think std::set, not std::vector; a sequence has order and may repeat. The empty set is \(\varnothing\), and \(|S|\) is the number of elements of a finite \(S\).

Index ranges. \([0,n)\) means the integers \(0, 1, \ldots, n-1\), the same half-open convention as a C++ loop. An indexed sum \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a_i\) has one term per index in that range.

Empty things. An empty sum is \(0\) and an empty product is \(1\). These are not arbitrary: they are the values that make recurrences correct at size zero, exactly as an accumulator is initialized to the identity of its operation. In the same spirit, there is exactly one empty sequence, so a formula counting sequences should return \(1\), not \(0\), at size zero. Off-by-one errors in combinatorics are almost always disagreements about this convention.

Functions. \(f : A \to B\) assigns one output in \(B\) to each input in \(A\). It is injective when different inputs give different outputs (no collisions), surjective when every element of \(B\) is hit, and bijective when both hold. A bijection between finite sets is a perfect matching between their elements, so it proves \(|A| = |B|\) — the single most useful proof technique in counting.

Indicators. \([P]\) is \(1\) when the proposition \(P\) is true and \(0\) otherwise. For instance \(\sum_{i=1}^{10} [\,2 \mid i\,] = 5\). Indicators let you move a condition out of the range of a sum and into the summand, which is often what unlocks an algebraic simplification.

Divisibility and floors. \(a \mid b\) (“\(a\) divides \(b\)”) means \(b = ak\) for some integer \(k\); its negation is \(a \nmid b\). The floor \(\lfloor x \rfloor\) is the greatest integer at most \(x\). Note \(\lfloor -3/2 \rfloor = -2\), while C++ integer division gives \(-1\): floor rounds down, the language truncates toward zero. Every chapter that uses floors means the mathematical one.

Logic: what exactly must be proved?

A surprising amount of contest debugging is really the discovery that you proved a different statement from the one you needed.

\(P \Rightarrow Q\) means: every instance satisfying \(P\) also satisfies \(Q\). Its converse \(Q \Rightarrow P\) is a genuinely different claim, and one of the two can hold while the other fails. \(P \Longleftrightarrow Q\) asserts both.

This matters constantly in reductions. When you say “the answer is yes exactly when the graph has a perfect matching”, you have made two claims: a yes-instance gives a matching, and a matching gives a yes-instance. Skipping the second is how you end up counting configurations that do not correspond to anything.

WarningWorked failure: a necessary condition is not a solution

If a positive integer is divisible by \(6\), then it is divisible by \(3\). The converse fails at \(n = 3\).

Practically: a necessary condition is a filter. It can prove an instance impossible, and that is valuable — checking parity or a gcd condition first can save an entire wrong approach. But passing the filter does not build a solution, and a solution written as “check the condition, then output yes” is correct only if you also proved sufficiency.

Quantifiers. \(\forall\) is “for every” and \(\exists\) is “there exists”. Their order is not decoration. “For every integer \(x\) there exists an integer \(y > x\)” is true. Swap the quantifiers — “there exists an integer \(y\) that is larger than every integer \(x\)” — and it is false. When reading a problem statement, note which quantity is allowed to depend on which; the dependency order is usually the whole difficulty of the problem.

To disprove a \(\forall\) statement, exhibit one counterexample. To negate “for every \(x\) there exists \(y\) with \(P(x,y)\)”, say “there exists an \(x\) such that every \(y\) fails \(P(x,y)\)” — negation flips each quantifier and negates the inside. Doing this mechanically is useful: the negation tells you exactly what a counterexample must look like, which is what your brute-force checker should search for.

Contrapositive and contradiction. The contrapositive of \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\), and the two are equivalent — if \(P\) forced \(Q\), then failing \(Q\) rules out \(P\). Sometimes one direction is far easier to argue. To show that \(n^2\) even forces \(n\) even, assume instead that \(n\) is odd, write \(n = 2k+1\), and compute \(n^2 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1\), which is odd. A proof by contradiction assumes the claim false and derives an impossibility; it is the right tool when the negation gives you something concrete to manipulate.

Induction: writing a correctness recurrence

Induction is the proof technique that most resembles code you already write. To prove a statement \(P(n)\) for every \(n \ge n_0\):

  1. prove the base case \(P(n_0)\);
  2. prove the step \(P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1)\).

The base starts a chain, and the step propagates it to every index. In strong induction the step may assume \(P(k)\) for all valid \(k < n\), which is the exact analogue of a dynamic program reading every smaller state. In fact the correspondence is precise: a DP is correct when its transition is an induction step and its dependency graph reaches only already-computed states. Writing the induction is writing the correctness proof of your DP.

Worked example: a closed form. Let \(S_n = 1 + 2 + \cdots + n\), with \(S_0 = 0\). Claim: \(S_n = n(n+1)/2\). At \(n = 0\) both sides are \(0\). Assuming it at \(n\),

\[S_{n+1} = S_n + (n+1) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + (n+1) = \frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}.\]

Each equality is the definition, the induction hypothesis, or plain algebra — a good proof lets the reader label every step that way.

One warning that will recur for the rest of the book: this derivation lives in the integers and rationals, where dividing by \(2\) is harmless. If you evaluate \(n(n+1)/2\) modulo \(m\), divide the even factor by \(2\) before reducing, because \(2\) may have no inverse modulo \(m\) (it has none modulo \(4\), for instance). The identity is true; the naive modular translation of it is not.

Worked example: every integer factors into primes. A prime is an integer \(p \ge 2\) whose only positive divisors are \(1\) and \(p\). Claim: every \(n \ge 2\) is a product of primes. Strong induction: if \(n\) is prime we are done; otherwise \(n = ab\) with \(2 \le a, b < n\), and the hypothesis factors both \(a\) and \(b\). Note carefully what this gives — existence of a factorization. That it is unique is a separate and harder fact, proved below from Bézout, and the gap between the two is not a technicality: uniqueness is what makes “compare prime signatures” a valid algorithm.

WarningFailed induction: all objects have the same color

Claim: in any set of objects, all objects have the same color. Base: a one-element set, trivially true. Step: given \(n+1\) objects, the first \(n\) share a color and the last \(n\) share a color, and the two groups overlap, so all \(n+1\) agree.

The conclusion is absurd, so the step must be broken — and it is, at exactly one place. Going from \(1\) to \(2\), the “first \(n\)” and “last \(n\)” groups are disjoint, so there is nothing to transfer the color through. An induction step must be valid at its first boundary, not merely for comfortable large \(n\). Whenever an induction feels too easy, test the step at the very smallest index.

Invariants, potentials, and extremal choices

Many contest problems ask whether a process can reach a target state. Searching the state space is hopeless when it is exponential; the mathematical move is to find a quantity that cannot change, and observe that the start and target disagree on it.

An invariant is a property preserved by every allowed transition. Proving one takes two steps: it holds initially, and if it holds before an arbitrary allowed move then it holds after.

A potential function assigns a number to a state and moves in a controlled direction. A nonnegative integer potential that strictly decreases proves termination, because a decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers is finite. A real-valued potential that merely decreases proves nothing: \(1, 1/2, 1/4, \ldots\) decreases forever.

NoteWorked example: an obstruction and a matching construction

A board has a \(0\) or \(1\) in each cell. One move picks an allowed pair of cells and flips both.

Obstruction. The parity of the number of ones never changes: flipping two cells changes the count by \(-2\), \(0\), or \(+2\). So a board containing exactly one \(1\) can never become all zeros. This argument needed no search at all.

Is parity enough? Only once we say which pairs are allowed. Model cells as vertices and allowed pairs as edges. If that graph is connected, even parity is also sufficient: take a spanning tree, process vertices from the leaves upward, and flip a leaf together with its parent exactly when the leaf holds a \(1\). A cleared leaf is never touched again, so the invariant “all processed vertices are zero” is maintained. When only the root remains, it must be \(0\), because the total parity is even and everything else is zero. The construction costs \(O(V + E)\) with one traversal.

Notice the shape of the complete answer: an invariant proves that some instances are impossible, and a construction proves that all the others are possible. Either half alone leaves the problem unsolved.

An extremal argument picks a smallest, largest, or otherwise most extreme object and then exploits what that object is unable to do. It works because every finite nonempty set of integers has a minimum, and — more strongly — every nonempty set of nonnegative integers has one even if it is infinite.

Worked example: a finite tree has a leaf. Take a longest simple path in a tree with at least two vertices, and look at one endpoint \(u\). Could \(u\) have a neighbour besides the next vertex on the path? If that neighbour is off the path, the path was not longest. If it is elsewhere on the path, we have a cycle, and a tree has none. So \(u\) has degree one. This is the reason that “repeatedly strip leaves” is a legitimate induction on trees, used later in the matrix–tree theorem in Chapter 07.

Euclid and Bézout: the first real tool

Everything in Chapter 01 rests on one identity, so it is worth deriving rather than quoting.

For nonnegative integers \(a, b\) not both zero, \(g = \gcd(a,b)\) is the greatest positive integer dividing both. Division with remainder writes \(a = qb + r\) with \(0 \le r < b\) whenever \(b > 0\). The key observation is that an integer divides both \(a\) and \(b\) exactly when it divides both \(b\) and \(r = a - qb\): each of \(a\) and \(r\) is an integer combination of the other pair. The two pairs therefore have the same set of common divisors, hence the same greatest one:

\[\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, r).\]

The remainders strictly decrease and stay nonnegative, so the process stops — a potential-function argument — and the last nonzero remainder is \(g\). That is Euclid’s algorithm, and the proof above is the whole of its correctness.

Now track a little more information. Every remainder produced along the way is an integer combination \(au + bv\) of the original inputs: this is true of the starting values (\(a = a\cdot 1 + b \cdot 0\) and \(b = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 1\)), and subtracting \(q\) times one combination from another produces a combination again. Since \(g\) is one of the remainders, there exist integers \(u, v\) with

\[au + bv = g.\]

This is Bézout’s identity, and the running bookkeeping of \((u,v)\) is exactly what the extended Euclidean algorithm returns.

Small instance. For \(a = 30\), \(b = 18\): the remainders are \(12\), then \(6\), then \(0\), so \(g = 6\). Back-substituting, \(6 = 18 - 12 = 18 - (30 - 18) = 2 \cdot 18 - 30\). Hence \(30x + 18y = 6\) has the solution \((x,y) = (-1, 2)\).

When is \(ax + by = c\) solvable, and what are all the solutions? Solvable exactly when \(g \mid c\). Necessity is immediate: \(g\) divides every value the left-hand side can take. Sufficiency: multiply Bézout’s coefficients by \(c/g\). And when \(a, b > 0\), every solution is obtained from a single solution \((x_0, y_0)\) as

\[x = x_0 + \frac{b}{g}t, \qquad y = y_0 - \frac{a}{g}t, \qquad t \in \mathbb Z.\]

Why are there no others? Subtract two solutions to get \((a/g)\,\Delta x = -(b/g)\,\Delta y\). The integers \(a/g\) and \(b/g\) are coprime, so \(b/g\) divides \(\Delta x\) — this is Bézout again — which is precisely the parameterization above. Degenerate coefficients are handled directly; if both \(a\) and \(b\) are zero, the equation is solvable exactly when \(c = 0\).

This completeness statement is what contest problems actually need. They rarely ask for a solution; they ask for the smallest nonnegative one, or for the number of solutions in a window, and those questions are answered by the parameter \(t\), not by whatever pair extended Euclid happened to return.

Cost. Euclid performs \(O(\log \max(a,b))\) divisions on positive inputs, because the larger argument at least halves every two steps. That counts arithmetic operations; on big integers each division has its own bit cost.

NoteWhy prime factorization is unique

Suppose a prime \(p\) divides \(ab\) but not \(a\). Then \(\gcd(p,a) = 1\), so Bézout gives \(pu + av = 1\). Multiply by \(b\):

\[pub + abv = b.\]

Both terms on the left are divisible by \(p\) — the first visibly, the second because \(p \mid ab\) — so \(p \mid b\). Hence a prime dividing a product divides one of the factors.

Uniqueness follows: a prime appearing in one factorization of \(n\) must appear in any other, so cancel it from both and repeat. Two factorizations of the same integer therefore agree up to order.

Double counting and bijections

The last foundational habit is the one that makes combinatorics feel like programming rather than memorization: before rearranging a sum, name the objects its terms count. Then, if you can count the same collection a second way, the two answers must agree — and you have proved an identity without any algebra at all.

The handshake identity. In a finite undirected graph, count incidences: pairs \((v, e)\) where \(v\) is an endpoint of edge \(e\). Group by vertex and you get \(\sum_v \deg(v)\). Group by edge and you get \(2|E|\), since each edge has two endpoints. The two totals count one set, so they are equal. (With self-loops, define degree by counting endpoint occurrences, and the statement survives.)

Binomial coefficients from a bijection. Let \(\binom{n}{k}\) be the number of \(k\)-element subsets of an \(n\)-element set; it is \(0\) when \(k < 0\) or \(k > n\). Count ordered lists of \(k\) distinct elements directly: \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\) choices. Now count them a second way: choose the underlying subset, then order it, which is \(\binom{n}{k} \cdot k!\) lists. Hence

\[\binom nk = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}, \qquad 0 \le k \le n,\]

where \(n!\) is the product of \(1\) through \(n\) and \(0! = 1\). The division is exact over the integers — the left side counts things. Over a modulus it needs its own justification, which is the subject of Chapter 04.

Splitting on whether one distinguished element is in the subset gives Pascal’s recurrence \(\binom nk = \binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}\) for \(n \ge 1\): the subsets avoiding the element, plus the subsets using it.

NoteWorked example: a team and its captain

From \(n \ge 1\) people, choose a nonempty team and designate one member as captain. How many outcomes?

Count by team size. A team of size \(k\) can be chosen in \(\binom nk\) ways and has \(k\) captain choices, giving \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} k \binom nk\).

Count by captain first. Pick the captain: \(n\) ways. Each of the remaining \(n-1\) people independently joins or does not: \(2^{n-1}\) ways.

Both describe the same outcomes, so

\[\sum_{k} k \binom nk = n\,2^{n-1}.\]

Check at \(n = 3\): the left side is \(1\cdot3 + 2\cdot3 + 3\cdot1 = 12\), and the right side is \(3 \cdot 4 = 12\). Note that the \(2^{n-1}\) counts subsets of the remaining people, not orderings of them — mixing up those two is the most common way a double count goes wrong.

Contest translation and checklist

Use a library routine only after your reduction has a written contract: what goes in, what comes out, and under what hypotheses. Keep four separate claims straight, because they fail in different ways:

  1. the model faithfully describes the problem;
  2. every valid solution is represented in the model (nothing is lost);
  3. the algorithm returns the quantity actually asked for;
  4. the resource bound fits the constraints.

A brute-force comparison on small inputs can refute any of the four, which makes it extremely worthwhile. It can never establish them for arbitrary inputs, which is why the derivation still has to exist.

Before coding, answer:

  • What is the domain, including the empty case, zero, and the boundary of every range?
  • Is my condition necessary, sufficient, or both — and which do I need?
  • Which concrete object does one summand, or one DP state, represent?
  • What is preserved by a transition, and what guarantees termination?
  • When I divide a count by a symmetry factor, are all the groups really the same size?
  • Does my complexity count arithmetic operations or actual machine work on numbers of this size?

Exercises, hints, and solutions

1. Quantifiers and a counterexample — concept check

Negate the statement “every pair of nonzero residues modulo \(m\) has a nonzero product”. Then find the smallest \(m \ge 2\) for which the original statement is false.

TipHint and solution

Hint: the negation asserts the existence of a bad pair; look for a modulus that factors.

The negation is: “there exist two nonzero residues modulo \(m\) whose product is zero.” Modulo \(2\) and modulo \(3\), multiplying out all pairs shows no such pair exists. Modulo \(4\), \(2 \cdot 2 = 0\). The answer is \(m = 4\).

This is the first appearance of the theme that dominates Chapter 01: nonzero does not mean invertible. Modulo \(4\), the residue \(2\) is nonzero yet has no reciprocal, so any derivation that “divides by \(2\)” there is invalid.

2. Count the same marked subset twice — derivation

For \(n \ge 2\), derive \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} k(k-1)\binom nk = n(n-1)2^{\,n-2}\).

TipHint and solution

Hint: mimic the team-and-captain count, but mark an ordered pair.

Count pairs consisting of a subset together with an ordered pair of two distinct chosen members.

By subset size: a \(k\)-element subset admits \(k(k-1)\) ordered pairs, giving the left-hand side.

By marked pair first: choose the ordered pair in \(n(n-1)\) ways, then let each of the remaining \(n-2\) people independently join or not, giving \(2^{n-2}\).

Both count the same objects, so the identity holds. Check at \(n = 3\): the left side is \(2 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 1 = 12\) and the right side is \(3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 12\).

3. All bounded solutions — modelling

Find every pair of nonnegative integers with \(6x + 9y = 45\) and \(x \le 6\).

TipHint and solution

Hint: divide by the gcd first, then use the full parameterization rather than stopping at one solution.

Since \(\gcd(6,9) = 3\) divides \(45\), solutions exist; dividing through gives \(2x + 3y = 15\). One solution is \((0,5)\), and the general one is \(x = 3t\), \(y = 5 - 2t\).

Now impose the constraints: \(y \ge 0\) gives \(t \le 2\), and \(x \ge 0\) with \(x \le 6\) gives \(0 \le t \le 2\). The pairs are \((0,5)\), \((3,3)\), \((6,1)\).

Guessing a single solution would have reported one pair out of three. The parameterization is what makes the enumeration complete.

4. Flip graph on a disconnected board — synthesis

Extend the two-cell flip model to an allowed-pair graph that may be disconnected. State a necessary and sufficient condition for reaching the all-zero state, and give a construction.

TipHint and solution

Hint: a move never crosses between components, so each component carries its own invariant.

Every move flips two endpoints of one edge, so it stays inside a single connected component; the parity of ones is therefore invariant per component. Hence even parity in every component is necessary.

It is also sufficient: apply the spanning-tree leaf procedure independently in each component. An isolated vertex is its own component with no edges, so it must already hold \(0\) — its parity condition says exactly that.

Total cost is \(O(V+E)\), with at most \(V - 1\) flips across all components. The answer combines an obstruction with a matching construction, which is strictly stronger than either half.

Summary and reference sheet

  • An implication needs one direction; an equivalence needs two, and reductions almost always need two.
  • Negating a quantified claim tells you what a counterexample looks like — and therefore what your brute-force checker should hunt for.
  • Induction needs a valid base and a step that works at the first boundary. Strong induction is the proof form of a dynamic program.
  • An invariant proves impossibility; a construction proves possibility; a complete answer usually needs both.
  • A strictly decreasing nonnegative integer potential proves termination. A decreasing real quantity does not.
  • Euclid: \(\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)\). Bézout: \(au + bv = \gcd(a,b)\). Hence \(ax + by = c\) is solvable exactly when \(\gcd(a,b) \mid c\), and all solutions form the one-parameter family above.
  • A prime dividing a product divides a factor; this is why prime factorizations are unique.
  • A bijection or a double count proves an equality by describing one finite collection in two ways. Factorial division is exact over the integers and needs separate justification over a modulus.

Continue with modular algebra, where Bézout immediately becomes the theory of division modulo \(m\). Later, combinatorics, linear algebra, and probability build their own objects on the same foundations.
