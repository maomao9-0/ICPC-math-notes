11 — Set Power Series

Counting with labels that cannot be reused

Here is a task that looks like it should be a standard subset DP, and is not: count the connected labelled graphs on \(n\) vertices. Counting all graphs is trivial — \(2^{\binom n2}\). Connectivity is the hard part, because a graph splits into components whose vertex sets are specific subsets, and different subsets induce different graphs.

The fix is to work with a product that remembers which labels each factor used. A polynomial product remembers only a size: combining a structure of size \(3\) with one of size \(2\) gives size \(5\), whatever labels were involved. Here we want a product that refuses to combine two structures sharing a vertex. Then “all objects” is the exponential of “connected objects”, exactly as in Chapter 10 — and a logarithm turns the easy count into the hard one.

That product is subset convolution, and the algebra it lives in is the set power series. Two things make the chapter work: a trick that computes the product in \(O(n^22^n)\) instead of \(3^n\), and a division-free way to take the logarithm so that the method survives small characteristic.

Prerequisites: partitions and counting, subset transforms, and the exp/log analogy from formal series. You should know how subset zeta and Möbius transforms respectively sum over subsets and undo that sum.

The objectives are to derive fast subset convolution, prove the connected-component identities, identify exactly when factorial division is legal, and implement the mathematical contracts using your own transform library.

Definitions: a polynomial whose labels cannot repeat

Let \(U=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\) and let \(R\) be a commutative ring with identity. A set series is a table \(f[S]\in R\) for every \(S\subseteq U\). For \(S\subseteq U\), write \(x^S=\prod_{i\in S}x_i\); this is a monomial, not an ordinary integer power. We impose \(x_i^2=0\) for each label. The resulting algebra is

\[R[x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}]/(x_0^2,\ldots,x_{n-1}^2).\]

Multiplying \(x^Ax^B\) returns \(x^{A\cup B}\) if \(A,B\) are disjoint, and zero otherwise. For example, \(x^{\{0,1\}}x^{\{1,2\}}=0\), but \(x^{\{0\}}x^{\{1,2\}}=x^{\{0,1,2\}}\). The empty-set coefficient is the constant term. The multiplicative identity has coefficient one at the empty set and zero elsewhere.

Suppose \(f[T]\) counts a first structure on labels \(T\), and \(g[S\setminus T]\) counts a second structure on the remaining labels. Summing over the choice of the first label set gives subset convolution:

\[ (f*g)[S]=\sum_{T\subseteq S}f[T]g[S\setminus T]. \]

Every label is assigned to exactly one of the two structures. Over all masks, the naive cost is \(\sum_S2^{|S|}=3^n\): each label is outside \(S\), in the first part, or in the second part.

Example. On \(\{0,1\}\), suppose \(f[\{0\}]=2\), \(f[\{1\}]=3\), \(g[\{0\}]=5\), \(g[\{1\}]=7\), with all other coefficients zero. Then \((f*g)[\{0,1\}]=2\cdot7+3\cdot5=29\). An ordinary size polynomial would also combine two structures using the same label, which is forbidden here.

Ranked convolution and the overlap invariant

The naive \(3^n\) is already better than brute force, but the transforms of Chapter 08 handle union convolution in \(O(n2^n)\) — they just do not enforce disjointness. The trick is to track cardinality alongside the union, and let arithmetic detect overlaps: if \(A \cup B = S\) and \(|A| + |B| = |S|\), then \(A\) and \(B\) must be disjoint.

Separate a series by cardinality before applying the subset zeta transform:

\[\widehat f_k[S]=\sum_{\substack{A\subseteq S\\|A|=k}}f[A], \qquad 0\le k\le n.\]

For each \(S\), multiply the rank polynomials and retain ranks \(0\) through \(n\):

\[H_k[S]=\sum_{i=0}^k\widehat f_i[S]\widehat g_{k-i}[S] =\sum_{\substack{A,B\subseteq S\\|A|+|B|=k}}f[A]g[B].\]

These pairs may overlap. Now Möbius-invert each rank layer. For a fixed pair \((A,B)\), its contribution to inverted entry \(S\) is multiplied by

\[\sum_{A\cup B\subseteq T\subseteq S}(-1)^{|S\setminus T|} =\begin{cases}1,&A\cup B=S,\\0,&A\cup B\ne S.\end{cases}\]

Indeed, every element of \(S\setminus(A\cup B)\) can be included or excluded, producing \((1-1)^{|S\setminus(A\cup B)|}\). Thus the inverted layer is

\[h_k[S]=\sum_{\substack{A\cup B=S\\|A|+|B|=k}}f[A]g[B].\]

Finally take \(k=|S|\). Since \(|A|+|B|=|A\cup B|+|A\cap B|\), this equality forces \(A\cap B=\varnothing\). Therefore \(h_{|S|}[S]=(f*g)[S]\).

WarningDo not clear intermediate overlap layers

Input layers above \(|S|\) are zero. Product layers above \(|S|\) can be necessary: Möbius inversion needs them to cancel overlaps. On \(U=\{0,1\}\), let \(f[\{0\}]=g[\{0\}]=1\). Then \(H_2[\{0\}]=H_2[U]=1\). Inversion subtracts the first from the second and correctly returns zero. Clearing \(H_2[\{0\}]\) prematurely creates a false disjoint pair on \(U\).

There are \(n+1\) zeta/inverse transforms, each costing \(O(n2^n)\) additions/subtractions, and \(O(n^2)\) rank products for each mask. The total is \(O(n^22^n)\) ring operations and \(O(n2^n)\) coefficient storage. A ring with subtraction suffices; factorials and field division play no role in this multiplication algorithm. OR convolution differs: it keeps overlap and does not enforce \(|A|+|B|=|S|\).

Functions of a set series

With a fast product in hand, we can run the whole exp/log story from Chapter 09 here — and this is where connected-component counting actually happens.

Let \(g[\varnothing]=0\). Any product of more than \(n\) copies of \(g\) must repeat a label, so \(g^{n+1}=0\). This property is nilpotence. It makes substitution into an infinite formal series finite.

When \(1,2,\ldots,n\) are units in \(R\), define

\[\exp(g)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{g^k}{k!},\qquad \log(1+g)=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k-1}\frac{g^k}{k}.\]

Over \(\mathbb F_p\), the sufficient local condition is \(p>n\). The usual finite formal identities show these operations are inverse. We will also derive partition recurrences that work without dividing by these integers.

What does \(g^k/k!\) count? A term of \(g^k[S]\) chooses an ordered list of \(k\) disjoint nonempty blocks covering \(S\), with weight equal to the product of their \(g\)-values. Each unordered partition has exactly \(k!\) orders, because its nonempty blocks have distinct label sets. Hence

\[[x^S]\exp(g)=\sum_{\pi\text{ a partition of }S}\prod_{B\in\pi}g[B].\]

The empty set has one partition with no blocks; its product is one. This finite partition sum makes sense over any commutative ring even when the displayed factorial quotients do not.

Three-label example. If \(g[B]=1\) for every nonempty \(B\), the contributions on three labels are one one-block partition, three two-block partitions, and one three-block partition. The answer is five. If singleton blocks have weight \(a\) and larger blocks have weight zero, the answer on any \(S\) is \(a^{|S|}\): only the singleton partition survives.

Connected objects and a division-free recurrence

Let \(A[S]\) count all objects on \(S\) and \(C[S]\) count connected ones. Assume every object has a unique partition into nonempty connected components, and the allowed choices and weights factor across that partition. Then \(A=\exp(C)\), so \(C=\log(A)\) where the quotient formulas are legal.

For a formula valid over any coefficient ring, choose the least label \(v\in S\). Its component has one uniquely determined label set \(T\) containing \(v\). Therefore

\[A[S]=\sum_{\substack{T\subseteq S\\v\in T}}C[T]A[S\setminus T], \qquad C[S]=A[S]-\sum_{\substack{T\subsetneq S\\v\in T}}C[T]A[S\setminus T].\]

Use \(A[\varnothing]=1\) and process nonempty masks by increasing size. The second formula isolates the term \(T=S\). Each object appears once because its component containing \(v\) is unique. Enumerating these transitions costs \(O(3^n)\) operations and \(O(2^n)\) storage.

Graph example. For all simple graphs on a fixed vertex subset, \(A[S]=2^{\binom{|S|}{2}}\). Two vertices have \(A=2\), so \(C=2-1=1\). Three vertices have \(A=8\): subtract the all-singleton graph and three graphs with a connected pair plus an isolated vertex, leaving \(C=8-1-3=4\). They are the three paths and the triangle.

Fast exp, inverse, and log without factorial division

The special relation \(x_i^2=0\) supplies a short recurrence. Split off one label, writing a series as \(f=f_0+x_{n-1}f_1\), where both parts use only the other \(n-1\) labels. The coefficient \(f_1[S]\) equals the old coefficient at \(S\cup\{n-1\}\).

For \(g[\varnothing]=0\), let \(E_0\) be its partition exponential on the smaller universe. The distinguished label belongs to exactly one component; choosing its other labels gives

\[E(g_0+x_{n-1}g_1)=E(g_0)+x_{n-1}\bigl(E(g_0)*g_1\bigr).\]

Compute \(E(g_0)\) recursively and perform one subset convolution. With the ranked multiplier the recurrence is \(T(n)=T(n-1)+O(n^22^n)\), giving \(T(n)=O(n^22^n)\). This operation equals the factorial exponential wherever that formula exists and supplies its combinatorial extension otherwise.

An ordinary multiplicative inverse requires only that \(f[\varnothing]\) be a unit. If \(u=f_0^{-1}\), direct multiplication and \(x_{n-1}^2=0\) give

\[f^{-1}=u-x_{n-1}(u*u*f_1).\]

This is another \(O(n^22^n)\) recursion. Finally, for \(A[\varnothing]=1\), connected extraction obeys

\[L(A_0+x_{n-1}A_1)=L(A_0)+x_{n-1}(A_0^{-1}*A_1).\]

This follows by inverting the exponential split: \(A_1=A_0*C_1\). Recompute smaller inverses if desired; the geometric sum of their \(O(k^22^k)\) costs is still \(O(n^22^n)\). The resulting \(L\) is the inverse of the partition exponential over any ring, and agrees with the displayed formal logarithm when its denominators are units. It is not permission to evaluate \(1/k\) in small characteristic.

Composition and power projection

For an ordinary polynomial \(F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^m a_kz^k\), substitution \(F(g)=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(m,n)}a_kg^k\) is defined whenever \(g[\varnothing]=0\). Successive subset multiplications give the explicit bound \(O(n^32^n)\) when \(m\le n\). More specialized composition algorithms improve this; do not infer their cost from a single convolution. For an EGF \(\sum F_kz^k/k!\), use unit factorials or interpret coefficients by the corresponding weighted partition sums.

Power projection reverses the question. Given weights \(w[S]\), compute

\[b_k=\sum_{S\subseteq U}w[S]\,[x^S]g^k\qquad(0\le k\le n).\]

For fixed \(g\), composition is a linear map from \((a_k)\) to the subset table. Its transpose maps \(w\) to \((b_k)\), because

\[\sum_S w[S]\,[x^S]F(g)=\sum_k a_kb_k.\]

This equality explains the word “transpose” without an extra combinatorial assumption. Computing successive powers gives the same \(O(n^32^n)\) baseline. A transposed implementation of a faster linear composition circuit can share its asymptotic operation count; that is an additional library contract, not an unexplained property of the basic multiplier.

Contest translation: coloured bipartite graphs

For Lights Out on Connected Graph, ARC105 F, assign a bit \(s_v\) to whether vertex \(v\) is flipped an odd number of times. An edge \(uv\) becomes blue exactly when \(s_u+s_v=1\) in \(\mathbb F_2\). Thus the retained graph must be bipartite, and the problem asks for connected bipartite spanning subgraphs.

The count easier to obtain is coloured bipartite subgraphs. For every vertex set \(S\) and every colour class \(T\subseteq S\), choose any subset of original edges crossing \(T,S\setminus T\). If \(e(T,S\setminus T)\) is their number, set

\[A[S]=\sum_{T\subseteq S}2^{e(T,S\setminus T)}.\]

Each summand counts graphs with that particular two-colouring. A bipartite graph with \(k\) components has \(2^k\) colourings, including a factor two for an isolated vertex. Therefore \(C=L(A)\) counts connected graphs with a colouring, and the answer for nonempty \(U\) is \(C[U]/2\). The modulus \(998244353\) makes two a unit. Summing first and dividing all \(A[S]\) by two would be wrong for disconnected graphs.

For the official limit \(n\le17\), a practical baseline uses \(O(3^n)\) cut terms and the distinguished-component recurrence; ranked multiplication can accelerate the latter. Precompute induced-edge counts \(e(S)\) so crossing counts are \(e(S)-e(T)-e(S\setminus T)\). The partition coefficient is maintained exactly throughout, rather than deduplicating colourings afterward.

Problem-solving checklist

  • Must the two objects use disjoint labels, or is overlap allowed?
  • What does the empty-set coefficient mean?
  • Are components uniquely determined and weights multiplicative?
  • Are you counting uncoloured objects, coloured objects, or objects with an extra mark?
  • Are divisions legal, or should you use the distinguished-component recurrence?
  • Have intermediate product ranks survived until Möbius inversion?

Exercises with hints and solutions

  1. Partition check. Give weight two to a singleton and weight three to a pair. What is the partition exponential on two labels?

    Hint: list partitions. Solution: the singleton partition contributes \(2\cdot2=4\) and the one-block partition contributes 3, so the answer is 7.

  2. Characteristic trap. Over \(\mathbb F_2\), let \(g=x_0+x_1\). Why is “compute \(g^2/2\)” invalid, and what is the partition exponential’s two-label coefficient?

    Hint: ordered and unordered partitions are different computations. Solution: \(g^2=2x_0x_1=0\), and two has no inverse. The unique partition into two singleton blocks still has weight one, so the division-free answer is one.

  3. Connected extraction. How many connected simple graphs exist on three labelled vertices?

    Hint: subtract component partitions from eight. Solution: one empty graph and three single-edge graphs are disconnected, giving four. This also checks the earlier recurrence.

  4. Coloured failure. For a two-vertex graph with one possible edge, compute \(A\) and \(C\) for the coloured bipartite model.

    Hint: the empty graph has four colourings. Solution: \(A[U]=4+2=6\), each singleton has \(A=2\), and \(C[U]=6-2\cdot2=2\). Dividing \(C[U]\) by two yields the unique connected graph. Dividing \(A[U]\) by two instead yields three, not the two uncoloured graphs.

  5. Projection model. If \(g\) has coefficient one on singletons and zero elsewhere, and \(w\) selects the full universe, determine every \(b_k\).

    Hint: count ordered singleton blocks. Solution: only \(k=n\) can cover \(U\); its coefficient is \(n!\). Hence \(b_n=n!\) and the other \(b_k\) vanish, with the arithmetic interpreted in the coefficient ring.

For a sustained modelling exercise, attempt ARC105 F before reading the reduction above. Chapter 20 supplies a synthesis check on the complete four-vertex graph.

Summary and reference

Operation Assumptions Derived bound
Subset convolution Ring addition, subtraction, multiplication \(O(n^22^n)\)
Partition exponential / connected extraction Empty coefficients zero / one \(O(n^22^n)\) via label split
Multiplicative inverse Constant coefficient a unit \(O(n^22^n)\)
General composition / projection baseline Inner constant zero; degrees at most \(n\) \(O(n^32^n)\)

The rank-overlap invariant and multiplication bound originate in Björklund, Husfeldt, Kaski and Koivisto, Fourier meets Möbius: fast subset convolution. The recurrences above derive their own exp/log contracts; they do not require treating a general composition algorithm as a black box.
