12 — Discrete Sums and Diophantine Tools

This chapter collects methods that turn an apparently huge integer range into a small algebraic object: a polynomial, a Euclidean recursion, or a continued fraction. They are especially valuable in ICPC because the statement rarely names the method.

1. Polynomial interpolation

A polynomial of degree at most \(d\) is determined by \(d+1\) distinct values. Lagrange interpolation gives

\[P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d} y_i\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.\]

At arbitrary points this costs \(O(d^{2})\) for one query. When \(x_i=i\), prefix/suffix products and factorials reduce one query to \(O(d)\) after \(O(d)\) preprocessing:

\[\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-j}{i-j} =\frac{\prod_{j<i}(x-j)\prod_{j>i}(x-j)}{i!(-1)^{d-i}(d-i)!}.\]

Preconditions are not cosmetic: sample points must be distinct in the field, hence \(d < \bmod\) for \(0..d\); denominators must be invertible. For many arbitrary queries use a product tree and multipoint evaluation.

2. Finite differences and polynomial sums

Define \(\Delta P(x)=P(x+1)-P(x)\). Each difference lowers degree by one, and

\[P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d} \Delta^k P(0){x\choose k}.\]

This Newton basis is ideal when values are consecutive or arguments shift repeatedly. It also proves that if \(P\) has degree \(d\), then

\[S(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}P(i)\]

has degree \(d+1\), because \(\Delta S=P\). Therefore evaluate \(S(0..d+1)\) directly and interpolate at huge \(n\).

Recognition signal: fixed exponent/degree, enormous upper bound, modular answer. Do not interpolate if the degree depends on \(n\) or if sample denominators vanish modulo the chosen prime.

3. Floor sum via Euclidean descent

Define

\[F(n,m,a,b)=\sum_{0\le i<n}\left\lfloor\frac{ai+b}{m}\right\rfloor,\]

with nonnegative inputs and \(m>0\). First strip quotient parts:

\[a=q_am+a',\quad b=q_bm+b'.\]

They contribute \(q_a n(n-1)/2+q_bn\). Now \(a,b<m\). Let \(y=a n+b\). If \(y<m\), stop. Counting lattice points below the line and swapping axes yields the smaller instance

\[F(n,m,a,b)=F(\lfloor y/m\rfloor,a,m,y\bmod m)\]

after the standard ACL parameter update. This is the Euclidean algorithm geometrically, so there are \(O(\log m)\) iterations.

Use __int128 when products can exceed 64 bits. For negative \(a\) or \(b\), first use mathematical floor division to normalize them; C++ truncating division is not floor division.

Applications include counting lattice points, sums of residues, Beatty sequences, and recursively counting points constrained by rational lines.

4. Continued fractions

Euclid’s quotients form the simple continued fraction

\[\frac pq=[a_0;a_1,\ldots,a_k].\]

Convergents satisfy

\[P_i=a_iP_{i-1}+P_{i-2},\qquad Q_i=a_iQ_{i-1}+Q_{i-2},\]

starting with \((P[-2],P[-1])=(0,1)\) and \((Q[-2],Q[-1])=(1,0)\). They are best approximations: if a denominator bound lies between convergent denominators, a convergent or semiconvergent is optimal. Adjacent convergents obey \(P_iQ_{i-1}-P_{i-1}Q_i=\pm1\), a built-in overflow/error check.

Use continued fractions for best rational approximations, modular inequalities, and Pell equations. Compare rationals with cross multiplication in __int128, never floating point.

5. Pell equations

For nonsquare \(D>0\), Pell’s equation is

\[x^{2}-Dy^{2}=1.\]

The continued fraction of \(\sqrt D\) is periodic. The first convergent satisfying the equation gives the fundamental solution \((x_1,y_1)\). Every positive solution follows from

\[(x_k+y_k\sqrt D)=(x_1+y_1\sqrt D)^{k},\]

or the recurrence

\[x'=x_1x+Dy_1y,\qquad y'=x_1y+y_1x.\]

For \(x^{2}-Dy^{2}=N\), existence and seed generation are subtler; do not assume the \(N=1\) theorem transfers unchanged. Contest tasks often bound the search enough to find seeds and then advance each orbit by the fundamental unit.

Values grow exponentially. Use big integers or stop with division-based overflow checks before multiplication.

6. Linear Diophantine equations and CRT viewpoint

The equation \(ax+by=c\) has an integer solution iff \(g=\gcd(a,b)\) divides \(c\). Extended Euclid finds \(u,v\) with \(au+bv=g\); scale by \(c/g\). All solutions are

\[x=x_0+k\frac bg,\qquad y=y_0-k\frac ag.\]

Intersect this parameterization with sign or interval constraints using correct floor/ceiling division. A congruence \(ax\equiv c\pmod m\) is the same equation \(ax+my=c\); after dividing by \(\gcd(a,m)\), invert in the reduced modulus.

For nonlinear-looking determinant constraints such as \(ad-bc=k\), fixing two variables often reduces the rest to Bézout. For quadratic forms, factorization, modular obstructions, descent, or Pell reduction may apply.

7. Stern–Brocot and modular extrema

The Stern–Brocot tree contains every positive reduced rational exactly once. Left/right runs are the continued-fraction coefficients, so whole runs can be jumped rather than traversed one edge at a time. Under a denominator or value bound, compare the two boundary fractions by __int128 cross multiplication and take the largest legal run.

Functions such as \(min_{0\le i<n} (a*i+b) \bmod m\) use the same Euclidean geometry as floor_sum: quotient away full multiples, then swap axes after the first wrap. Keep half-open interval conventions consistent.

8. Bernoulli numbers and Faulhaber sums

Bernoulli numbers are defined by

\[\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}B_k\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.\]

They give

\[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i^{d}=\frac1{d+1}\sum_{j=0}^{d}{d+1\choose j}B_j n^{d+1-j}.\]

This computes all power-sum formulas through one FPS inversion and factorial scaling. Check the \(B_1\) sign convention and require all denominators through \(d+1\) to be invertible. For one degree and one query, consecutive-point interpolation is often simpler; Bernoulli preprocessing wins for many degrees.

9. Pitfalls

  • C++ % and / on negatives do not implement Euclidean remainder/floor division.
  • Lagrange denominators vanish if the interpolation degree reaches the modulus.
  • Inferring “it is polynomial” from samples is not a proof; establish a degree bound.
  • \(a*n+b\) can overflow before the floor sum shrinks it.
  • Pell recurrences need all seed orbits for generalized equations.
  • Extended-GCD coefficients may overflow even if the final normalized answer fits.

10. Practice, in order

  1. Advanced — The Sum of the k-th Powers, CF 622F (English, 2600). Consecutive-point interpolation. Official tutorial.
    Hints
    1. The prefix sum is a polynomial in \(n\) of degree \(k+1\).
    2. Evaluate it at \(0..k+1\) directly.
    3. Use prefix/suffix Lagrange products for one huge query.
  2. Advanced — S = 1, AtCoder ABC340 F (English, 500). Bézout and determinant construction. Official editorial.
    Hints
    1. Rewrite the required area as an equation of the form \(Xb-Ya=±2\).
    2. Check the gcd divisibility condition.
    3. Scale extended-Euclid coefficients and map signs carefully.
  3. Mastery — Cowmpany Cowmpensation, CF 995F (English, 2700). Tree DP whose answer is polynomial. Official tutorial.
    Hints
    1. First solve for small color bounds with a tree DP.
    2. Prove each subtree’s count is a polynomial and bound its degree by subtree size.
    3. Evaluate enough consecutive points, then interpolate at \(D\).
  4. Mastery — Sum of Min of Mod of Linear, AtCoder ARC182 E (English, 800). Floor sums inside a modular-minimum decomposition. Official English editorial.
    Hints
    1. Sort and deduplicate the offsets; compare every candidate with the smallest offset.
    2. The active minimum changes exactly when \((Ck+A_i) \bmod M\) wraps.
    3. Counts of wraps and sums of one linear residue sequence are differences of floor_sum calls.
  5. Capstone — Diophantine Equation, Project Euler 66 (English). Pell equations and unbounded integer growth. Continued-fraction/Pell tutorial.
    Hints
    1. For each nonsquare \(D\), expand \(\sqrt{D}\) periodically using integer triples \((m,d,a)\).
    2. Generate convergents until \(x²-Dy²=1\).
    3. Compare the minimal \(x\) values with arbitrary-precision integers; they grow much faster than \(D\).
