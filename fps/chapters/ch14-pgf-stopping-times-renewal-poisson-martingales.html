14 — Probability Generating Functions, Renewal Stopping Times, Poissonization & Martingales

Part IV · Probabilistic Generating Functions, Lattice Walks & Multivariate Series

In Chapters 01 through 13, our generating functions encoded deterministic enumeration sequences: \([x^n]A(x)\) counted unlabeled structures of size \(n\), and \(n!\,[x^n]\widehat{A}(x)\) counted labeled structures on \(n\) vertices. In this chapter we open Part IV (Probabilistic Generating Functions, Lattice Walks & Multivariate Series) by turning the formal power series ring \(K[[z]]\) into an algebraic engine for stochastic processes and random stopping times.

When a random process evolves step by step—tossing biased coins until one of \(m\) competing patterns appears, drawing coupons with multiplicity thresholds \(B_i\), flipping random switches on a hypercube until a target state is reached, or redistributing tokens among \(n\) piles until one player holds everything—computing the expected stopping time \(\mathbb{E}[T]\), its variance \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\), or the winning probabilities \(P_i\) via state-space Gaussian elimination often requires \(O(V^3)\) time over an exponentially large state space. Probabilistic Generating Functions (PGFs) collapse these infinite-horizon Markov chains into compact polynomial and exponential equations where \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\) are extracted by algebraic behavior at \(z = 1\).

14.1 PGF Fundamentals, Tail-Sum Conservation & Factorial Moments

Concrete Combinatorial Setup: Counting by When the Process Stops

Suppose a randomized algorithm or stochastic game runs in discrete steps \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) until a target stopping condition \(\mathcal{E}\) occurs for the first time. Let \(T \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \cup \{\infty\}\) denote the stopping time—the exact step index at which the process halts. At every step \(n \ge 0\), the probability mass of all possible trajectories partitions cleanly into two disjoint sets of histories:

  1. Terminating histories (\(T = n\)): Trajectories that were still active after step \(n - 1\) and satisfy the stopping condition \(\mathcal{E}\) for the very first time at step \(n\). We denote their probability by \[f_n = \mathbb{P}(T = n) \qquad (n \ge 0).\]
  2. Surviving (active) histories (\(T > n\)): Trajectories that have not yet triggered \(\mathcal{E}\) at any of steps \(0, 1, \dots, n\), and therefore continue to step \(n + 1\). We denote their probability by \[g_n = \mathbb{P}(T > n) \qquad (n \ge 0).\]

We package these two sequences into two ordinary generating functions in \(\mathbb{Q}[[z]]\) (or \(\mathbb{F}_p[[z]]\) when all step probabilities have denominators coprime to \(p\)):

  • The Probabilistic Generating Function (PGF) of the stopping time \(T\): \[F(z) = \mathbb{E}[z^T] = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(T = n) z^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n z^n.\]
  • The Survival (or Tail) Generating Function of the active trajectories: \[G(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(T > n) z^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} g_n z^n.\]

Whenever the process halts with probability \(1\) (\(\mathbb{P}(T < \infty) = 1\)), evaluating \(F(z)\) at \(z = 1\) sums the probabilities of all mutually exclusive halting steps: \[F(1) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(T = n) = \mathbb{P}(T < \infty) = 1.\]

Single-Step Derivation of the Stopping-Time Conservation Identity

Why do we introduce both \(F(z)\) and \(G(z)\) instead of working with \(F(z)\) alone? Because in combinatorial applications (such as pattern matching, coupon collection, and random walks), describing a surviving state in \(G(z)\) is often much simpler than describing a first-hit state in \(F(z)\), and the two series are locked together by a universal algebraic identity.

  • ** Diagnostic Signal**: Whenever a problem asks for the expected stopping time \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) or variance \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\) of a process whose “still running after \(n\) steps” configurations are easier to characterize than its “stopped at step \(n\) for the first time” configurations, introduce both \(F(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(T = n)z^n\) and \(G(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(T > n)z^n\).
  • ** Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad**: How are \(f_n\) and \(g_n\) related at step \(n\)? Every trajectory that is still alive after step \(n - 1\) (mass \(g_{n-1}\)) either stops at step \(n\) (mass \(f_n\)) or stays alive after step \(n\) (mass \(g_n\)). Translating \(g_{n-1} = f_n + g_n\) into generating functions will let us eliminate \(F(z)\) or \(G(z)\) at will.

Let us execute this derivation step by step:

  1. Base case at step \(n = 0\): Before any step is taken, the total probability mass is \(1\). At \(n = 0\), a trajectory either stops immediately at step \(0\) (probability \(f_0 = \mathbb{P}(T = 0)\)) or survives past step \(0\) (probability \(g_0 = \mathbb{P}(T > 0)\)): \[f_0 + g_0 = 1.\]
  2. Conservation of probability mass at step \(n \ge 1\): The event \(\{T > n - 1\}\) (surviving step \(n - 1\)) is the disjoint union of \(\{T = n\}\) (stopping at step \(n\)) and \(\{T > n\}\) (surviving step \(n\)). Therefore, for every integer \(n \ge 1\): \[f_n + g_n = g_{n-1} \qquad (n \ge 1).\]
  3. Summing over all \(n \ge 0\) with weight \(z^n\): Multiply the \(n = 0\) equation by \(z^0 = 1\), multiply each \(n \ge 1\) equation by \(z^n\), and add them over all \(n \ge 0\): \[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n z^n + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} g_n z^n = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} g_{n-1} z^n.\]
  4. Re-indexing the right-hand sum: Factor \(z\) out of the right-hand sum and substitute \(m = n - 1 \ge 0\): \[F(z) + G(z) = 1 + z \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} g_m z^m = 1 + z G(z).\]
  5. Collecting the \(G(z)\) terms: Subtract \(z G(z)\) from both sides to obtain the Stopping-Time Conservation Identity: \[\boxed{F(z) + (1 - z) G(z) = 1 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad G(z) = \frac{1 - F(z)}{1 - z}.}\]

Factorial Moments, Ordinary Moments & Variance Extraction

In the formal power series ring \(K[[z]]\), the formal derivative operator \(D_z = \frac{d}{dz}\) maps \(z^n \mapsto n z^{n-1}\). Applying \(D_z\) \(k\) times to \(F(z)\) pulls down the \(k\)-th falling factorial \(n^{\underline{k}} = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\): \[F^{(k)}(z) = \sum_{n=k}^{\infty} n^{\underline{k}} f_n z^{n-k}.\] When \(F(z)\) (and its derivatives) converge at \(z = 1\)—or when \(F(z)\) is a rational function whose pole at \(z = 1\) has been cancelled so \(z = 1\) is regular—evaluating \(F^{(k)}(z)\) at \(z = 1\) yields the \(k\)-th factorial moment of \(T\): \[F^{(k)}(1) = \sum_{n=k}^{\infty} n^{\underline{k}} \mathbb{P}(T = n) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[T^{\underline{k}}\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[T(T-1)\cdots(T-k+1)\right].\]

Now let us differentiate the conservation identity \(F(z) = 1 - (1 - z)G(z)\) \(k\) times with respect to \(z\) for \(k \ge 1\). Because \((1 - z)\) has degree \(1\), its first derivative is \(-1\) and all higher derivatives vanish. By the Leibniz product rule: \[F^{(k)}(z) = -(1 - z) G^{(k)}(z) + k G^{(k-1)}(z) \qquad (k \ge 1).\] Setting \(z = 1\) annihilates the \((1 - z) G^{(k)}(1)\) term and leaves a direct bridge between the derivatives of \(F\) and \(G\): \[\boxed{\mathbb{E}\!\left[T^{\underline{k}}\right] = F^{(k)}(1) = k G^{(k-1)}(1) \qquad (k \ge 1).}\]

Specializing to \(k = 1\) and \(k = 2\) gives the fundamental formulas of competitive programming probability:

  1. First Moment (Expected Stopping Time) & Tail-Sum Formula (\(k = 1\)): \[\mathbb{E}[T] = F'(1) = G(1) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(T > n).\] Notice what \(G(1) = \sum_{n \ge 0} g_n\) says combinatorially: every step \(n \ge 0\) in which the process is still alive (\(T > n\)) contributes \(+1\) to the total lifespan \(T\), confirming \(T = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbf{1}[T > n]\).
  2. Second Factorial Moment (\(k = 2\)): \[\mathbb{E}[T(T - 1)] = F''(1) = 2 G'(1) = 2 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \, \mathbb{P}(T > n).\]
  3. Variance in Terms of \(F(z)\) and \(G(z)\): Since \(T^2 = T(T - 1) + T\), linearity of expectation gives \(\mathbb{E}[T^2] = \mathbb{E}[T(T - 1)] + \mathbb{E}[T] = F''(1) + F'(1)\). Subtracting \(\mathbb{E}[T]^2 = F'(1)^2\) yields: \[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(T) = F''(1) + F'(1) - F'(1)^2 = 2 G'(1) + G(1) - G(1)^2.}\]
  4. Arbitrary \(k\)-th Ordinary Moments via the Euler Operator \(\vartheta_z = z D_z\) (Ch 06): Whereas \(D_z^k\) extracts falling factorials \(n^{\underline{k}}\), applying the Euler differential operator \(\vartheta_z = z \frac{d}{dz}\) (which maps \(z^n \mapsto n z^n\)) \(k\) times pulls down \(n^k\) directly: \[\left(\vartheta_z^k F\right)(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n^k f_n z^n \implies \mathbb{E}[T^k] = \left.\left(\vartheta_z^k F\right)(z)\right|_{z=1}.\] By the Stirling operator identity \(\vartheta_z^k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ j \end{matrix}\right\} z^j D_z^j\) established in Chapter 06, evaluating at \(z = 1\) converts factorial moments into ordinary moments: \[\mathbb{E}[T^k] = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ j \end{matrix}\right\} F^{(j)}(1) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ j \end{matrix}\right\} j \, G^{(j-1)}(1) \qquad (k \ge 1).\]
NoteBridge to Moment & Cumulant Generating Functions (\(z = e^t\))

Substituting \(z = e^t\) into the PGF \(F(z) = \mathbb{E}[z^T]\) converts the Euler operator \(\vartheta_z = z \frac{d}{dz}\) into ordinary differentiation \(\frac{d}{dt}\), producing the Moment Generating Function (MGF): \[M_T(t) = F(e^t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tT}] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}[T^k] \frac{t^k}{k!}.\] Because \(M_T(0) = F(1) = 1\), its formal logarithm \(K_T(t) = \ln F(e^t) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \kappa_k \frac{t^k}{k!}\) is well-defined in \(\mathbb{Q}[[t]]\) and is called the Cumulant Generating Function. Expanding \(\ln(1 + (M_T(t) - 1))\) up to \(t^2\) gives \[K_T(t) = \ln\!\left(1 + \mathbb{E}[T] t + \mathbb{E}[T^2] \frac{t^2}{2!} + O(t^3)\right) = \mathbb{E}[T] t + \left(\mathbb{E}[T^2] - \mathbb{E}[T]^2\right) \frac{t^2}{2!} + O(t^3),\] so the first cumulant is \(\kappa_1 = \mathbb{E}[T]\) and the second cumulant is \(\kappa_2 = \operatorname{Var}(T)\). Because \(\ln(F_1(e^t) F_2(e^t)) = \ln F_1(e^t) + \ln F_2(e^t)\), all cumulants \(\kappa_k\) are additive across independent random variables.

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace: Geometric Stopping Time & Small Coefficient Verification

Let us verify the entire §14.1 pipeline on a concrete process where every coefficient can be checked by hand. Suppose each step \(n \ge 1\) succeeds with probability \(p \in (0, 1)\) and fails with probability \(q = 1 - p\), and \(T \ge 1\) is the step of the first success.

  1. Coefficients for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\):
    • At \(n = 0\): \(f_0 = \mathbb{P}(T = 0) = 0\), and \(g_0 = \mathbb{P}(T > 0) = 1\) (since \(f_0 + g_0 = 1\)).
    • At \(n = 1\): \(f_1 = p\), and \(g_1 = q\) (check: \(f_1 + g_1 = p + q = 1 = g_0\)).
    • At \(n = 2\): \(f_2 = qp\), and \(g_2 = q^2\) (check: \(f_2 + g_2 = q(p + q) = q = g_1\)).
    • In general, for \(n \ge 1\), \(f_n = q^{n-1}p\) and \(g_n = q^n\).
  2. Closed-form generating functions: \[F(z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} p q^{n-1} z^n = \frac{pz}{1 - qz}, \qquad G(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} q^n z^n = \frac{1}{1 - qz}.\]
  3. Verifying conservation \(F(z) + (1 - z)G(z) = 1\): \[F(z) + (1 - z)G(z) = \frac{pz + (1 - z)}{1 - qz} = \frac{1 - (1 - p)z}{1 - qz} = \frac{1 - qz}{1 - qz} = 1.\]
  4. Extracting expectation and variance via \(G(z)\): Since \(1 - q = p\), evaluating \(G(z) = (1 - qz)^{-1}\) and \(G'(z) = q(1 - qz)^{-2}\) at \(z = 1\) gives: \[\mathbb{E}[T] = G(1) = \frac{1}{1 - q} = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad G'(1) = \frac{q}{(1 - q)^2} = \frac{q}{p^2},\] \[\operatorname{Var}(T) = 2G'(1) + G(1) - G(1)^2 = \frac{2q}{p^2} + \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{2(1 - p) + p - 1}{p^2} = \frac{1 - p}{p^2} = \frac{q}{p^2}.\] Notice that differentiating \(G(z) = (1 - qz)^{-1}\) once was noticeably cleaner than differentiating \(F(z) = pz(1 - qz)^{-1}\) twice by the quotient rule!

14.2 Formal Taylor Shift \(z = 1 + t \bmod t^3\) for \(0/0\) Pole Cancellation

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

In finite-state Markov chains, string automata, and renewal processes, solving a system of linear equations over \(\mathbb{Q}(z)\) (or simplifying a sum of partial fractions) expresses the stopping-time PGF as a rational function \[F(z) = \frac{P(z)}{Q(z)},\] where \(P(z), Q(z) \in K[z]\) are polynomials (or truncated series).

  • ** Diagnostic Signal: You have derived a rational PGF \(F(z) = \frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}\) and need \(\mathbb{E}[T] = F'(1)\) or \(\operatorname{Var}(T) = F''(1) + F'(1) - F'(1)^2\), but** substituting \(z = 1\) gives \(P(1) = 0\) and \(Q(1) = 0\) because an uncancelled factor \((1 - z)^v\) (\(v \ge 1\)) divides both numerator and denominator.
  • ** Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Why is \((1 - z)\) dividing both \(P(z)\) and \(Q(z)\)? For instance, in §14.3 we will see that unnormalized visit series \(H(z)\) have a simple pole at \(z = 1\); clearing denominators across \(m\) states introduces \((1 - z)^v\) into both \(P(z)\) and \(Q(z)\). If we try to compute \(F'(1)\) via the quotient rule \(F'(z) = \frac{P'(z)Q(z) - P(z)Q'(z)}{Q(z)^2}\), plugging in \(z = 1\) gives \(\frac{0}{0}\)! Worse, applying L’Hôpital’s rule to \(F''(z) = \frac{d^2}{dz^2}\left(\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}\right)\) when \(P(1) = Q(1) = 0\) requires differentiating up to order \(v + 2\). How do we cancel the root at \(z = 1\) algebraically** and extract \(F(1), F'(1), F''(1)\) in one stroke? Instead of differentiating in \(z\) and then substituting \(z = 1\), we substitute \(z = 1 + t\) first! In the local coordinate \(t = z - 1\), Taylor’s theorem says \[F(1 + t) = F(1) + F'(1) t + \frac{F''(1)}{2!} t^2 + O(t^3).\] Furthermore, a shared root at \(z = 1\) of multiplicity \(v\) becomes a shared monomial factor \(t^v\) in \(P(1 + t)\) and \(Q(1 + t)\), which we can strip by shifting indices before performing a 3-term formal power series division modulo \(t^3\)!
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: The \(0/0\) Quotient-Rule & L’Hôpital Trap at \(z = 1\)
  • The Trap: Attempting to evaluate \(F'(1) = \frac{P'(1)Q(1) - P(1)Q'(1)}{Q(1)^2}\) when \(Q(1) \equiv 0 \pmod p\). In modular arithmetic over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), inv(Q(1)) either returns \(0\) (via Fermat’s Little Theorem qpow(0, p - 2) == 0) or triggers a division-by-zero assertion, silently corrupting \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\). Even when \(v = 1\) is cancelled manually by L’Hôpital’s rule (\(F(1) = P'(1)/Q'(1)\)), computing \(F'(1)\) and \(F''(1)\) via repeated L’Hôpital quotients requires up to \(Q^{(3)}(1)\) and \(P^{(3)}(1)\) with intricate cross-terms.
  • The Remedy: Never apply the quotient rule or manual L’Hôpital differentiation to \(P(z)/Q(z)\) at \(z = 1\). Always expand \(P(1 + t)\) and \(Q(1 + t)\) in the jet ring \(K[t]/(t^{v+3})\), strip their shared \(t\)-adic valuation \(v = \operatorname{val}_t Q(1 + t)\) by shifting \(p_j = [t^{v+j}]P(1+t)\) and \(q_j = [t^{v+j}]Q(1+t)\) for \(j \in \{0, 1, 2\}\), and divide in \(K[t]/(t^3)\).

Single-Step Derivation of the 3-Term Formal Quotient Engine in \(K[t]/(t^3)\)

Let \(P(z), Q(z) \in K[z]\) be polynomials of degree at most \(N\) over a field \(K\) of characteristic \(p = 0\) or \(p > N\) (or \(p > v + 2\) if \(P(1+t), Q(1+t)\) are constructed directly in \(t\)).

  1. Step 1 (Taylor Shift to \(z = 1 + t\)): Using the polynomial Taylor Shift engine from Chapter 06 (or direct coefficient accumulation when \(P\) and \(Q\) have sparse/structured forms), write: \[\widetilde{P}(t) = P(1 + t) = \sum_{j=0}^{N} \tilde{p}_j t^j, \qquad \widetilde{Q}(t) = Q(1 + t) = \sum_{j=0}^{N} \tilde{q}_j t^j,\] where \(\tilde{p}_j = \frac{P^{(j)}(1)}{j!} = \sum_{n=j}^{N} \binom{n}{j} [z^n]P(z)\) and \(\tilde{q}_j = \frac{Q^{(j)}(1)}{j!} = \sum_{n=j}^{N} \binom{n}{j} [z^n]Q(z)\).

  2. Step 2 (Valuation Cancellation): Let \(v = \operatorname{val}_t(\widetilde{Q}(t)) = \min\{j \ge 0 : \tilde{q}_j \neq 0\}\) be the \(t\)-adic valuation of the denominator. Because \(F(z) = P(z)/Q(z)\) is regular at \(z = 1\) with \(F(1) = 1\), \(\widetilde{P}(t)\) must also vanish to order at least \(v\) at \(t = 0\) (\(\tilde{p}_0 = \dots = \tilde{p}_{v-1} = 0\)) and satisfy \(\tilde{p}_v = \tilde{q}_v \neq 0\). Dividing both \(\widetilde{P}(t)\) and \(\widetilde{Q}(t)\) by \(t^v\) in the Laurent field \(K((t))\) leaves two series with nonzero constant terms: \[p_0 + p_1 t + p_2 t^2 + O(t^3) = t^{-v}\widetilde{P}(t), \qquad q_0 + q_1 t + q_2 t^2 + O(t^3) = t^{-v}\widetilde{Q}(t),\] where \(p_r = \tilde{p}_{v+r}\) and \(q_r = \tilde{q}_{v+r}\) for \(r \in \{0, 1, 2\}\), and \(q_0 \in K^\times\) is invertible.

  3. Step 3 (3-Term Formal Division in \(K[t]/(t^3)\)): We seek \(F(1 + t) \equiv c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 \pmod{t^3}\) such that \[\left(q_0 + q_1 t + q_2 t^2\right)\left(c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2\right) \equiv p_0 + p_1 t + p_2 t^2 \pmod{t^3}.\] Comparing coefficients of \(t^0\), \(t^1\), and \(t^2\) one power at a time gives:

    • \([t^0]\) equation: \(q_0 c_0 = p_0 \implies c_0 = \frac{p_0}{q_0} = 1\) (confirming proper probability normalization \(F(1) = 1\)).
    • \([t^1]\) equation: \(q_0 c_1 + q_1 c_0 = p_1 \implies c_1 = \frac{p_1 - c_0 q_1}{q_0} = \frac{p_1 - q_1}{q_0}\).
    • \([t^2]\) equation: \(q_0 c_2 + q_1 c_1 + q_2 c_0 = p_2 \implies c_2 = \frac{p_2 - c_0 q_2 - c_1 q_1}{q_0} = \frac{p_2 - q_2}{q_0} - \frac{q_1(p_1 - q_1)}{q_0^2}\).

  4. Step 4 (Reading off \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\)): Since \(F(1 + t) = 1 + F'(1) t + \frac{F''(1)}{2} t^2 + O(t^3) = 1 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 + O(t^3)\), matching coefficients of \(t^1\) and \(t^2\) yields \(F'(1) = c_1\) and \(F''(1) = 2c_2\). Therefore: \[\boxed{\mathbb{E}[T] = c_1 = \frac{p_1 - q_1}{q_0}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(T) = 2c_2 + c_1 - c_1^2.}\]

Hand-Checkable Worked Example: Pole-Cancelled Rational PGF (\(N = 3\))

Suppose a 3-state Markov chain for coin tossing until HH (with an uneliminated absorbing-state factor \((1 - z)\) in both numerator and denominator) produces the unreduced rational PGF \[F(z) = \frac{P(z)}{Q(z)} = \frac{z^2(1 - z)}{(4 - 2z - z^2)(1 - z)} = \frac{z^2 - z^3}{4 - 6z + z^2 + z^3}.\] Notice that plugging in \(z = 1\) gives \(P(1) = 1 - 1 = 0\) and \(Q(1) = 4 - 6 + 1 + 1 = 0\), a \(0/0\) indeterminate form. Let us apply the 4-step Formal Jet Engine by hand:

  1. Substitute \(z = 1 + t\):
    • Numerator: \[\widetilde{P}(t) = (1 + t)^2 - (1 + t)^3 = (1 + 2t + t^2) - (1 + 3t + 3t^2 + t^3) = -t - 2t^2 - t^3.\]
    • Denominator: \[\widetilde{Q}(t) = 4 - 6(1 + t) + (1 + t)^2 + (1 + t)^3 = 4 - (6 + 6t) + (1 + 2t + t^2) + (1 + 3t + 3t^2 + t^3) = -t + 4t^2 + t^3.\]
  2. Cancel common valuation \(v = 1\): Both \(\widetilde{P}(t)\) and \(\widetilde{Q}(t)\) have valuation \(v = 1\). Dividing both by \(t^1\) gives \[p_0 + p_1 t + p_2 t^2 = -1 - 2t - t^2, \qquad q_0 + q_1 t + q_2 t^2 = -1 + 4t + t^2,\] so \((p_0, p_1, p_2) = (-1, -2, -1)\) and \((q_0, q_1, q_2) = (-1, 4, 1)\).
  3. Extract \(c_0, c_1, c_2\) in \(K[t]/(t^3)\):
    • \(c_0 = \frac{p_0}{q_0} = \frac{-1}{-1} = 1\) (confirming \(F(1) = 1\)).
    • \(c_1 = \frac{p_1 - q_1}{q_0} = \frac{-2 - 4}{-1} = 6 \implies \mathbb{E}[T] = 6\).
    • \(c_2 = \frac{p_2 - q_2 - c_1 q_1}{q_0} = \frac{-1 - 1 - 6(4)}{-1} = \frac{-26}{-1} = 26 \implies \mathbb{E}[T(T-1)] = 2c_2 = 52\).
  4. Compute variance: \[\operatorname{Var}(T) = 2c_2 + c_1 - c_1^2 = 52 + 6 - 36 = 22.\] In just three lines of polynomial arithmetic in \(K[t]/(t^3)\), we cancelled the \(0/0\) singularity and obtained both \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 6\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T) = 22\).
TipAlgorithm Contract: Formal Jet Moment Extractor (jet_moments_at_one)
  • Input & Exact Index Boundaries: Shifted Taylor coefficient arrays \(\tilde{p}[0 \dots N]\) and \(\tilde{q}[0 \dots N]\) representing \(P(1+t) = \sum_{j=0}^{N} \tilde{p}_j t^j\) and \(Q(1+t) = \sum_{j=0}^{N} \tilde{q}_j t^j\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p[t]\), with common zero-multiplicity \(v = \operatorname{val}_t(\widetilde{Q}) \in [0, N - 2]\).
  • Preconditions:
    1. \(v = \min\{j \in [0, N] : \tilde{q}[j] \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\}\) exists with \(v + 2 \le N\).
    2. \(\tilde{p}[j] \equiv 0 \pmod p\) for all \(0 \le j < v\), and \(\tilde{p}[v] \equiv \tilde{q}[v] \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\) (so \(\lim_{z \to 1} F(z) = 1\)).
    3. Characteristic condition: \(p > N\) if \(\tilde{p}, \tilde{q}\) are computed from \(P(z), Q(z)\) via \(O(N \log N)\) binomial Taylor shift (Ch 06), or \(p > 2\) if \(\tilde{p}, \tilde{q}\) are constructed directly in \(t\) modulo \(t^3\).
  • Postconditions (Output): Pair \((\mathbb{E}[T], \operatorname{Var}(T)) \in \mathbb{F}_p^2\) where \(\mathbb{E}[T] = c_1 = \frac{p_1 - q_1}{q_0}\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T) = 2c_2 + c_1 - c_1^2\) with \(c_2 = \frac{p_2 - q_2 - c_1 q_1}{q_0}\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space if full polynomial Taylor shift is needed; \(O(\log p)\) time and \(O(1)\) space once the 3-term reduced jets \((p_0, p_1, p_2)\) and \((q_0, q_1, q_2)\) are extracted.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: Shifting \(z = 1 + t\) is a ring automorphism of \(K[z]\) mapping the ideal \((z - 1)^k\) to \((t^k)\), and after stripping \(t^v\), the relation \(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2} c_j t^j\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2} q_j t^j\right) \equiv \sum_{j=0}^{2} p_j t^j \pmod{t^3}\) holds in \(K[t]/(t^3)\) with \(q_0 \in K^\times\).
// Extracts (E[T], Var(T)) mod p from shifted jets P(1+t), Q(1+t) after stripping t^v
pair<Mint, Mint> jet_moments_from_reduced(Mint p0, Mint p1, Mint p2,
                                          Mint q0, Mint q1, Mint q2) {
    assert(q0 != 0 && p0 == q0); // Precondition: q0 in K^x and F(1) = p0/q0 = 1
    Mint inv_q0 = q0.inv();
    Mint c1 = (p1 - q1) * inv_q0;               // E[T] = F'(1)
    Mint c2 = (p2 - q2 - c1 * q1) * inv_q0;     // E[T(T-1)] / 2 = F''(1) / 2
    Mint var_T = 2 * c2 + c1 - c1 * c1;         // Var(T) = F''(1) + F'(1) - F'(1)^2
    return {c1, var_T};
}

14.3 Renewal First-Passage Deconvolution (\(F = H_{0 \to E} / H_{E \to E}\)) & Continuous-Time Poissonization

Part A: First-Passage Renewal Deconvolution & The \((1-z)\) Pole Cancellation Trick

In many random walk and toggle problems—such as flipping switches on an \(n\)-dimensional hypercube ([ZJOI2019] 开关), flipping cells in a grid (ARC 136 F), or moving 3 particles on \(\mathbb{Z}\) until they meet (ABC 289 Ex)—computing the probability \(f_n = \mathbb{P}(T_{u \to v} = n)\) that the process reaches a target state \(v\) from initial state \(u\) at step \(n\) for the first time seems to require tracking the entire history to forbid earlier visits at steps \(1, \dots, n - 1\).

  • ** Diagnostic Signal: It is easy to compute the unconstrained visit probability** \(h_{u \to v, n} = \mathbb{P}(X_n = v \mid X_0 = u)\) of being at state \(v\) at step \(n\) (regardless of whether \(v\) was visited earlier)—either via coordinate-wise EGF products, Walsh–Hadamard / eigenvalues, or binomial convolutions—and the process is Markovian (time-homogeneous) upon reaching \(v\).
  • ** Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: How are unconstrained visits to \(v\) related to the first** visit to \(v\)? Every walk that starts at \(u\) and stands on \(v\) at step \(n\) must have visited \(v\) for the very first time at some uniquely determined step \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}\). After reaching \(v\) at step \(k\), the remaining \(n - k\) steps form an unconstrained walk from \(v\) back to \(v\) of length \(n - k\)! Because a convolution in \(n\) becomes a product of OGFs, this partitions \(H_{u \to v}(z)\) into \(F_{u \to v}(z) \cdot H_{v \to v}(z)\).

Let us write out this Renewal First-Passage Decomposition step by step:

  1. Define the three OGFs:
    • Unconstrained visit OGF from \(u\) to \(v\): \(H_{u \to v}(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(X_n = v \mid X_0 = u) z^n\).
    • Unconstrained return OGF from \(v\) to \(v\): \(H_{v \to v}(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(X_n = v \mid X_0 = v) z^n\) (note that \([z^0]H_{v \to v}(z) = 1\)).
    • First-passage PGF from \(u\) to \(v\): \(F_{u \to v}(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(T_{u \to v} = n) z^n\).
  2. Partition the event \(\{X_n = v\}\) by the first hitting step \(T_{u \to v} = k\): Since \(\{T_{u \to v} = 0\}, \{T_{u \to v} = 1\}, \dots, \{T_{u \to v} = n\}\) partition the event \(\{X_n = v\}\), the Markov property at step \(k\) gives: \[\mathbb{P}(X_n = v \mid X_0 = u) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \mathbb{P}(T_{u \to v} = k) \cdot \mathbb{P}(X_n = v \mid X_k = v) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \mathbb{P}(T_{u \to v} = k) \cdot \mathbb{P}(X_{n-k} = v \mid X_0 = v).\]
  3. Translate the discrete convolution into \(K[[z]]\): Multiplying by \(z^n\) and summing over \(n \ge 0\) recognizes the right-hand side as the Cauchy product of \(F_{u \to v}(z)\) and \(H_{v \to v}(z)\): \[H_{u \to v}(z) = F_{u \to v}(z) \cdot H_{v \to v}(z).\]
  4. Invert \(H_{v \to v}(z)\): Because \([z^0]H_{v \to v}(z) = \mathbb{P}(X_0 = v \mid X_0 = v) = 1 \in K^\times\) is always a unit in \(K[[z]]\), \(H_{v \to v}(z)\) is formally invertible (Ch 01), giving the Renewal First-Passage Formula: \[\boxed{F_{u \to v}(z) = \frac{H_{u \to v}(z)}{H_{v \to v}(z)}.}\]
WarningFailure Modes & Remedies: Renewal Deconvolution Traps
  1. Trap 1 (Confusing Any-Visit \(H_{u \to v}(z)\) with First-Passage \(F_{u \to v}(z)\) & Evaluating \(H_{v \to v}(1)\) Directly):
    • What Goes Wrong: Computing the unconstrained visit OGF \(H_{u \to v}(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(X_n = v \mid X_0 = u) z^n\) via FWT / EGF and attempting to evaluate \(H'_{u \to v}(1)\) directly, or attempting to divide \(H'_{u \to v}(1) / H_{v \to v}(1)\) without clearing the \((1 - z)^{-1}\) pole.
    • Why It Fails: In a recurrent Markov chain without absorption at \(v\), the target state \(v\) is visited infinitely often! Both \(H_{u \to v}(z)\) and \(H_{v \to v}(z)\) have a simple pole \(\frac{\pi_v}{1 - z}\) at \(z = 1\) (so \(H_{u \to v}(1) = H_{v \to v}(1) = \infty\), or a division-by-zero error \(\frac{1}{1 - 1} \bmod p\)). Furthermore, \(H_{u \to v}(z)\) overcounts trajectories by every subsequent return to \(v\).
    • Remedy: First divide out returns via \(F_{u \to v}(z) = \frac{H_{u \to v}(z)}{H_{v \to v}(z)}\), and then multiply both numerator and denominator by \((1 - z)\) to define the pole-cancelled series \(\widetilde{H}_{u \to v}(z) = (1 - z)H_{u \to v}(z)\) and \(\widetilde{H}_{v \to v}(z) = (1 - z)H_{v \to v}(z)\) before evaluating at \(z = 1 + t \bmod t^3\).
  2. Trap 2 (Asymmetric Multi-Target Sets Break Single-Series Renewal Deconvolution):
    • What Goes Wrong: Suppose the stopping condition is reaching any state in a target subset \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq V\) with \(|\mathcal{T}| \ge 2\). Writing \(H_{u \to \mathcal{T}}(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(X_n \in \mathcal{T} \mid X_0 = u)z^n\) and dividing by \(H_{\mathcal{T} \to \mathcal{T}}(z)\) fails if different states \(w, w' \in \mathcal{T}\) have different future return series \(H_{w \to \mathcal{T}}(z) \neq H_{w' \to \mathcal{T}}(z)\): \[H_{u \to \mathcal{T}}(z) = \sum_{w \in \mathcal{T}} F_{u \to w}^{\text{first in }\mathcal{T}}(z) \, H_{w \to \mathcal{T}}(z),\] which does not factor into \(F_{u \to \mathcal{T}}(z) \cdot H_{\mathcal{T} \to \mathcal{T}}(z)\)!
    • Remedy: You may apply scalar division \(F_{u \to \mathcal{T}}(z) = H_{u \to \mathcal{T}}(z) / H_{\text{return}}(z)\) if and only if Target Return Symmetry holds: there is a single series \(H_{\text{return}}(z)\) such that \(H_{w \to \mathcal{T}}(z) = H_{\text{return}}(z)\) for every \(w \in \mathcal{T}\) (for example, by translation invariance in ABC 289 Ex or row/column permutation symmetry in ARC 136 F). When \(\mathcal{T}\) is asymmetric and small (\(|\mathcal{T}| = m \le 300\)), set up an \(m \times m\) matrix renewal system over \(w \in \mathcal{T}\) instead (as in Penney’s Game, §14.4).

The EGF-to-OGF Borel Transform Bridge & \((1-z)\) Pole Cancellation

How do we apply \(F_{u \to v}(z) = H_{u \to v}(z) / H_{v \to v}(z)\) when the walk length is unbounded and we want \(\mathbb{E}[T_{u \to v}] = F'_{u \to v}(1)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T_{u \to v})\)?

In independent-coordinate toggle problems (such as flipping \(n\) switches where switch \(i\) is flipped at each step with probability \(p_i\), \(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i = 1\)), the number of steps distributed across the \(n\) coordinates in an \(m\)-step walk is a labeled tuple (Ch 04), so the natural generating function that factors across coordinates \(i = 1, \dots, n\) is the Exponential Generating Function (EGF): \[\widehat{H}_{u \to v}(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(X_m = v \mid X_0 = u) \frac{x^m}{m!} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{e^{p_i x} + (-1)^{u_i \oplus v_i} e^{-p_i x}}{2}\right).\] Expanding this finite product (via a 1D knapsack DP over the discrete exponents) expresses both \(\widehat{H}_{u \to v}(x)\) and \(\widehat{H}_{v \to v}(x)\) as linear combinations of exponentials: \[\widehat{H}_{u \to v}(x) = \sum_{\lambda} c_{\lambda} e^{\lambda x}, \qquad \widehat{H}_{v \to v}(x) = \sum_{\lambda} d_{\lambda} e^{\lambda x},\] where the eigenvalues \(\lambda \in [-1, 1]\) are rational multiples of \(1/P\) (\(P = \sum A_i\)).

To pass from the EGF \(\widehat{H}(x)\) to the OGF \(H(z)\) needed by the renewal equation \(F(z) = H_{u \to v}(z) / H_{v \to v}(z)\), we apply the Borel–Laplace Transform \(\mathcal{B}: K[[x]] \to K[[z]]\), defined coefficient-wise by multiplying \(\frac{x^m}{m!}\) by \(m!\) (or analytically by \(\mathcal{B}[\widehat{H}](z) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} \widehat{H}(zt)\,dt\)): \[\mathcal{B}\!\left[e^{\lambda x}\right](z) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} m! \left[x^m\right] \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^k x^k}{k!}\right) z^m = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} (\lambda z)^m = \frac{1}{1 - \lambda z}.\] By linearity, applying \(\mathcal{B}\) term by term converts the exponential sums into partial-fraction rational OGFs: \[H_{u \to v}(z) = \sum_{\lambda} \frac{c_{\lambda}}{1 - \lambda z}, \qquad H_{v \to v}(z) = \sum_{\lambda} \frac{d_{\lambda}}{1 - \lambda z}.\]

Now look closely at what happens at \(z = 1\):

  • For an ergodic Markov chain (or up to parity), the unique maximum eigenvalue is \(\lambda = 1\), with coefficient \(c_1 = d_1 = \pi_v > 0\) equal to the stationary weight of state \(v\) (e.g., \(\pi_v = 2^{-n}\) on the \(n\)-cube).
  • Therefore, at \(z = 1\), the \(\lambda = 1\) summand \(\frac{\pi_v}{1 - z}\) diverges in both \(H_{u \to v}(z)\) and \(H_{v \to v}(z)\): every recurrent state is visited infinitely many times in an infinite walk, so \(\sum_{m \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(X_m = v) = \infty\)!

How do we remove this \(\infty / \infty\) singularity at \(z = 1\)? Multiply both numerator and denominator of \(F_{u \to v}(z) = \frac{H_{u \to v}(z)}{H_{v \to v}(z)}\) by \((1 - z)\)! Define the pole-cancelled visit series: \[\widetilde{H}_{u \to v}(z) = (1 - z) H_{u \to v}(z) = c_1 + (1 - z) \sum_{\lambda \neq 1} \frac{c_{\lambda}}{1 - \lambda z}, \qquad \widetilde{H}_{v \to v}(z) = (1 - z) H_{v \to v}(z) = d_1 + (1 - z) \sum_{\lambda \neq 1} \frac{d_{\lambda}}{1 - \lambda z}.\] Now every summand with \(\lambda \neq 1\) has a non-vanishing denominator \(1 - \lambda \neq 0\) at \(z = 1\)! Part B: Continuous-Time Poissonization, Truncated Exponential EGFs & Min–Max IE

Let us now turn to the second major PGF engine of §14.3: Generalized Coupon Collection with Multiplicity Thresholds (AGC 038 E — Gachapon, Luogu P4707 — 重返现世, UOJ 390 — 百鸽笼).

Suppose there are \(N\) items; at each discrete step, item \(i\) is drawn independently with probability \(p_i = A_i / S\) (\(\sum_{i=1}^N p_i = 1\)), and item \(i\) requires at least \(B_i \ge 1\) copies. Let \(T_i\) be the discrete step at which item \(i\) receives its \(B_i\)-th copy. We want to compute:

  • The expected time until all items meet their quota: \(T_{\text{all}} = \max_{1 \le i \le N} T_i\), or

  • The expected time until at least \(k\) items meet their quota: \(T_{(k)} = \operatorname{kthmin}_{1 \le i \le N} T_i = \operatorname{Kthmax}_{1 \le i \le N} T_i\) where \(K = N - k + 1\).

  • ** Diagnostic Signal: The stopping condition is a conjunction** (“every item \(i\) has \(\ge B_i\) copies”, i.e. \(\max_i T_i\)) or a \(k\)-out-of-\(N\) threshold, and the item probabilities \(p_i = A_i/S\) and quotas \(B_i\) have moderate total sums (\(\sum A_i \le 400\), \(\sum B_i \le 400\)).

  • ** Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad**: Why is \(\max_{i \in [N]} T_i\) hard while \(\min_{i \in T} T_i\) is easy?

    1. First, by Min–Max Inclusion–Exclusion (Ch 08), we convert any maximum (or \(K\)-th maximum) of stopping times into a linear combination of subset minimums \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{i \in T} T_i]\): \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{i \in [N]} T_i\right] = \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subseteq [N]} (-1)^{|T|-1} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{i \in T} T_i\right],\] and more generally for the \(K\)-th largest stopping time (\(K = N - k + 1\)): \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{Kthmax}_{i \in [N]} T_i\right] = \sum_{\substack{T \subseteq [N] \\ |T| \ge K}} (-1)^{|T|-K} \binom{|T|-1}{K-1} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{i \in T} T_i\right].\]
    2. Now fix a non-empty subset \(T \subseteq [N]\), and let \(T_{\min}(T) = \min_{i \in T} T_i\) be the first step at which some item \(i \in T\) reaches \(B_i\) copies. How do we compute \(\mathbb{E}[T_{\min}(T)]\)? There are two completely equivalent ways to derive the exact same EGF formula—the Discrete Conditioning View and the Continuous-Time Poissonization View—and seeing both makes the identity unforgettable:

View 1: Discrete Active-Subset Conditioning + Tail-Sum Identity

  1. Filter out steps outside \(T\): At each step, the drawn item falls into \(T\) with probability \[P_T = \sum_{i \in T} p_i = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{i \in T} A_i.\] Draws that land outside \(T\) (probability \(1 - P_T\)) do not change the counts of any item in \(T\). Since each hit inside \(T\) takes an expected \(\frac{1}{P_T}\) total steps (by Wald’s identity / geometric waiting time between hits inside \(T\)), if \(M_T\) is the number of hits inside \(T\) until some \(i \in T\) reaches \(B_i\) copies, then \[\mathbb{E}[T_{\min}(T)] = \frac{1}{P_T} \mathbb{E}[M_T] = \frac{1}{P_T} \sum_{K=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(M_T > K).\]
  2. Count surviving \(K\)-hit sequences inside \(T\) via Truncated Exponential EGF: Conditioned on landing inside \(T\), each step chooses item \(i \in T\) with conditional probability \(\frac{p_i}{P_T}\). The event \(\{M_T > K\}\) holds if and only if, after \(K\) hits inside \(T\), every item \(i \in T\) has appeared \(c_i < B_i\) times (with \(\sum_{i \in T} c_i = K\)). By the labeled tuple construction (Ch 04), summing the multinomial probability over all valid tuples \((c_i)_{i \in T}\) is the coefficient of \(\frac{x^K}{K!}\) in the product of truncated exponential EGFs: \[\mathbb{P}(M_T > K) = \sum_{\substack{\sum_{i \in T} c_i = K \\ 0 \le c_i < B_i}} \frac{K!}{\prod_{i \in T} c_i!} \prod_{i \in T} \left(\frac{p_i}{P_T}\right)^{c_i} = \frac{K!}{P_T^K} [x^K] \prod_{i \in T} \left(\sum_{c=0}^{B_i - 1} \frac{(p_i x)^c}{c!}\right).\]
  3. Multiply by \(\frac{1}{P_T}\) and sum over \(K \ge 0\): \[\boxed{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{i \in T} T_i\right] = \sum_{K=0}^{\sum_{i \in T}(B_i - 1)} K! \left(\frac{1}{P_T}\right)^{K+1} [x^K] \prod_{i \in T} \left(\sum_{c=0}^{B_i - 1} \frac{(p_i x)^c}{c!}\right).}\]

View 2: Continuous-Time Poissonization & Laplace Integral

Instead of drawing one item at each discrete tick \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), imagine that draws arrive according to a continuous-time Poisson process of total rate \(\lambda = 1\) (so the time between consecutive draws is \(\operatorname{Exp}(1)\) with mean \(1\)). By Wald’s identity, the expected continuous time \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) until the stopping condition holds equals the expected discrete number of draws \(\mathbb{E}[T]\)!

By the Poisson Thinning Theorem, splitting a rate-\(1\) Poisson process where each arrival is assigned to bin \(i\) with probability \(p_i\) produces \(N\) mutually independent Poisson processes of rates \(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_N\). At continuous time \(t \ge 0\), the number of items in bin \(i\) is \(N_i(t) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(p_i t)\) independently across all \(i\), so the probability that bin \(i\) has strictly fewer than \(B_i\) items at time \(t\) is: \[\mathbb{P}(N_i(t) < B_i) = e^{-p_i t} \sum_{c=0}^{B_i - 1} \frac{(p_i t)^c}{c!}.\] Because the bins are independent in continuous time, the probability that every bin \(i \in T\) has fewer than \(B_i\) items at time \(t\) is simply the product over \(i \in T\): \[\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\min}(T) > t) = \prod_{i \in T} \mathbb{P}(N_i(t) < B_i) = e^{-P_T t} \prod_{i \in T} \left(\sum_{c=0}^{B_i - 1} \frac{(p_i t)^c}{c!}\right).\] Integrating \(\mathbb{E}[\tau_{\min}(T)] = \int_0^\infty \mathbb{P}(\tau_{\min}(T) > t)\,dt\) and applying the Euler gamma/Laplace integral \[\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{t^K}{K!} e^{-P_T t} \, dt = \frac{1}{P_T^{K+1}}\] term-by-term to \([t^K] \prod_{i \in T} \sum_{c=0}^{B_i-1} \frac{(p_i t)^c}{c!}\) reproduces the exact same boxed formula in a single integral!

14.4 The Singing Kingdom KMP Border Theorem, Multi-Pattern Correlation Systems & Doob’s Optional Stopping Martingale

Part A: Single-Pattern Hitting Time (The Singing Kingdom) & KMP Border Polynomial

Suppose characters from an alphabet \(\Sigma\) are generated independently one by one, where letter \(c \in \Sigma\) is chosen at each step with probability \(p_c \in (0, 1)\) (\(\sum_{c \in \Sigma} p_c = 1\); in the uniform case on an alphabet of size \(\sigma = |\Sigma|\), \(p_c = \frac{1}{\sigma}\)). Given a target pattern string \(S = s_1 s_2 \dots s_L\) of length \(L\), the process halts at the exact step \(T\) when \(S\) appears as a contiguous substring (hence as the suffix of the generated string) for the first time. How do we compute \(\mathbb{E}[T]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\) in \(O(L)\) time?

  • ** Diagnostic Signal**: We are generating random characters until a target string \(S[1 \dots L]\) (or one of \(m\) target strings \(S_1, \dots, S_m\)) appears as a suffix for the first time.
  • ** Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Why is the Goulden–Jackson / KMP automaton transfer matrix not needed here? Let \(F(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(T = n)z^n\) be the stopping-time PGF and \(G(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(T > n)z^n\) be the survival GF of all trajectories \(w\) that have not yet** produced \(S\). What happens if we take a surviving trajectory \(w\) (which does not contain \(S\)) and forcibly append the entire pattern \(S = s_1 s_2 \dots s_L\) to the end of \(w\)? On the one hand, the appended string \(w \cdot S\) certainly ends with \(S\), so the stopping condition must be triggered somewhere during the appending of those \(L\) characters! On the other hand, because \(w\) itself was \(S\)-free, the first occurrence of \(S\) in \(w \cdot S\) must end at some position \(k \in \{1, 2, \dots, L\}\) inside the appended copy of \(S\). If it ends at the \(k\)-th appended character, then the last \(k\) characters of the stopped string are \(s_1 s_2 \dots s_k\) (a prefix of \(S\) of length \(k\)), while by definition of stopping they must also equal \(s_{L-k+1} \dots s_L\) (the suffix of \(S\) of length \(k\))! A length \(k \in \{1, \dots, L\}\) whose prefix \(S[1 \dots k]\) equals the suffix \(S[L-k+1 \dots L]\) is precisely a KMP border of \(S\)!

Let us formalize this Forced-Appending Bijection as a single-step derivation in \(K[[z]]\):

  1. Definition of the Border Set \(\operatorname{Borders}(S)\): Define the set of all non-empty border lengths of \(S\): \[\operatorname{Borders}(S) = \left\{ k \in \{1, 2, \dots, L\} \;\middle|\; S[1 \dots k] = S[L - k + 1 \dots L] \right\}.\] Note that \(k = L\) is always in \(\operatorname{Borders}(S)\). Using the standard 1-indexed KMP failure table \(\pi[1 \dots L]\) (computable in \(O(L)\) time), \(\operatorname{Borders}(S)\) is enumerated in decreasing order by iterating \(k = L, \pi[L], \pi[\pi[L]], \dots\) while \(k > 0\).

  2. Weight of Forcibly Appending \(S\) to \(G(z)\): Each surviving string \(w\) of length \(n\) has probability weight \(\mathbb{P}(w) z^n\) in \(G(z)\). Appending the specific characters \(s_1, s_2, \dots, s_L\) multiplies its weight by \(\prod_{t=1}^{L} (p_{s_t} z)\). Summing over all surviving strings \(w\) gives the total generating function of forcibly appended trajectories: \[\text{LHS}(z) = G(z) \prod_{t=1}^{L} \left(p_{s_t} z\right).\]

  3. Partitioning by the Exact First-Stop Offset \(k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)\): Every trajectory \(w \cdot S\) where \(w\) is \(S\)-free contains \(S\) for the first time ending at a unique index \(n + k\) with \(1 \le k \le L\). For this to happen at offset \(k\):

    • The prefix \(w \cdot s_1 \dots s_k\) is a valid halting trajectory (it stops for the first time at step \(n + k\)), which is generated by \(F(z)\).
    • The overlap condition \(S[1 \dots k] = S[L - k + 1 \dots L]\) requires \(k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)\). Conversely, for every halting trajectory \(u\) (which ends with \(S\)) and every \(k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)\), stripping the last \(k\) characters of \(u\) leaves an \(S\)-free prefix \(w\) such that \(w \cdot S\) hits \(S\) for the first time at \(u = w \cdot s_1 \dots s_k\), followed by the remaining \(L - k\) deterministic characters \(s_{k+1} \dots s_L\)! Because those remaining \(L - k\) characters \(s_{k+1} \dots s_L\) carry weight \(\prod_{t=k+1}^{L} (p_{s_t} z)\), summing over all \(k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)\) gives the exact bijection: \[\boxed{G(z) \prod_{t=1}^{L} \left(p_{s_t} z\right) = F(z) \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} \prod_{t=k+1}^{L} \left(p_{s_t} z\right).}\]

  4. Dividing by \(\prod_{t=1}^{L} (p_{s_t} z)\) to Isolate \(G(z)\): Notice how dividing both sides by \(\prod_{t=1}^{L} (p_{s_t} z) = \left(\prod_{t=1}^{k} p_{s_t} z\right)\left(\prod_{t=k+1}^{L} p_{s_t} z\right)\) cancels the suffix product \(\prod_{t=k+1}^{L} (p_{s_t} z)\) on the right-hand side and leaves the reciprocal of the prefix product \(\prod_{t=1}^{k} (p_{s_t} z)\): \[\boxed{G(z) = F(z) \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} z^{-k} \prod_{t=1}^{k} p_{s_t}^{-1}.}\]

  5.   6. Extracting Stopping-Time Variance \(\operatorname{Var}(T)\): Let \(W_k = \prod_{t=1}^k p_{s_t}^{-1}\) be the reciprocal prefix probability of border \(k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)\) (so \(W_k = \sigma^k\) in the uniform case), and let \(E = G(1) = \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} W_k\). Differentiating \(G(z) = F(z) \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} W_k z^{-k}\) at \(z = 1\) via the product rule gives: \[G'(1) = F'(1) \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} W_k + F(1) \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} (-k) W_k = E^2 - \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} k W_k.\] Substituting \(G(1) = E\) and \(2G'(1) = 2E^2 - 2\sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} k W_k\) into \(\operatorname{Var}(T) = 2G'(1) + G(1) - G(1)^2\) yields the String Hitting-Time Variance Formula: \[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(T) = \mathbb{E}[T]^2 + \mathbb{E}[T] - 2 \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} k \prod_{t=1}^{k} p_{s_t}^{-1}.}\]

WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: The Non-Uniform Prefix vs. Suffix Cancellation Trap
  • The Trap: When letter probabilities \(p_c\) are non-uniform, a classic error is to guess that a border of length \(k\) contributes the reciprocal probability of the length-\(k\) suffix \(\prod_{t=L-k+1}^{L} p_{s_t}^{-1}\), or that for a pair of strings \((S_i, S_j)\) with overlap \(S_i[L_i - k + 1 \dots L_i] = S_j[1 \dots k]\), it doesn’t matter whether we use \(S_i\)’s prefix or \(S_j\)’s prefix.
  • Concrete Counterexample: Take \(S = \texttt{ABA}\) (\(L = 3\)) with \(p_{\texttt{A}} = \frac{1}{3}\) and \(p_{\texttt{B}} = \frac{2}{3}\). Here \(\operatorname{Borders}(\texttt{ABA}) = \{1, 3\}\). For \(k = 1\), \(S[1\dots 1] = S[3\dots 3] = \texttt{A}\) matches, but what if we have two competing strings \(S_1 = \texttt{BA}\) and \(S_2 = \texttt{AB}\) where \(S_1\)’s suffix of length \(k=1\) (\(\texttt{A}\)) matches \(S_2\)’s prefix of length \(k=1\) (\(\texttt{A}\))? When forcibly appending \(S_2 = \texttt{AB}\) to \(G(z)\), we multiply \(G(z)\) by \(p_{\texttt{A}} p_{\texttt{B}} z^2\), and the remaining tail of \(S_2\) after the length-\(k\) overlap is \(S_2[k+1 \dots L_2] = \texttt{B}\) (weight \(p_{\texttt{B}} z\)). Dividing by \(\prod_{t=1}^{L_2} (p_{S_2[t]} z)\) cancels the tail of \(S_2\) and leaves \(\prod_{t=1}^{k} p_{S_2[t]}^{-1}\)—the reciprocal of the prefix of the appended string \(S_2\) (which equals the suffix of the stopping string \(S_1\), not the prefix of \(S_1\)!).
  • The Remedy: Always remember which factor cancels: appending \(S_j\) leaves the tail \(S_j[k+1 \dots L_j]\) on the RHS, so dividing by the full weight of \(S_j\) leaves \(\prod_{t=1}^{k} p_{S_j[t]}^{-1}\) (the reciprocal of the length-\(k\) prefix of \(S_j\), which is identical to the length-\(k\) suffix of \(S_i\)).

Part B: Multi-Pattern Competing Stopping Times (Penney’s Game & Guibas–Odlyzko Linear System)

Now suppose \(m\) players choose patterns \(S_1, S_2, \dots, S_m\) of lengths \(L_1, \dots, L_m\) (where no pattern \(S_i\) is a contiguous substring of any \(S_j\) for \(i \neq j\), as otherwise \(S_j\) could never win before \(S_i\)). Characters are generated at random until one of the \(m\) patterns appears as a suffix for the first time, halting the game and declaring that pattern the winner. How do we find the winning probability \(P_i = \mathbb{P}(S_i \text{ wins})\) for every \(i = 1, \dots, m\), as well as the expected game duration \(\mathbb{E}[T]\)?

  1. Decompose \(F(z)\) by the winning pattern: For each \(i \in \{1, \dots, m\}\), let \(F_i(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \mathbb{P}(T = n \text{ and } S_i \text{ wins}) z^n\) be the PGF of trajectories won by \(S_i\). Then the total stopping-time PGF is \(F(z) = \sum_{i=1}^m F_i(z)\), and evaluating at \(z = 1\) gives: \[P_i = F_i(1), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m} P_i = F(1) = 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = F'(1) = G(1).\]
  2. Define the Directed Overlap / Correlation Set \(\mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)\): For any ordered pair of patterns \((S_i, S_j)\), let \(\mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)\) be the set of non-empty overlap lengths \(k\) such that the suffix of \(S_i\) of length \(k\) equals the prefix of \(S_j\) of length \(k\): \[\mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j) = \left\{ k \in \{1, \dots, \min(L_i, L_j)\} \;\middle|\; S_i[L_i - k + 1 \dots L_i] = S_j[1 \dots k] \right\}.\]
  3. Forcibly Append \(S_j\) to \(G(z)\) for Each \(j = 1, \dots, m\): Take any surviving string \(w\) counted by \(G(z)\) (which contains none of \(S_1, \dots, S_m\)) and forcibly append \(S_j\). The game must halt at some first occurrence of some pattern \(S_i\) (\(1 \le i \le m\)) ending at offset \(k \in \mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)\) inside the appended copy of \(S_j\), followed by the remaining \(L_j - k\) characters \(S_j[k+1 \dots L_j]\): \[G(z) \prod_{t=1}^{L_j} \left(p_{S_j[t]} z\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} F_i(z) \sum_{k \in \mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)} \prod_{t=k+1}^{L_j} \left(p_{S_j[t]} z\right) \qquad (1 \le j \le m).\]
  4. Divide by \(\prod_{t=1}^{L_j} (p_{S_j[t]} z)\) and Evaluate at \(z = 1\): Setting \(z = 1\) produces \(m\) linear equations in the \(m + 1\) unknowns \(P_1, \dots, P_m\) and \(G(1) = \mathbb{E}[T]\), which together with \(\sum_{i=1}^m P_i = 1\) form a non-singular \((m + 1) \times (m + 1)\) linear system: \[\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{m} C_{i, j} P_i - G(1) = 0 \quad (1 \le j \le m), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m} P_i = 1,}\] where the Guibas–Odlyzko correlation coefficient \(C_{i, j}\) is \[C_{i, j} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)} \prod_{t=1}^{k} p_{S_j[t]}^{-1} \qquad \left(\text{or } C_{i, j} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)} \sigma^k \text{ in the uniform case}\right).\]

Hand-Checkable Worked Example: Penney’s Game (HHT vs. HTT on a Fair Coin, \(\sigma = 2\))

Suppose Player 1 chooses \(S_1 = \texttt{HHT}\) and Player 2 chooses \(S_2 = \texttt{HTT}\), and a fair coin (\(\sigma = 2\)) is tossed until one pattern appears. Despite both patterns having individual expected hitting time \(2^3 = 8\) when played alone, let us compute who wins when they compete head-to-head:

  1. Compute the \(2 \times 2\) correlation overlaps \(\mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)\):
    • \(\mathcal{O}(\texttt{HHT}, \texttt{HHT}) = \{3\} \implies C_{1, 1} = 2^3 = 8\).
    • \(\mathcal{O}(\texttt{HHT}, \texttt{HTT}) = \{2\}\) (since suffix \(\texttt{HT}\) of \(S_1\) matches prefix \(\texttt{HT}\) of \(S_2\)) \(\implies C_{1, 2} = 2^2 = 4\).
    • \(\mathcal{O}(\texttt{HTT}, \texttt{HHT}) = \emptyset\) (suffixes \(\texttt{T}, \texttt{TT}, \texttt{HTT}\) of \(S_2\) do not match prefixes \(\texttt{H}, \texttt{HH}, \texttt{HHT}\) of \(S_1\)) \(\implies C_{2, 1} = 0\).
    • \(\mathcal{O}(\texttt{HTT}, \texttt{HTT}) = \{3\} \implies C_{2, 2} = 2^3 = 8\).
  2. Write the \((2 + 1) \times (2 + 1)\) linear system: \[\begin{cases} 8 P_1 + 0 P_2 = G(1) & (j = 1: \text{append } \texttt{HHT}), \\ 4 P_1 + 8 P_2 = G(1) & (j = 2: \text{append } \texttt{HTT}), \\ P_1 + P_2 = 1. & \end{cases}\]
  3. Solve by hand: Comparing the first two equations gives \(8 P_1 = 4 P_1 + 8 P_2 \implies 4 P_1 = 8 P_2 \implies P_1 = 2 P_2\). Combined with \(P_1 + P_2 = 1\), we obtain: \[P_1 = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad P_2 = \frac{1}{3}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = G(1) = 8 P_1 = \frac{16}{3}.\] Even though both \(\texttt{HHT}\) and \(\texttt{HTT}\) have the exact same solitary hitting time \(8\), \(\texttt{HHT}\) beats \(\texttt{HTT}\) two-thirds of the time because any path heading toward \(\texttt{HTT}\) must pass through \(\texttt{HT}\) (\(C_{1,2} = 4\)), which \(\texttt{HHT}\) frequently intercepts first!

Part C: Two Martingale Bridges — Fair Casino Gamblers & Separable Potential Drift Duality

Why did the KMP border sum \(\sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} \prod_{t=1}^k p_{s_t}^{-1}\) and the Guibas–Odlyzko system \(\sum_{i=1}^m C_{i,j} P_i = G(1)\) take such clean linear forms at \(z = 1\)? And how do we compute expected stopping times for multi-pile token processes (like CF 1349D — Slime and Biscuits) where there are \(n \le 10^5\) piles and no simple 1D EGF factorization exists?

Both questions are answered by the Martingale Optional Stopping Engine, which forms an exact algebraic dual to PGF differentiation at \(z = 1\).

Bridge 1: Li’s Fair Casino Martingale & Doob’s Optional Stopping Theorem

A discrete-time stochastic process \((M_n)_{n \ge 0}\) adapted to the history \((X_1, \dots, X_n)\) is called a martingale if \(\mathbb{E}[|M_n|] < \infty\) and its conditional one-step drift is zero everywhere: \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[M_{n+1} - M_n \;\middle|\; X_1, \dots, X_n\right] = 0 \qquad (\forall n \ge 0).\] By Doob’s Optional Stopping Theorem, if \(T\) is a stopping time such that \(\mathbb{E}[T] < \infty\) and the one-step increments \(|M_{n+1} - M_n|\) are uniformly bounded on \(\{T > n\}\) (which always holds on a finite state space with exponentially decaying tail \(\mathbb{P}(T > n) = O(\rho^n)\), \(\rho < 1\)), then the expected value of \(M_T\) at the random stopping time \(T\) equals its initial value \(M_0\): \[\boxed{\mathbb{E}[M_T] = \mathbb{E}[M_0].}\]

Let us see how H. Li’s (1980) Fair Casino Construction derives the Singing Kingdom and Penney’s Game formulas in 30 seconds:

  1. The Casino Rules (Betting on Pattern \(S = s_1 \dots s_L\)): Before each step \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), a new gambler \(n\) walks into a fair casino with initial capital \(\$1\).

    • Gambler \(n\) bets their entire \(\$1\) that the \(n\)-th character will be \(s_1\). Because \(s_1\) occurs with probability \(p_{s_1}\), fair odds pay \(p_{s_1}^{-1}\) per dollar: if \(X_n = s_1\), Gambler \(n\) now holds \(\$p_{s_1}^{-1}\); otherwise Gambler \(n\) goes broke (\(\$0\)) and leaves.
    • If Gambler \(n\) survived step \(n\), then at step \(n + 1\) they bet their entire fortune \(\$p_{s_1}^{-1}\) that \(X_{n+1} = s_2\) at fair odds \(p_{s_2}^{-1}\) (growing to \(\$p_{s_1}^{-1} p_{s_2}^{-1}\) on success, or \(\$0\) on failure), and so on up to \(s_L\).

  2. Zero-Drift Martingale Invariant: Because every single bet is fair (\(\mathbb{E}[\text{payout}] = p_c \cdot p_c^{-1} + (1 - p_c) \cdot 0 = 1\)), the casino’s net profit is a martingale! Specifically, after \(n\) steps, let \(W_n\) be the total fortune held by all gamblers currently in the casino. Since \(n\) gamblers have entered with \(\$1\) each (total entry fees \(\$n\)), the net wealth process \[M_n = W_n - n \qquad (n \ge 0, \;\; M_0 = 0)\] is a martingale!

  3. Evaluating \(W_T\) at the Stopping Time \(T\): At the exact moment \(T\) when the generated string ends with \(S_i\) (say in Penney’s Game, where our gamblers bet on \(S_j\)), which gamblers are still alive and holding money in the casino? A gambler who entered \(k\) steps ago (for \(1 \le k \le L_j\)) has survived all \(k\) of their bets \(S_j[1], \dots, S_j[k]\) if and only if the last \(k\) characters of the stopped string \(S_i\) equal \(S_j[1 \dots k]\)—that is, \(k \in \mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)\)! And that gambler holds exactly \(\prod_{t=1}^k p_{S_j[t]}^{-1}\) dollars. Therefore, conditioned on \(S_i\) winning at step \(T\), the total casino payout at time \(T\) is deterministically \(C_{i, j} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{O}(S_i, S_j)} \prod_{t=1}^k p_{S_j[t]}^{-1}\). Applying Doob’s Optional Stopping Theorem \(\mathbb{E}[M_T] = \mathbb{E}[W_T - T] = 0\) immediately gives \[\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[W_T] = \sum_{i=1}^{m} P_i C_{i, j},\] reproducing the Guibas–Odlyzko system (and for \(m = 1\), \(\mathbb{E}[T] = C_{1, 1} = \sum_{k \in \operatorname{Borders}(S)} \prod_{t=1}^k p_{s_t}^{-1}\)) without a single derivative!

Bridge 2: Separable Martingale Potentials, Poisson Drift Duality & Tridiagonal Order Reduction

What if the process is not a string automaton, but a symmetric multi-pile game on an enormous state space \(\Omega\)—such as \(n\) players holding \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\) biscuits with \(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = S\) (CF 1349D — Slime and Biscuits), where at each step a random biscuit is transferred to another player until one player holds all \(S\) biscuits?

  • ** Diagnostic Signal: You have a symmetric Markov process on tuples \((a_1, \dots, a_n)\) with a conserved sum \(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = S\) (or active components merging/splitting symmetrically), stopping when a single coordinate reaches \(S\). The number of partition states is far too large for Gaussian elimination, and** the one-step transition probabilities of a single coordinate \(a_i = x\) depend only on \(x\), \(n\), and \(S\).
  • ** Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: By Doob’s Optional Stopping Theorem, if we can construct a potential function** \(\Phi: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) on the state space such that every non-terminal state \(X_t = (a_1, \dots, a_n)\) has constant expected one-step drift \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi(X_{t+1}) - \Phi(X_t) \;\middle|\; X_t\right] = -1,\] then \(M_t = \Phi(X_t) + t\) is a martingale! If moreover every terminal state \(X_T\) (a permutation of \((S, 0, \dots, 0)\)) has the same terminal potential \(\Phi_{\text{end}}\), then \(\mathbb{E}[M_T] = \mathbb{E}[M_0]\) immediately gives: \[\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_T) + T] = \Phi(X_0) \implies \boxed{\mathbb{E}[T] = \Phi(X_0) - \Phi_{\text{end}}.}\] How do we construct \(\Phi(a_1, \dots, a_n)\) without solving a system over all partitions of \(S\)? Exploit symmetry and additive separability: seek \(\Phi(a_1, \dots, a_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(a_i)\) for a single 1D function \(f: \{0, 1, \dots, S\} \to K\)! Then \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_{t+1}) - \Phi(X_t) \mid X_t] = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[f(a_i') - f(a_i) \mid a_i]\). How do we make a sum of \(n\) single-pile drifts equal \(-1\) for every tuple \((a_1, \dots, a_n)\) satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = S\)? Because \(\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{a_i}{S} = 1\) (and equivalently \(\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{S - a_i}{S(n - 1)} = 1\)) holds identically on the hyperplane \(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = S\), we can rewrite the right-hand side \(-1\) as \(-\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{S - a_i}{S(n - 1)}\) (or \(-\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{a_i}{S}\)) and equate the \(i\)-th summands term-by-term!

Let us execute this Separable Potential & First-Difference Reduction step by step:

  1. Single-Pile Drift Equation: Suppose a pile of size \(x \in \{0, 1, \dots, S - 1\}\) transitions in one step to \(x - 1\) with probability \(p^-(x)\), to \(x + 1\) with probability \(p^+(x)\), and stays at \(x\) otherwise. Requiring the single-pile drift to equal \(-w(x)\) (where \(\sum_{i=1}^n w(a_i) = 1\) whenever \(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = S\), e.g. \(w(x) = \frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)}\) or \(w(x) = \frac{x}{S} - c\)) gives the 1D tridiagonal balance equation: \[p^-(x)\big(f(x - 1) - f(x)\big) + p^+(x)\big(f(x + 1) - f(x)\big) = -w(x) \qquad (0 \le x < S).\]
  2. Order Reduction via First Differences \(g(x) = f(x + 1) - f(x)\): Notice that \(f\) appears only through its adjacent differences! Define the first-difference sequence \[g(x) = f(x + 1) - f(x) \qquad (0 \le x < S), \qquad \text{with } f(0) = 0 \text{ and } f(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{x-1} g(j).\] Substituting \(f(x + 1) - f(x) = g(x)\) and \(f(x - 1) - f(x) = -g(x - 1)\) collapses the second-order tridiagonal system for \(f\) into a first-order two-term recurrence for \(g\): \[\boxed{p^+(x) \, g(x) - p^-(x) \, g(x - 1) = -w(x) \qquad (0 \le x < S).}\] Since \(p^-(0) = 0\) (an empty pile cannot lose a token), the \(x = 0\) equation immediately determines \(g(0) = -\frac{w(0)}{p^+(0)}\), and each subsequent \(g(1), g(2), \dots, g(S - 1)\) is computed in \(O(1)\) time by a single forward sweep!
  3. Recovering \(\mathbb{E}[T]\): Since every terminal state has one pile of size \(S\) and \(n - 1\) piles of size \(0\), its terminal potential is \(\Phi_{\text{end}} = f(S) + (n - 1)f(0) = f(S)\). Thus \[\boxed{\mathbb{E}[T] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(a_i) - f(S) \quad (\text{for drift } -1), \qquad \text{or } f(S) - \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(a_i) \quad (\text{for drift } +1).}\]
NoteAlgorithm Contract: Separable Potential & Optional Stopping Extractor

  • Preconditions:
    1. \(p^-(0) = 0\) and \(p^+(x) \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\) for all \(0 \le x < S\) (holds whenever \(S < p\) and \(n - 1 < p\)).
    2. Partition-of-unity condition: \(\sum_{i=1}^n w(a_i) = 1\) for every configuration \((a_1, \dots, a_n)\) with \(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = S\).

  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(n + S)\) time (using linear-time modular inverses up to \(S\)) and \(O(S)\) auxiliary space.

NoteOperator Duality (Elegia’s Theorem): Why Renewal Pole Cancellation and Martingale Potentials Are Identical

Why can problems like [ZJOI2019] 开关, CF 1349D — Slime and Biscuits, and CF 1479E — School Clubs be solved both by the §14.3 PGF Renewal Quotient \(F_{u \to t}(z) = \widetilde{H}_{u \to t}(z) / \widetilde{H}_{t \to t}(z)\) and by the §14.4 Martingale Potential method?

Let \(P\) be the transition matrix of an ergodic Markov chain with stationary distribution \(\pi\). The matrix of unconstrained visit OGFs is the resolvent \(H(z) = \sum_{m \ge 0} z^m P^m = (I - zP)^{-1}\), and the pole-cancelled matrix \(\widetilde{H}(z) = (1 - z)H(z)\) satisfies \[(I - zP)\widetilde{H}(z) = (1 - z)I.\] Differentiating both sides with respect to \(z\) at \(z = 1\) (where \(\widetilde{H}_{v \to t}(1) = \pi_t\) for all \(v\)) gives \[(I - P)\widetilde{H}'(1) - P\widetilde{H}(1) = -I \implies (I - P)\!\left(-\widetilde{H}'(1)\right) = I - \mathbf{1}\pi^T.\] Looking at column \(t\) (corresponding to target state \(t\)), the vector \(\phi_u = -\widetilde{H}'_{u \to t}(1)\) satisfies the Poisson Drift Equation: \[(I - P)\phi = e_t - \pi_t \mathbf{1} \Longleftrightarrow \mathbb{E}[\phi(X_1) - \phi(X_0) \mid X_0 = u] = \pi_t - \mathbf{1}[u = t].\] Dividing \(\phi_u - \phi_t\) by \(\pi_t\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[T_{u \to t}] = \frac{\phi_u - \phi_t}{\pi_t} = \frac{\widetilde{H}'_{t \to t}(1) - \widetilde{H}'_{u \to t}(1)}{\pi_t}\)! Thus:

  • Renewal PGF Deconvolution (§14.3) solves the Poisson drift equation in the spectral / generating-function domain by diagonalizing \(P\) via EGF products and differentiating \(\widetilde{H}(z)\) at \(z = 1\).
  • The Martingale Potential Method (§14.4) solves the exact same Poisson drift equation in the time / state-space domain by exploiting symmetry \(\Phi = \sum_{i=1}^n f(a_i)\) and reducing the tridiagonal difference equation to \(g(x) = f(x+1) - f(x)\).

Stepping Stone 1 (#115) — Codeforces 1392H: ZS Shuffles Cards

  • Source: Codeforces Global Round 10, Problem H

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

A deck contains \(n\) numbered cards (\(1, 2, \dots, n\)) and \(m\) identical jokers (\(n + m\) cards in total), shuffled uniformly at random. You maintain a set \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\) of collected numbered cards, initially \(S = \emptyset\).

In each round (epoch), you draw cards one by one from the top of the deck without replacement:

  • If you draw a numbered card \(x \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), you add \(x\) to \(S\) (\(S \leftarrow S \cup \{x\}\)) and continue drawing from the deck.
  • If you draw a joker, the current round ends immediately: you take all cards drawn during this round, shuffle them back into the remaining deck to form a fresh uniformly random permutation of all \(n + m\) cards, and start the next round (keeping your set \(S\)).

At the moment a joker is drawn (ending the round), if \(S = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\), the game stops; otherwise all drawn cards are reshuffled back into the deck and the next round begins. Compute the expected total number of card draws (including jokers) until the game stops, modulo \(998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le n, m \le 2 \times 10^6\), time limit \(2.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(n + m)\) time and \(O(n + m)\) (or \(O(1)\) auxiliary) space.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n = 2, m = 1\))

With \(n = 2\) numbered cards and \(m = 1\) joker (\(3\) cards total):

  • Single-epoch length \(\mathbb{E}[L]\): In any permutation of \(2\) numbered cards and \(1\) joker, each of the \(2\) numbered cards precedes the lone joker with probability \(\frac{1}{m + 1} = \frac{1}{2}\). Including the joker itself (\(+1\) draw), the expected number of cards drawn in one round is \(\mathbb{E}[L] = 1 + \frac{2}{2} = 2\).
  • Number of rounds \(\mathbb{E}[R]\): In one round, the joker is at position \(1, 2,\) or \(3\) with probability \(1/3\) each, revealing \(0, 1,\) or \(2\) numbered cards. By Min–Max IE (or conditioning on \(|S|\)):
    • For a subset \(T \subseteq \{1, 2\}\) of size \(k = 1\), card \(x \in T\) is drawn in a round with probability \(\frac{1}{m+1} = \frac{1}{2}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{x \in T} R_x] = 2\).
    • For \(T = \{1, 2\}\) (\(k = 2\)), at least one of the \(2\) cards precedes the \(m = 1\) joker unless the joker is first among all \(k + m = 3\) cards (probability \(\frac{m}{k+m} = \frac{1}{3}\)), so a round hits \(T\) with probability \(\frac{k}{k+m} = \frac{2}{3}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{x \in \{1,2\}} R_x] = \frac{3}{2}\).
    • By Min–Max IE, \(\mathbb{E}[R] = \binom{2}{1}(2) - \binom{2}{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) = 4 - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{5}{2}\).
  • Total expected draws: By Wald’s Renewal Identity, \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[R] \cdot \mathbb{E}[L] = \frac{5}{2} \cdot 2 = 5 \equiv 5 \pmod{998244353}\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Notice that whenever a joker is drawn, all cards drawn in that round are reshuffled back into the deck, resetting the deck’s permutation while preserving your collected set \(S\). This divides the process into rounds (epochs). Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

By Wald’s Identity (since the event \(\{R \ge r\}\) depends only on rounds \(1, \dots, r-1\) and is independent of the fresh shuffle in round \(r\)), total expected draws factor as \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \mathbb{E}[R] \cdot \mathbb{E}[L]\):

  1. Expected cards per round \(\mathbb{E}[L]\): By linearity of expectation among the \(n\) numbered cards and \(m\) jokers, any fixed numbered card \(x\) appears before all \(m\) jokers with probability \(\frac{1}{m + 1}\). Adding \(+1\) for the joker that ends the round gives \(\mathbb{E}[L] = 1 + \frac{n}{m + 1} = \frac{n + m + 1}{m + 1}\).
  2. Expected number of rounds \(\mathbb{E}[R]\): Apply Min–Max Inclusion–Exclusion (Ch 08 / §14.3) over the \(n\) numbered cards! Let \(R_x\) be the round in which card \(x\) is drawn for the first time, so \(R = \max_{1 \le x \le n} R_x\). For a subset \(T \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\) of size \(k = |T|\), what is the probability \(p_k\) that a single round draws at least one card from \(T\) before the first joker?
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Among the \(k\) cards in \(T\) and the \(m\) jokers (\(k + m\) cards whose relative order is uniformly random), a round misses all of \(T\) if and only if the very first of these \(k + m\) cards is one of the \(m\) jokers (probability \(\frac{m}{k + m}\)). Therefore, a round hits \(T\) with probability \(p_k = 1 - \frac{m}{k + m} = \frac{k}{k + m}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{x \in T} R_x] = \frac{1}{p_k} = \frac{k + m}{k} = 1 + \frac{m}{k}\). Sum over all \(\binom{n}{k}\) subsets of size \(k\) (or use the identity \(\sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} \frac{1}{k} = H_n\)) to simplify \(\mathbb{E}[R]\) to a clean \(O(n)\) harmonic sum!

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Renewal Factorization into Rounds \(\mathbb{E}[R]\) and Epoch Length \(\mathbb{E}[L]\)

Although the number of cards \(L_r\) drawn in round \(r\) is correlated with how many new elements are added to \(S\) during round \(r\), notice that for every round \(r \ge 1\), the event \(\{R \ge r\}\) (meaning the game did not stop at the end of round \(r - 1\), so \(S \neq \{1, \dots, n\}\) before round \(r\) begins) is determined purely by rounds \(1, \dots, r - 1\) and is therefore independent of the fresh shuffle in round \(r\)! Consequently, writing total draws as \(T = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} L_r \cdot \mathbf{1}[R \ge r]\) and taking expectations gives Wald’s Identity: \[\mathbb{E}[T] = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}\!\left[L_r \cdot \mathbf{1}[R \ge r]\right] = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{E}[L_r] \, \mathbb{P}(R \ge r) = \mathbb{E}[L] \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}(R \ge r) = \mathbb{E}[L] \cdot \mathbb{E}[R].\]

2. Expected Length of a Single Round \(\mathbb{E}[L]\)

In a single round, we draw until the first of the \(m\) jokers appears. Consider any one of the \(n\) numbered cards, say card \(x\). In the uniformly random relative order of the \(m + 1\) cards \(\{x\} \cup \{\text{all } m \text{ jokers}\}\), card \(x\) is the very first of these \(m + 1\) cards with probability \(\frac{1}{m + 1}\). By linearity of expectation across all \(n\) numbered cards plus the \(1\) joker that terminates the round: \[\mathbb{E}[L] = 1 + \sum_{x=1}^{n} \frac{1}{m + 1} = 1 + \frac{n}{m + 1} = \frac{n + m + 1}{m + 1}.\]

3. Expected Number of Rounds \(\mathbb{E}[R]\) via Two Dual Bridges

Let \(R_x \ge 1\) be the index of the first round in which numbered card \(x\) is drawn before a joker. The game ends at round \(R = \max_{1 \le x \le n} R_x\).

  • Method A (Min–Max Inclusion–Exclusion + Binomial Harmonic Identity): By Min–Max IE (§14.3): \[\mathbb{E}[R] = \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subseteq [n]} (-1)^{|T|-1} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{x \in T} R_x\right].\] Fix a subset \(T \subseteq [n]\) of size \(k = |T|\). In each round, \(\min_{x \in T} R_x\) is triggered if at least one of the \(k\) cards in \(T\) appears before all \(m\) jokers. Among the \(k + m\) cards in \(T \cup \{\text{jokers}\}\), the first card belongs to \(T\) with probability \(p_k = \frac{k}{k + m}\). Because rounds are independent, \(\min_{x \in T} R_x \sim \operatorname{Geom}(p_k)\) with PGF \(F_k(z) = \frac{p_k z}{1 - (1 - p_k)z}\) and expectation \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{x \in T} R_x] = F'_k(1) = \frac{1}{p_k} = \frac{k + m}{k} = 1 + \frac{m}{k}\). Summing over all \(\binom{n}{k}\) subsets of size \(k \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) and splitting \(1 + \frac{m}{k}\) using \(\sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1}\binom{n}{k} = 1 - (1 - 1)^n = 1\) gives: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[R] &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} \left(1 + \frac{m}{k}\right) \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} + m \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} \frac{1}{k} \\ &= 1 + m \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} \frac{1}{k}. \end{aligned}\] Recall the standard binomial-harmonic identity \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} \frac{1}{k} = H_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i}\) (provable by integrating \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} x^{k-1} = \frac{1 - (1 - x)^n}{x}\) from \(0\) to \(1\)). Thus: \[\mathbb{E}[R] = 1 + m \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i} = 1 + m H_n.\]

  • Method B (Direct Markov Conditioning on \(i = n - |S|\) Missing Cards): Whenever \(i \ge 1\) numbered cards remain uncollected, a round collects at least one of them with probability \(\frac{i}{i + m}\); conditioned on collecting at least one, the relative order of those \(i\) missing cards and \(m\) jokers still has the same distribution! In fact, each of the \(n\) numbered cards has a \(\frac{m}{i + m}\) chance of having a joker appear immediately before the \(i\)-th last new card is discovered, immediately yielding \(\mathbb{E}[R] = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{m}{i}\).

4. Final Formula & Algorithm Contract

Combining \(\mathbb{E}[L]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[R]\) yields the closed-form answer: \[\boxed{\mathbb{E}[T] = \left(1 + \frac{n}{m + 1}\right) \left(1 + m \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i}\right) \pmod{998244353}.}\]

  • Input & Preconditions: Integers \(n, m \in [1, 2 \times 10^6]\) representing \(n\) numbered cards and \(m\) jokers; prime modulus \(p = 998244353 > \max(n, m + 1)\), so all modular inverses \(\operatorname{inv}[1 \dots \max(n, m+1)] \bmod p\) are well-defined.
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(\mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{n + m + 1}{m + 1}\left(1 + m H_n\right) \bmod 998244353\).
  • Index & Truncation Boundaries: Harmonic sum index \(i\) runs over \(1 \le i \le n\), and linear modular inverse table is precomputed up to \(\max(n, m + 1)\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(n + m)\) time and \(O(n + m)\) space using the linear modular inverse table \(\operatorname{inv}[i] = (p - \lfloor p/i \rfloor) \operatorname{inv}[p \bmod i] \bmod p\) (or \(O(n \log p)\) time and \(O(1)\) auxiliary space).
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: After iteration \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), the harmonic accumulator holds \(H_i = \sum_{k=1}^{i} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{i}{k} \frac{1}{k} = \sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{1}{j} \bmod p\), which equals the expected number of rounds per joker when \(i\) numbered cards remain.
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: Because the game only checks whether \(S = \{1, \dots, n\}\) when a joker is drawn to end the round, the final round still requires drawing \(1\) joker (\(+1\) in \(\mathbb{E}[R] = 1 + m H_n\) and \(+1\) in \(\mathbb{E}[L] = 1 + \frac{n}{m+1}\)); omitting either \(+1\) undercounts the terminating joker draw.

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

There are \(N\) points numbered \(1, 2, \dots, N\) on a number line, all initially unpainted, and \(M\) segments \([L_1, R_1], [L_2, R_2], \dots, [L_M, R_M]\) with \(1 \le L_j \le R_j \le N\).

At each step, you choose one of the \(M\) segments uniformly at random (independently with probability \(1/M\) each) and paint all integer points in \([L_j, R_j]\). Compute the expected number of steps until all \(N\) points are painted, modulo \(998244353\) (it is guaranteed that every point \(1 \dots N\) is covered by at least one segment).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N, M \le 400\), \(1 \le L_j \le R_j \le N\), time limit \(2.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(N^2 (N + M))\) time and \(O(N^2 + N M)\) space.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 3, M = 3\))

Suppose \(N = 3\) and the \(M = 3\) segments are \(I_1 = [1, 2]\), \(I_2 = [2, 3]\), and \(I_3 = [2, 2]\).

  • For any non-empty subset of points \(T \subseteq \{1, 2, 3\}\), let \(c(T)\) be the number of segments that miss \(T\) completely (contain no point of \(T\)). Then \(M - c(T)\) segments hit \(T\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{x \in T} T_x] = \frac{M}{M - c(T)}\):
    • \(T = \{1\}\): missed by \(I_2 = [2,3]\) and \(I_3 = [2,2]\) (\(c = 2\)), so \(\mathbb{E}[T_1] = \frac{3}{3 - 2} = 3\).
    • \(T = \{3\}\): missed by \(I_1 = [1,2]\) and \(I_3 = [2,2]\) (\(c = 2\)), so \(\mathbb{E}[T_3] = \frac{3}{3 - 2} = 3\).
    • \(T = \{1, 3\}\): missed only by \(I_3 = [2, 2]\) which lies strictly inside the gap \((1, 3)\) (\(c = 1\)), so \(\mathbb{E}[\min(T_1, T_3)] = \frac{3}{3 - 1} = \frac{3}{2}\).
    • Any subset \(T\) containing point \(2\) (\(T \in \{\{2\}, \{1,2\}, \{2,3\}, \{1,2,3\}\}\)) hits all 3 segments (\(c = 0\)), so \(\mathbb{E}[\min_{x \in T} T_x] = \frac{3}{3} = 1\), and their alternating sum \(\sum_{S \subseteq \{1,3\}} (-1)^{|S|} (1) = (1 - 1)^2 = 0\) cancels completely!
  • Summing by Min–Max IE gives \(\mathbb{E}[\max_{x} T_x] = 3 + 3 - \frac{3}{2} + 0 = \frac{9}{2} \equiv 499122181 \pmod{998244353}\).

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Apply Min–Max IE to the \(N\) points: \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{1 \le x \le N} T_x\right] = \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}} (-1)^{|T|-1} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{x \in T} T_x\right].\] For a subset of points \(T \subseteq \{1, \dots, N\}\), let \(c(T)\) be the number of segments \([L_j, R_j]\) that contain no point of \(T\) (\([L_j, R_j] \cap T = \emptyset\)). Then at each step, the chosen segment hits \(T\) with probability \(\frac{M - c(T)}{M}\), so \(\min_{x \in T} T_x\) is a geometric stopping time with expectation \(\frac{M}{M - c(T)}\). Why is \(c(T)\) easy to compute by dynamic programming over the sorted elements \(i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_r\) of \(T\)? Because a segment \([L_j, R_j]\) misses \(T\) if and only if it lies strictly between two consecutive chosen points of \(T\) (with dummy boundary points \(i_0 = 0\) and \(i_{r+1} = N + 1\))!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Min–Max Inclusion–Exclusion Reduction to Missed-Segment Counts

Let \(T_x\) be the step at which point \(x \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) is first covered. By Min–Max Inclusion–Exclusion (§14.3): \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{1 \le x \le N} T_x\right] = \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subseteq [N]} (-1)^{|T|-1} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{x \in T} T_x\right].\] For a non-empty subset \(T \subseteq [N]\), let \(c(T) = \#\{j \in [M] : [L_j, R_j] \cap T = \emptyset\}\) be the number of segments disjoint from \(T\). Since every point in \([N]\) is covered by at least one segment and \(T \neq \emptyset\), at least one segment intersects \(T\), so \(0 \le c(T) \le M - 1\). At each step, the probability of picking a segment that intersects \(T\) is \(p(T) = \frac{M - c(T)}{M}\). Thus \(\min_{x \in T} T_x\) has PGF \(F_T(z) = \frac{p(T) z}{1 - (1 - p(T))z}\) and expectation: \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{x \in T} T_x\right] = F'_T(1) = \frac{M}{M - c(T)}.\]

2. Interval-Gap Decomposition of \(c(T)\)

Write the elements of \(T\) in increasing order as \(1 \le i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_r \le N\), and augment them with sentinel boundaries \(i_0 = 0\) and \(i_{r+1} = N + 1\). A segment \([L_j, R_j] \subseteq [1, N]\) contains no point of \(T\) if and only if there exists a unique index \(t \in \{0, 1, \dots, r\}\) such that \([L_j, R_j]\) is contained entirely inside the open gap \((i_t, i_{t+1})\), i.e., \(i_t + 1 \le L_j \le R_j \le i_{t+1} - 1\). Therefore, letting \(\operatorname{cnt}[l][r]\) denote the number of segments \([L_j, R_j]\) satisfying \(l \le L_j \le R_j \le r\) (with \(\operatorname{cnt}[l][r] = 0\) if \(l > r\)), the missed-segment count decomposes additively across the \(r + 1\) consecutive gaps: \[c(T) = \sum_{t=0}^{r} \operatorname{cnt}[i_t + 1][i_{t+1} - 1].\]

3. Polynomial / Knapsack DP over the Last Selected Point

We compute \(\operatorname{cnt}[l][r]\) for all \(1 \le l \le r \le N\) in \(O(N^2 + M)\) time via 2D inclusion–exclusion: \[\operatorname{cnt}[l][r] = \operatorname{exact}[l][r] + \operatorname{cnt}[l + 1][r] + \operatorname{cnt}[l][r - 1] - \operatorname{cnt}[l + 1][r - 1].\] Next, define \(\text{dp}[i][k]\) for \(1 \le i \le N\) and \(0 \le k \le M\) as the signed sum \(\sum (-1)^{|T|-1}\) over all subsets \(T \subseteq [i]\) with \(\max(T) = i\) whose prefix gaps \((0, i_1), (i_1, i_2), \dots, (i_{r-1}, i)\) contain a total of \(k\) segments:

  • Base case (\(i_1 = i\), meaning \(T = \{i\}\) of size \(1\), sign \((-1)^{1-1} = +1\)): \[\text{dp}[i]\big[\operatorname{cnt}[1][i - 1]\big] \mathrel{+}= 1.\]
  • Transitions from previous point \(j \in \{1, \dots, i - 1\}\): Let \(g = \operatorname{cnt}[j + 1][i - 1]\). Appending \(i\) to a subset ending at \(j\) increases \(|T|\) by \(1\) (flipping the sign by \(-1\)) and adds \(g\) missed segments: \[\text{dp}[i][k + g] \mathrel{-}= \text{dp}[j][k] \qquad (0 \le k \le M - g).\]
  • Final Extraction (closing the suffix gap \((i, N + 1)\)): For a subset \(T\) with \(\max(T) = i\), adding the final gap \(\operatorname{cnt}[i + 1][N]\) gives its total missed segments \(c(T) = k + \operatorname{cnt}[i + 1][N]\): \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{1 \le x \le N} T_x\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{k=0}^{M - 1 - \operatorname{cnt}[i+1][N]} \text{dp}[i][k] \cdot \frac{M}{M - \left(k + \operatorname{cnt}[i + 1][N]\right)} \pmod{998244353}.\]

4. Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Exact Index Boundaries: Number of points \(N \in [1, 400]\), number of segments \(M \in [1, 400]\), and \(1\)-indexed segment endpoints \([L_j, R_j] \subseteq [1, N]\) for \(j = 1, \dots, M\).
  • Preconditions: Every point \(x \in [N]\) is covered by at least one segment, ensuring \(k + \operatorname{cnt}[i+1][N] \le M - 1 < M\) for all reachable states with \(\text{dp}[i][k] \neq 0\), and \(M < 998244353\).
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(\mathbb{E}[\max_{1 \le x \le N} T_x] \bmod 998244353\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N^2 + M)\) time to build \(\operatorname{cnt}[l][r]\) and \(O(N^2 M)\) time for the \(O(N^2)\) gap transitions over degree-\(M\) polynomials; \(O(N^2 + NM)\) space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: After completing outer index \(i \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), \(\sum_{k=0}^{M} \text{dp}[i][k] u^k = \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subseteq [i], \max(T)=i} (-1)^{|T|-1} u^{\sum_{t=0}^{r-1} \operatorname{cnt}[i_t+1][i_{t+1}-1]}\) encodes the exact generating polynomial of prefix-missed segments for all subsets ending at \(i\).
  • Failure Mode & Characteristic-\(p\) Condition: If some point \(x \in [N]\) is covered by zero segments, a subset \(T = \{x\}\) avoids all \(M\) segments (\(k(T) = M\)), causing the geometric denominator \(M - k(T) = 0\) to diverge (\(\mathbb{E}[\max T_x] = \infty\)) and triggering division by zero in \(\operatorname{inv}[M - k_{\text{tot}}]\).

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

1. Min–Max IE + Truncated Exponential EGF Formula

Let \(T_i\) be the step at which toy \(i\) reaches \(B_i\) copies. Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n = 2, a = (2, 1)\))

In the problem statement, the transition probability \(\frac{1}{|U|}\) changes dynamically whenever a pigeonhole becomes full and drops out of \(U\). Why can we replace this dynamic process by an infinite static process where at every step we pick one of the \(n\) pigeonholes uniformly at random (with probability \(1/n\) each) and allow already-full pigeonholes to keep receiving dummy pigeons, defining \(T_j\) as the step at which pigeonhole \(j\) receives its \(a_j\)-th pigeon?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

In the static process, any step that lands on an already-full pigeonhole is simply a self-loop on the set of non-full pigeonholes; conditioning on landing inside the non-full set \(U\) picks each \(j \in U\) uniformly with probability \(\frac{1}{|U|}\)! Therefore, the probability distribution of the relative order in which the \(n\) pigeonholes reach their thresholds \(a_j\) is 100% identical in both processes! Now fix pigeonhole \(i\). We want \(\mathbb{P}(T_i > T_j \text{ for all } j \neq i)\). By Inclusion–Exclusion over the subset \(T \subseteq [n] \setminus \{i\}\) of pigeonholes that violate this condition (i.e. \(T_j > T_i\) for all \(j \in T\)): \[\mathbb{P}(\text{hole } i \text{ is last}) = \sum_{T \subseteq [n] \setminus \{i\}} (-1)^{|T|} \mathbb{P}\!\left(T_j > T_i \text{ for all } j \in T\right).\] Notice that the event \(\{T_j > T_i \text{ for all } j \in T\}\) depends only on steps that land in \(T \cup \{i\}\) (an active set of size \(|T| + 1\))!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Conditioned on steps landing in \(T \cup \{i\}\) (each with probability \(\frac{1}{|T| + 1}\)), \(T_j > T_i\) holds for all \(j \in T\) if and only if, at the exact step when \(i\) receives its \(a_i\)-th pigeon, every \(j \in T\) has received \(0 \le c_j < a_j\) pigeons. NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Equivalence to Uniform Continuous-Time Poissonization

Consider \(n\) independent Poisson processes of rate \(\lambda = 1\), one for each pigeonhole \(j \in \{1, \dots, n\}\). At any moment when \(U \subseteq [n]\) is the set of non-full pigeonholes, by the memorylessness and competing-exponentials property of Poisson processes, the next arrival among \(U\) is equally likely to occur in any pigeonhole \(j \in U\) (probability \(1/|U|\))! Therefore, if \(\tau_j\) is the continuous time at which pigeonhole \(j\) receives its \(a_j\)-th arrival (an \(\operatorname{Erlang}(a_j, 1)\) random variable with density \(f_j(t) = \frac{t^{a_j - 1}}{(a_j - 1)!} e^{-t}\) and survival function \(\mathbb{P}(\tau_j > t) = e^{-t} \sum_{c=0}^{a_j - 1} \frac{t^c}{c!}\)), the probability that pigeonhole \(i\) is the last to fill is: \[P_i = \mathbb{P}\!\left(\tau_i > \tau_j \text{ for all } j \neq i\right) = \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{t^{a_i - 1}}{(a_i - 1)!} e^{-t} \prod_{j \neq i} \left(1 - e^{-t} \sum_{c=0}^{a_j - 1} \frac{t^c}{c!}\right) dt.\]

2. Expanding the Subset Product via Inclusion–Exclusion

Expanding the product over \(j \neq i\) as a sum over subsets \(T \subseteq [n] \setminus \{i\}\) gives: \[P_i = \sum_{T \subseteq [n] \setminus \{i\}} (-1)^{|T|} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{t^{a_i - 1}}{(a_i - 1)!} e^{-(|T| + 1)t} \prod_{j \in T} \left(\sum_{c=0}^{a_j - 1} \frac{t^c}{c!}\right) dt.\] Letting \(m = |T| \in \{0, \dots, n - 1\}\) and \(K = \sum_{j \in T} c_j \in \{0, \dots, \sum_{j \neq i}(a_j - 1)\}\), and evaluating the Laplace integral \(\int_{0}^{\infty} t^{K + a_i - 1} e^{-(m + 1)t} \, dt = \frac{(K + a_i - 1)!}{(m + 1)^{K + a_i}}\) yields: \[\boxed{P_i = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \sum_{K=0}^{S} \operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K] \cdot \binom{K + a_i - 1}{a_i - 1} \cdot \frac{K!}{(m + 1)^{K + a_i}},}\] where \(\operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K]\) is the coefficient of \(x^K\) in the signed EGF subset sum over \([n] \setminus \{i\}\): \[\operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K] = [y^m x^K] \prod_{j \neq i} \left(1 - y \sum_{c=0}^{a_j - 1} \frac{x^c}{c!}\right).\]

3. Rollback EGF Knapsack in \(O(n^2 S^2)\)

Instead of running the \((n - 1)\)-item knapsack from scratch \(n\) times in \(O(n^3 S^2)\), we first compute the full \(n\)-item 2D polynomial product \[D(y, x) = \prod_{j=1}^{n} \left(1 - y \sum_{c=0}^{a_j - 1} \frac{x^c}{c!}\right)\] in \(O(n^2 S^2)\) time. Then, for each \(i = 1, \dots, n\), we obtain \(\operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K]\) by dividing \(D(y, x)\) by \(\left(1 - y \sum_{c=0}^{a_i - 1} \frac{x^c}{c!}\right)\) in a single forward pass over \(m = 0, \dots, n - 1\): \[\operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K] = D[m][K] + \sum_{c=0}^{\min(K, a_i - 1)} \operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m - 1][K - c] \cdot \frac{1}{c!}.\]

4. Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Exact Index Boundaries: Number of pigeonholes \(n \in [1, 30]\) and \(1\)-indexed capacities \(a_1, \dots, a_n \in [1, 30]\), with maximum survival degree \(S = \sum_{j=1}^n (a_j - 1) \le 870\).
  • Preconditions: \(n + \sum_{j=1}^n a_j \le 930 < 998244353\), so all factorials \(K!\) and powers \((m + 1)^{-(K + a_i)}\) are invertible modulo \(998244353\).
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(P_1, \dots, P_n \bmod 998244353\), satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^n P_i \equiv 1 \pmod{998244353}\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: Building \(D(y, x)\) takes \(O(n S \cdot \max a_i) = O(n S^2)\) operations; each of the \(n\) rollbacks takes \(O(n S a_i)\) operations, summing to \(O(n S^2) = O\big(n (\sum a_i)^2\big)\) total time and \(O(n S)\) space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: During the rollback for item \(i\), the recurrence \(\operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K] = D[m][K] + \sum_{c=0}^{\min(K, a_i - 1)} \operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m - 1][K - c] \frac{1}{c!}\) maintains \(\sum_{m, K} \operatorname{dp}^{(-i)}[m][K] y^m x^K = D(y, x) \cdot \left(1 - y \sum_{c=0}^{a_i - 1} \frac{x^c}{c!}\right)^{-1} \bmod y^n\).

Suppose \(A = 0, B = 2, C = 0\) and \(T = 2\):

1. Verification of Target Return Symmetry & Parity Check

First, at step \(n\), particle \(1\) is at coordinate \(X\) if and only if \(X \equiv A + n \pmod 2\). Therefore, all three particles can meet at step \(n\) only if \(A \equiv B \equiv C \pmod 2\); if \(A, B, C\) do not all have the same parity, the answer is immediately \(0\).

Assume \(A \equiv B \equiv C \pmod 2\). Let \(\mathcal{T} = \{(X, X, X) : X \in \mathbb{Z}\}\). For any meeting state \(w = (X, X, X) \in \mathcal{T}\), the probability of being in \(\mathcal{T}\) again after \(m\) steps is: \[r_m = \sum_{Y \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{P}\!\left((X_{1,m}, X_{2,m}, X_{3,m}) = (Y, Y, Y) \;\middle|\; (X, X, X)\right) = 8^{-m} \sum_{j=0}^{m} \binom{m}{j}^3,\] where \(j = \frac{m + (Y - X)}{2}\) is the number of \(+1\) steps taken by each of the 3 particles. 2. Computing \(h_m\) and \(r_m\) for All \(m \le T\) via Factorial Convolution

(j + \alpha)! (j + \beta)!}}_{A_j} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{(m - j)! (m - j - \alpha)! (k - \beta)!} & \text{if } k - \alpha \ge 0 \text{ and } k - \beta \ge 0, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}\] Similarly, the return sequence \(r_m\) is the special case \(\alpha = \beta = 0\) of the exact same identity, obtained by squaring \(C(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{T} \frac{z^k}{(k!)^3}\) in one NTT multiplication: \[\boxed{r_m = 8^{-m} (m!)^3 \, [z^m]\!\left(C(z)^2\right).}\]

1. Separable Martingale Potential Construction

Let \(\Phi(a_1, \dots, a_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(a_i)\) with \(f(0) = 0\). In one transition from \((a_1, \dots, a_n)\), pile \(i\) (of size \(x = a_i\)) moves to \(x + 1\) with probability \(\frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)}\) and to \(x - 1\) with probability \(\frac{x}{S}\). By linearity of expectation, the one-step conditional drift of \(\Phi\) is: \[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Phi(X_{t+1}) - \Phi(X_t) \;\middle|\; X_t\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \frac{S - a_i}{S(n - 1)} \big(f(a_i + 1) - f(a_i)\big) + \frac{a_i}{S} \big(f(a_i - 1) - f(a_i)\big) \right).\] Because \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i = S\) is conserved along every trajectory, we have the exact identity \[\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{S - a_i}{S(n - 1)} = \frac{n S - S}{S(n - 1)} = 1.\] Therefore, if \(f(0), f(1), \dots, f(S)\) satisfy the single-pile balance equation \[\frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)} \big(f(x + 1) - f(x)\big) + \frac{x}{S} \big(f(x - 1) - f(x)\big) = \frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)} \qquad (0 \le x < S),\] then summing over \(i = 1, \dots, n\) guarantees \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi(X_{t+1}) - \Phi(X_t) \mid X_t] = +1\) at every non-terminal state!

2. First-Difference Order Reduction & Optional Stopping

Define \(g(x) = f(x + 1) - f(x)\) for \(0 \le x < S\). Substituting \(f(x + 1) - f(x) = g(x)\) and \(f(x - 1) - f(x) = -g(x - 1)\) into the single-pile balance equation gives: \[\frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)} g(x) - \frac{x}{S} g(x - 1) = \frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)}.\] For every \(0 \le x < S\), the factor \(S - x \ge 1\) is nonzero modulo \(998244353\) (since \(S \le 3 \times 10^5 < 998244353\)). Dividing by \(\frac{S - x}{S(n - 1)}\) yields the two-term recurrence: \[\boxed{g(0) = 1, \qquad g(x) = 1 + \frac{x(n - 1)}{S - x} g(x - 1) \quad (1 \le x < S).}\] Recovering \(f(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{x-1} g(j)\) (with \(f(0) = 0\)) makes \(M_t = \Phi(X_t) - t\) a martingale. At the stopping time \(T\), one player holds \(S\) biscuits and the remaining \(n - 1\) players hold \(0\) biscuits, so \(\Phi(X_T) = f(S) + (n - 1)f(0) = f(S)\) deterministically. By Doob’s Optional Stopping Theorem (\(\mathbb{E}[M_T] = \mathbb{E}[M_0]\)): \[\mathbb{E}[f(S) - T] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(a_i) \implies \boxed{\mathbb{E}[T] = f(S) - \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(a_i) \pmod{998244353}.}\]
