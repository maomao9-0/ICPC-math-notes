6.1 The Golden Rule of Index Alignment & Vandermonde Identities

Plain-Language Introduction: Why Binomial Sums Stall and How FPS Unlocks Them

In competitive programming, counting problems and dynamic programming transitions frequently culminate in a sum of products of binomial coefficients, powers, and DP states: \[S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} a_k b_{n-k}, \qquad T_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n+k}{2k} 2^{n-k}, \qquad \text{or} \qquad U_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{R}{a+k}\binom{S}{b+k}.\] Evaluated naively, computing \(S_n\) for all \(0 \le n \le N\) takes \(O(N^2)\) operations, and computing a single \(S_N\) when \(N \le 10^{18}\) is impossible by direct iteration.

Every algebraic simplification of a binomial sum rests on a single structural observation: a binomial coefficient \(\binom{A}{B}\) is not an opaque arithmetic function—it is a single coefficient extracted from a simple power series (\((1+x)^A\) or \((1-x)^{-B-1}\)), and its factorial expansion \(\frac{A!}{B!(A-B)!}\) separates into three independent pieces depending on the upper index \(A\), the lower index \(B\), and their difference \(A - B\). Whenever we align indices so that each factor depends only on \(k\), only on \(n-k\), or only on \(n\), a quadratic loop collapses into a single \(O(N \log N)\) polynomial multiplication—or, when the factors are pure binomial generating functions, into an \(O(1)\) closed-form identity.

ImportantLocal Algebraic & Characteristic Hypotheses for Section 6.1

Throughout this section, let \(K\) be a commutative ring with identity \(1_K\).

  1. Ring-Level Identities (\(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) and arbitrary \(K[[x]]\) without division): For any non-negative integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\), the ordinary binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{k}\) is an integer counting \(k\)-element subsets of an \(n\)-element set. Thus polynomial identities such as \((1+x)^n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} x^k\), upper-index negation \(\binom{-r}{k} = (-1)^k \binom{r+k-1}{k}\) for \(r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\), and the Chu–Vandermonde convolution hold over any commutative ring \(K\) (including \(\mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z}\) for composite \(M\) and \(\mathbb{F}_p\) for any prime \(p\)) with zero divisions required.
  2. Factorial Decoupling & EGF Scaling (\(p > N\)): Whenever we split \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) to form exponential generating functions \(\sum_{k=0}^N \frac{a_k}{k!} x^k\), we divide by \(k!\) for \(0 \le k \le N\). This requires \(k! \in K^\times\) for all \(0 \le k \le N\). Over a prime field \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\), this holds if and only if \(p > N\). When \(p \le N\) or \(K = \mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z}\) is non-prime, dividing by \(k!\) is illegal; we must instead use division-free polynomial identities (such as the Falling-Factorial Absorption Identity in Section 6.4 or Lucas’s Theorem in Section 6.3).

The Golden Rule of Index Alignment: Additive vs. Difference Convolutions

Suppose we wish to compute a sequence \(c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N\) defined by a sum over an internal index \(k\). Before touching any generating function, we apply the Golden Rule of Index Alignment:

Golden Rule of Index Alignment: Factor every summand into three separated components—a factor \(\alpha(n)\) depending only on the external query index \(n\), a factor \(\beta(k)\) depending only on the first summation index \(k\), and a factor \(\gamma(m)\) depending only on the complementary index \(m\): \[c_n = \alpha(n) \sum_{k + m = n} \beta(k)\, \gamma(m) \qquad \text{or} \qquad d_n = \alpha(n) \sum_{j - i = n} \beta(i)\, \gamma(j).\]

Depending on whether the two internal indices sum to \(n\) or differ by \(n\), we route to one of two convolution primitives from Chapter 01:

  1. Regime 1 — Constant-Sum (Additive Cauchy) Convolution (\(k + m = n\)): When \(m = n - k\) with \(0 \le k \le n \le N\), the sum is \[c_n = \alpha(n) \sum_{k=0}^n \beta(k)\, \gamma(n - k).\] Defining the formal power series \(B(x) = \sum_{k=0}^N \beta(k) x^k\) and \(G(x) = \sum_{m=0}^N \gamma(m) x^m\), the inner sum is the exact Cauchy product coefficient \([x^n](B(x)G(x))\). For example, if \(c_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} a_k b_{n-k}\) and \(p > N\), expanding \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) yields \[c_n = n! \sum_{k=0}^n \left(\frac{a_k}{k!}\right) \left(\frac{b_{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\right) = n! \, [x^n] \left(\sum_{k=0}^N \frac{a_k}{k!} x^k\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^N \frac{b_m}{m!} x^m\right).\]

  2. Regime 2 — Constant-Difference (Cross-Correlation / Reverse-Indexed) Convolution (\(j - i = n\)): Frequently—most notably in Polynomial Taylor Shift (Section 6.3), inclusion-exclusion upper-triangular sums, and substring correlation—the sum runs over \(0 \le i \le j \le N\) with a fixed difference \(j - i = n\) (equivalently \(j = i + n\)): \[d_n = \alpha(n) \sum_{i=0}^{N-n} u_i\, v_{i+n} = \alpha(n) \sum_{j=n}^N u_{j-n}\, v_j \qquad (0 \le n \le N).\] Here the indices \(i\) and \(j = i+n\) do not sum to \(n\); instead, \((-i) + j = n\). Because standard NTT polynomial multiplication requires non-negative exponents, we shift the negative index \(-i\) into \([0, N]\) by defining the reversed polynomial \[\widetilde{U}(x) = x^N U(x^{-1}) = \sum_{i=0}^N u_i x^{N-i} = \sum_{r=0}^N u_{N-r} x^r, \qquad V(x) = \sum_{j=0}^N v_j x^j.\] Multiplying \(\widetilde{U}(x)\) and \(V(x)\) adds the exponents \((N - i) + j = N + (j - i) = N + n\): \[[x^{N+n}] \bigl(\widetilde{U}(x) V(x)\bigr) = \sum_{\substack{0 \le i, j \le N \\ (N - i) + j = N + n}} u_i\, v_j = \sum_{i=0}^{N-n} u_i\, v_{i+n}.\] Thus \(d_n = \alpha(n) [x^{N+n}]\bigl(\widetilde{U}(x) V(x)\bigr)\) for all \(0 \le n \le N\) in a single polynomial multiplication of degree \(N\) by degree \(N\) (output length \(2N + 1\)).

TipHand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 3\)): Cross-Correlation via Polynomial Reversal

Let \(N = 3\) and suppose we want \(d_n = \sum_{i=0}^{3-n} u_i v_{i+n}\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\).

  • Set \(\widetilde{U}(x) = u_3 + u_2 x + u_1 x^2 + u_0 x^3\) (indices reversed: \([x^r]\widetilde{U} = u_{3-r}\)) and \(V(x) = v_0 + v_1 x + v_2 x^2 + v_3 x^3\).
  • Let us inspect the coefficients of \(\widetilde{U}(x)V(x)\) at degrees \(3 + n \in \{3, 4, 5, 6\}\):
    • \([x^{3+0}](\widetilde{U}V) = [x^3](\widetilde{U}V) = u_3 v_3 + u_2 v_2 + u_1 v_1 + u_0 v_0 = d_0\).
    • \([x^{3+1}](\widetilde{U}V) = [x^4](\widetilde{U}V) = u_2 v_3 + u_1 v_2 + u_0 v_1 = d_1\).
    • \([x^{3+2}](\widetilde{U}V) = [x^5](\widetilde{U}V) = u_1 v_3 + u_0 v_2 = d_2\).
    • \([x^{3+3}](\widetilde{U}V) = [x^6](\widetilde{U}V) = u_0 v_3 = d_3\). Every difference sum \(d_n\) sits cleanly at index \(N + n\) of the product array!

The Four Foundational Binomial & Vandermonde Identities

When the summands \(\beta(k)\) and \(\gamma(n-k)\) are themselves binomial coefficients, we do not even need an NTT: the product \(B(x)G(x)\) simplifies algebraically into a single power of \((1+x)\) or \((1-x)^{-1}\). Let us establish the four foundational identities from first principles.

1. Extended Binomial Coefficients & Upper-Index Negation

For any formal variable or ring element \(R \in K\) (when \(\mathbb{Q} \subseteq K\), or in \(\mathbb{Z}\) when \(R \in \mathbb{Z}\)) and any integer \(k\), the generalized binomial coefficient is defined by \[\binom{R}{k} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{R^{\underline{k}}}{k!} = \dfrac{R(R-1)\cdots(R-k+1)}{k!} & \text{if } k \ge 0, \\[6pt] 0 & \text{if } k < 0. \end{cases}\] What happens when the upper index is negative, say \(R = -r\) for a positive integer \(r \ge 1\)? Let us factor \(-1\) out of each of the \(k\) factors in the numerator: \[\binom{-r}{k} = \frac{(-r)(-r-1)(-r-2)\cdots(-r-k+1)}{k!} = (-1)^k \frac{r(r+1)(r+2)\cdots(r+k-1)}{k!} = (-1)^k \binom{r+k-1}{k}.\] Consequently, the generalized binomial series \((1 - x)^{-r} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \binom{-r}{k} (-x)^k\) takes the ubiquitous positive-coefficient form: \[\frac{1}{(1 - x)^r} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{-r}{k} (-1)^k x^k = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{r + k - 1}{k} x^k = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{r + k - 1}{r - 1} x^k \in \mathbb{Z}[[x]].\] Combinatorially, \([x^k](1 - x)^{-r} = \binom{r+k-1}{k}\) counts weak compositions of \(k\) into \(r\) non-negative integer parts \(c_1 + c_2 + \dots + c_r = k\) (or multisets of size \(k\) chosen from \(r\) types).

2. Chu–Vandermonde Convolution (Sum of Lower Indices Fixed)

Question Answered: How many ways can we choose \(n\) items from a disjoint union of \(R\) red items and \(S\) silver items, classified by how many red items \(k\) are chosen?

For any \(R, S\) and any integer \(n \ge 0\): \[\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{R}{k} \binom{S}{n - k} = \binom{R + S}{n}.\]

  • Single-Step Algebraic Proof: \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{R}{k} \binom{S}{n-k} &= \sum_{k=0}^n \Big([x^k](1+x)^R\Big) \Big([x^{n-k}](1+x)^S\Big) && \text{(encoding each binomial as a coefficient of }(1+x)\text{)} \\ &= [x^n] \Big((1+x)^R (1+x)^S\Big) && \text{(definition of Cauchy product in }K[[x]]\text{)} \\ &= [x^n] (1+x)^{R+S} && \text{(exponent law in }K[[x]]\text{)} \\ &= \binom{R+S}{n}. && \text{(extracting }[x^n]\text{)} \end{aligned}\] Because this identity holds as a polynomial identity in \(\mathbb{Q}[R, S]\), it holds for negative upper indices \(R = -r, S = -s\) as well!

3. Parallel / Upper-Index Convolution (Sum of Upper Indices Fixed)

Question Answered: Given \(n + 1\) points \(1, 2, \dots, n+1\) in a row, how many ways can we choose \(a + b + 1\) points, classified by the position \(k + 1\) of the \((a+1)\)-st chosen point?

For any non-negative integers \(a, b, n \ge 0\): \[\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{k}{a} \binom{n - k}{b} = \binom{n + 1}{a + b + 1}.\]

  • Combinatorial Double-Count: To choose \(a + b + 1\) elements from \(\{1, 2, \dots, n+1\}\), let the \((a+1)\)-st smallest chosen element be \(k + 1\) (where \(a \le k \le n - b\)). We must choose \(a\) elements from the \(k\) elements \(\{1, \dots, k\}\) to its left (\(\binom{k}{a}\) ways) and \(b\) elements from the \(n - k\) elements \(\{k+2, \dots, n+1\}\) to its right (\(\binom{n-k}{b}\) ways). Summing over all \(k\) gives \(\binom{n+1}{a+b+1}\).
  • Single-Step Generating Function Proof: Shifting indices by \(i = k - a\) and \(j = (n - k) - b\) (so \(i + j = n - a - b\)), we recognize the negative-binomial generating function \(\sum_{i \ge 0} \binom{a+i}{a} x^i = (1 - x)^{-(a+1)}\): \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{k}{a} \binom{n-k}{b} &= \sum_{i + j = n - a - b} \binom{a + i}{i} \binom{b + j}{j} && \text{(symmetry }\binom{a+i}{a}=\binom{a+i}{i}\text{ and index shift)} \\ &= [x^{n - a - b}] \left(\frac{1}{(1 - x)^{a+1}} \cdot \frac{1}{(1 - x)^{b+1}}\right) && \text{(Cauchy product of negative-binomial OGFs)} \\ &= [x^{n - a - b}] \frac{1}{(1 - x)^{a + b + 2}} && \text{(combining denominators)} \\ &= \binom{(a + b + 2) + (n - a - b) - 1}{n - a - b} = \binom{n + 1}{a + b + 1}. && \text{(extracting }[x^{n-a-b}]\text{)} \end{aligned}\] More generally, for an \(m\)-fold convolution \(\sum_{k_1 + \dots + k_m = N} \prod_{r=1}^m \binom{k_r}{A_r}\), multiplying the \(m\) generating functions \(\prod_{r=1}^m x^{A_r}(1 - x)^{-(A_r + 1)} = x^{\sum A_r} (1 - x)^{-\sum (A_r + 1)}\) immediately yields \(\binom{N + m - 1}{\sum_{r=1}^m A_r + m - 1}\) (see SS-2: AtCoder ARC 110 D in Section 6.5).

4. Symmetry Flipping When Both Lower Indices Carry \(+k\)

What if a sum has \(+k\) in both lower indices, such as \[U = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \binom{R}{a + k} \binom{S}{b + k}\] for non-negative integers \(R \ge 0\) and arbitrary \(S, a, b\)?

NoteDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Symmetry Flipping
  • Diagnostic Signal: You see a product of two binomial coefficients \(\binom{R}{a+k}\binom{S}{b+k}\) where \(k\) appears with the same sign (\(+k\) and \(+k\)) in both lower positions, preventing direct Chu–Vandermonde application (which requires \(+k\) and \(-k\)).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    1. Look at the two upper indices \(R\) and \(S\). At least one of them (say \(R\)) is a non-negative integer.
    2. For a non-negative integer \(R \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\), symmetry \(\binom{R}{m} = \binom{R}{R - m}\) flips the sign of \(m\) in the lower index!
    3. Apply \(\binom{R}{a + k} = \binom{R}{R - a - k}\). Now the two lower indices are \((R - a - k)\) and \((b + k)\), whose sum \((R - a - k) + (b + k) = R - a + b\) is independent of \(k\)!
    4. Apply Chu–Vandermonde: \[\sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \binom{R}{a + k}\binom{S}{b + k} = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} \binom{R}{R - a - k}\binom{S}{b + k} = \binom{R + S}{R - a + b}.\] In particular, setting \(R = S = n\) and \(a = b = 0\) gives the classic sum of squares \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}^2 = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{n-k}\binom{n}{k} = \binom{2n}{n}\).
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Upper-Index vs. Lower-Index Vandermonde Confusion & Negative-Upper-Index Symmetry Trap
  1. The \(+1\) Offset Trap in Parallel / Upper-Index Vandermonde:
    • Trap: Writing \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{k}{a}\binom{n-k}{b} = \binom{n}{a+b}\) by false analogy with Chu–Vandermonde \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{R}{k}\binom{S}{n-k} = \binom{R+S}{n}\).
    • Algebraic Root Cause & Remedy: In Chu–Vandermonde, we multiply \((1+x)^R (1+x)^S = (1+x)^{R+S}\), so the upper indices simply add (\(R + S\)) and the lower index stays \(n\). In Upper-Index Vandermonde (Identity 3), the generating function of \(\binom{k}{a}\) is \(\frac{x^a}{(1-x)^{a+1}}\), so multiplying two such series gives \(\frac{x^{a+b}}{(1-x)^{(a+1)+(b+1)}} = \frac{x^{a+b}}{(1-x)^{a+b+2}}\). The extra \(+1\) in each denominator exponent adds up to \(+2\), shifting both the upper and lower indices of the result by \(+1\): \(\binom{n+1}{a+b+1}\)! Always check \(n = a + b\): the only nonzero term is \(k = a\), giving \(\binom{a}{a}\binom{b}{b} = 1 = \binom{a+b+1}{a+b+1}\).
  2. The Negative-Upper-Index Symmetry Trap (\(\binom{R}{k} \neq \binom{R}{R-k}\) for \(R < 0\)):
    • Trap: Applying symmetry \(\binom{R}{k} = \binom{R}{R-k}\) when the upper index \(R\) is a negative integer (or non-integer) \(R = -r < 0\).
    • Algebraic Root Cause & Remedy: By definition, \(\binom{R}{m} = \frac{R^{\underline{m}}}{m!}\) for \(m \ge 0\) and \(\binom{R}{m} = 0\) for \(m < 0\). When \(R = -r < 0\) and \(k \ge 0\), the quantity \(R - k = -r - k < 0\) is negative, so \(\binom{R}{R-k} = 0\) whereas \(\binom{-r}{k} = (-1)^k \binom{r+k-1}{k} \neq 0\)! Rule: Never apply symmetry \(\binom{A}{B} = \binom{A}{A-B}\) unless you have first verified that \(A \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) is a non-negative integer. If \(A = -r < 0\), apply upper-index negation \(\binom{-r}{k} = (-1)^k \binom{r+k-1}{k}\) first so the new upper index \(r + k - 1 \ge 0\) is non-negative, and only then apply symmetry \(\binom{r+k-1}{k} = \binom{r+k-1}{r-1}\)!

Engine 1: Wilf’s Snake Oil Method (The External Generating Function Swap)

Herbert Wilf coined the name “Snake Oil” for a method that cures seemingly intractable binomial sums by refusing to evaluate the inner sum \(\sum_k\) directly. Instead, we embed the sum into a generating function over the free external parameter \(n\), swap the order of summation, collapse the \(n\)-sum FIRST using a basic binomial kernel, and read off \([x^n]\) at the end.

    1. Identify the free variable: Pick the external integer parameter \(n\) (or \(m\)) that appears in only one binomial coefficient, and define the external OGF \(F(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} S_n x^n\).
    2. Interchange \(\sum_n\) and \(\sum_k\): Let \(k\) range over all integers \(\mathbb{Z}\) (relying on the natural boundary convention \(\binom{A}{B} = 0\) when \(B < 0\) or \(B > A \ge 0\) so you do not have to fight manual summation limits!) and pull \(\sum_{k}\) outside \(\sum_{n \ge 0}\).

    4. Collapse the outer \(k\)-sum: The outer sum over \(k\) is now a standard generating function (geometric series, binomial theorem, or Catalan series) evaluated at a rational expression \(w(x)\).
    5. Extract \([x^n] F(x)\): Use partial fractions or negative-binomial expansion to read off \(S_n = [x^n] F(x)\) (and check \(n = 0\) or \(m = 0\) boundary valuations!).

Worked Derivation 1: Evaluating \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n+k}{2k} 2^{n-k}\) via Snake Oil

Problem: For \(n \ge 0\), find a closed form for the binomial sum \[S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n+k}{2k} 2^{n-k}.\]

  • Hand-Checkable Small Cases (\(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\)):
    • \(n = 0\): \(\binom{0}{0} 2^0 = 1\).
    • \(n = 1\): \(\binom{1}{0} 2^1 + \binom{2}{2} 2^0 = 2 + 1 = 3\).
    • \(n = 2\): \(\binom{2}{0} 2^2 + \binom{3}{2} 2^1 + \binom{4}{4} 2^0 = 4 + 6 + 1 = 11\).
    • \(n = 3\): \(\binom{3}{0} 2^3 + \binom{4}{2} 2^2 + \binom{5}{4} 2^1 + \binom{6}{6} 2^0 = 8 + 24 + 10 + 1 = 43\). Notice the differences: \(3 - 1 = 2\), \(11 - 3 = 8\), \(43 - 11 = 32\). Let us see how Snake Oil derives the exact closed form without guessing!
  • Step-by-Step Verbalized Derivation:
    1. Form the external OGF \(F(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty S_n x^n\) and swap sums: \[\begin{aligned} F(x) &= \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{n+k}{2k} 2^{n-k} && \text{(extending }k > n\text{ safely since }\binom{n+k}{2k}=0\text{ for }k > n\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty 2^{-k} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \binom{n+k}{2k} (2x)^n. && \text{(factoring }2^{n-k} = 2^{-k}\cdot 2^n\text{ and interchanging }\sum_n, \sum_k\text{)} \end{aligned}\]
    2. Collapse the inner \(n\)-sum using Kernel K3 at variable \(z = 2x\): By Kernel K3, \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty \binom{n+k}{2k} z^n = \frac{z^k}{(1 - z)^{2k+1}}\). Substituting \(z = 2x\): \[\begin{aligned} F(x) &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty 2^{-k} \frac{(2x)^k}{(1 - 2x)^{2k+1}} && \text{(applying Kernel K3 with }z = 2x\text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{1 - 2x} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left(\frac{x}{(1 - 2x)^2}\right)^k. && \text{(canceling }2^{-k}\cdot 2^k = 1\text{ and grouping }k\text{-th powers)} \end{aligned}\]
    3. Sum the outer geometric series over \(k\): Since \(\operatorname{val}\!\left(\frac{x}{(1-2x)^2}\right) = 1 \ge 1\), the geometric series converges in \(\mathbb{Q}[[x]]\): \[\begin{aligned} F(x) &= \frac{1}{1 - 2x} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{x}{(1 - 2x)^2}} && \text{(summing }\sum_{k \ge 0} w^k = \frac{1}{1-w}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{1 - 2x}{(1 - 2x)^2 - x} = \frac{1 - 2x}{1 - 5x + 4x^2} = \frac{1 - 2x}{(1 - 4x)(1 - x)}. && \text{(clearing denominator and factoring)} \end{aligned}\]
    4. Partial fraction decomposition and \([x^n]\) extraction: Decomposing \(\frac{1 - 2x}{(1 - 4x)(1 - x)} = \frac{A}{1 - 4x} + \frac{B}{1 - x}\): at \(x = 1/4\), \(A = \frac{1 - 1/2}{1 - 1/4} = \frac{2}{3}\); at \(x = 1\), \(B = \frac{1 - 2}{1 - 4} = \frac{1}{3}\). Thus \[F(x) = \frac{2/3}{1 - 4x} + \frac{1/3}{1 - x} \implies S_n = [x^n] F(x) = \frac{2 \cdot 4^n + 1}{3}.\] Checking \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\): \(\frac{2+1}{3} = 1\), \(\frac{8+1}{3} = 3\), \(\frac{32+1}{3} = 11\), \(\frac{128+1}{3} = 43\). Exact match!

Worked Derivation 2 (Catalan Dual Sum & Boundary Pitfall): \(\sum_{k \ge 0} \binom{n+k}{m+2k} \binom{2k}{k} \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1}\)

Problem: For integers \(n \ge 0\) and \(m \ge 1\), evaluate \[S(n, m) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \binom{n+k}{m+2k} \binom{2k}{k} \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1},\] and explain why the formula changes at the boundary \(m = 0\).

  • Step-by-Step Verbalized Derivation:
    1. Choose the free variable: Here \(n\) appears only in the upper index of \(\binom{n+k}{m+2k}\), and \(m \ge 1\) guarantees \(m + 2k \ge 1 > k\) for all \(k \ge 0\). Form \(F_m(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty S(n, m) x^n\) and apply Kernel K4: \[\begin{aligned} F_m(x) &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{2k}{k} \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \binom{n+k}{m+2k} x^n && \text{(swapping }\sum_n\text{ and }\sum_k\text{)} \\ &= \frac{x^m}{(1 - x)^{m+1}} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k} \left(\frac{-x}{(1 - x)^2}\right)^k. && \text{(applying Kernel K4)} \end{aligned}\]
    2. Recognize the Catalan generating function: Recall from Chapter 02 that the Catalan OGF \(C(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k} z^k = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4z}}{2z}\) satisfies \(1 + z C(z)^2 = C(z)\). At \(z = \frac{-x}{(1 - x)^2}\), what is \(1 - 4z\)? It is a perfect square: \[1 - 4z = 1 + \frac{4x}{(1 - x)^2} = \frac{(1 - x)^2 + 4x}{(1 - x)^2} = \frac{(1 + x)^2}{(1 - x)^2}.\] Taking the unique formal power series square root with constant term \(+1\) gives \(\sqrt{1 - 4z} = \frac{1 + x}{1 - x}\), so \[C\!\left(\frac{-x}{(1 - x)^2}\right) = \frac{1 - \dfrac{1 + x}{1 - x}}{\dfrac{-2x}{(1 - x)^2}} = \frac{\dfrac{-2x}{1 - x}}{\dfrac{-2x}{(1 - x)^2}} = 1 - x.\]
    3. Substitute back and extract \([x^n]\): \[F_m(x) = \frac{x^m}{(1 - x)^{m+1}} \cdot (1 - x) = \frac{x^m}{(1 - x)^m} \implies S(n, m) = [x^n] \frac{x^m}{(1 - x)^m} = \binom{n - 1}{m - 1} \quad (m \ge 1).\]
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: The \(m = 0\) Boundary Valuation Trap in Kernel K4

What if \(m = 0\) in \(S(n, 0) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \binom{n+k}{2k} \binom{2k}{k} \frac{(-1)^k}{k+1}\)?

  • The Trap: Blindly plugging \(m = 0\) into \(\binom{n-1}{m-1} = \binom{n-1}{-1} = 0\) claims \(S(n, 0) = 0\) for all \(n \ge 0\). Yet for \(n = 0, m = 0\), the \(k = 0\) term is \(\binom{0}{0}\binom{0}{0}\frac{1}{1} = 1 \neq 0\)!
  • Why It Happens: Actually, Kernel K4 at \(m = 0\) still holds (\(\sum_{n \ge 0} \binom{n+k}{2k} x^n = \frac{x^k}{(1-x)^{2k+1}}\) for all \(k \ge 0\)), so \(F_0(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x} \cdot (1 - x) = 1 = x^0\)! The mistake is in step 3: for \(m = 0\), \([x^n] \frac{x^0}{(1-x)^0} = [x^n] 1 = [n = 0]\), whereas the negative-binomial formula \([x^{n-m}](1-x)^{-m} = \binom{n-1}{m-1}\) is only valid for \(m \ge 1\)!
  • Remedy: Always check the exponent of \((1 - x)^{-m}\) before applying \(\binom{m + r - 1}{m - 1}\): when \(m = 0\), \((1 - x)^0 = 1\) has \([x^n] 1 = [n = 0]\).

Engine 2: Egorychev’s Coefficient-Extraction Method in \(K[[u]]\)

What if the free parameter \(n\) appears in two or more binomial coefficients at once—such as \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}(-1)^k\) or \(\sum_{k} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k}\binom{2n-2k}{n-1}\)—so that Snake Oil cannot isolate \(n\) into a single factor?

Georgy Egorychev developed a systematic method (originally phrased via complex Cauchy contour integrals \(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint \frac{(1+u)^A}{u^{B+1}} du\), and translated into pure competitive-programming formal power series algebra by Elegia and EI) that replaces all but one binomial coefficient by coefficient extraction operators \([u^B]\) in an auxiliary variable \(u\), swaps \([u^B]\) outside \(\sum_k\), and collapses the \(k\)-sum via the binomial or geometric series!

NoteThe 6-Rule Egorychev Substitution Dictionary in \(K[[u]]\)

Let \(A \ge 0\) and \(B \in \mathbb{Z}\) be integers. In the formal power series ring \(K[[u]]\):

Rule ID Binomial Expression Formal Power Series Representation When to Choose This Encoding (Diagnostic Signal)
E1 (Fixed-Upper) \(\displaystyle\binom{A}{B}\) \(\displaystyle[u^B] (1 + u)^A\) Use when the upper index \(A\) does not depend on \(k\) (or is linear in \(k\)), and \(B\) is a linear function of \(k\) such as \(k\), \(n - k\), or \(m - 2k\). Automatically vanishes for \(B < 0\) or \(B > A\)!

Look closely at the contrast between Rule E4 and Rule E5:

  • If you keep one factor \(\binom{n}{k}\) outside the \([u^m]\) operator and convert the remaining binomials into \([u^m] G(u) W(u)^k\), then \(\binom{n}{k}\) naturally restricts \(k\) to \(0 \le k \le n\), so \(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} W(u)^k = (1 + W(u))^n\) is an exact finite polynomial sum! You do not even need \(\operatorname{val}(W(u)) \ge 1\); \(W(u)\) can even contain \(u^{-1}\) (such as \(W(u) = -\frac{1+u}{u}\)), because \(\binom{n}{k}\) is only summed over \(0 \le k \le n\).
  • If instead you convert all binomials into \([u^{\dots}]\) and sum \(\sum_{k \ge 0} W(u)^k = \frac{1}{1 - W(u)}\), you must ensure that for all \(k > n\), the extracted coefficient \([u^{\dots}]\) of the original summand is identically \(0\)! The cleanest way to guarantee this is to use Rule E1 on the factor whose lower index is \(n - k\) (so for \(k > n\), the exponent \(n - k < 0\) has coefficient \(0\) in \((1+u)^A\)).

Worked Derivation 3: Evaluating \(\sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} \binom{n+k}{k}\) via Egorychev Extraction

Problem: For any integer \(n \ge 0\), evaluate \[S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} \binom{n+k}{k}.\]

  • Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    • Both \(\binom{n}{k}\) and \(\binom{n+k}{k}\) contain the free parameter \(n\), so Snake Oil on \(n\) does not apply directly.
    • Keep \(\binom{n}{k}\) intact for Rule E4 (\(\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} W(u)^k = (1 + W(u))^n\)).
    • How should we encode the second factor \(\binom{n+k}{k}\)? Notice that its upper index is \(n + k\) and its lower index is \(k\), so their difference is \((n + k) - k = n\), which is independent of \(k\)!
    • By symmetry \(\binom{n+k}{k} = \binom{n+k}{n}\), the lower index is now \(n\) (independent of \(k\)!), so applying Rule E1 gives \[\binom{n+k}{k} = \binom{n+k}{n} = [u^n] (1 + u)^{n + k} = [u^n] \Big((1 + u)^n \cdot (1 + u)^k\Big).\] Notice how [u^n] has zero \(k\)-dependence—we don’t even need an index shift!
  • Single-Step Verbalized Derivation: \[\begin{aligned} S_n &= \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} \binom{n+k}{n} && \text{(symmetry }\binom{n+k}{k} = \binom{n+k}{n}\text{ since }n, k \ge 0\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} [u^n] (1 + u)^{n+k} && \text{(Rule E1: }\binom{n+k}{n} = [u^n](1+u)^{n+k}\text{)} \\ &= [u^n] (1 + u)^n \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} \big(-(1 + u)\big)^k && \text{(linearity of }[u^n]\text{ and factoring }(1+u)^{n+k}\text{)} \\ &= [u^n] (1 + u)^n \Big(1 - (1 + u)\Big)^n && \text{(Rule E4: Binomial Theorem }\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} W^k = (1+W)^n\text{)} \\ &= [u^n] (1 + u)^n (-u)^n && \text{(simplifying }1 - (1+u) = -u\text{)} \\ &= (-1)^n [u^0] (1 + u)^n = (-1)^n. && \text{(Rule E3 shift }[u^n] u^n F(u) = [u^0] F(u)\text{)} \end{aligned}\]

Worked Derivation 4: Evaluating \(\sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} \binom{2n - 2k}{n - 1}\) via Egorychev

Problem: Given \(n \ge 1\), evaluate \(T_n = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} \binom{2n - 2k}{n - 1}\).

  • Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    • Keep \(\binom{n}{k}\) intact for Rule E4.
    • Encode \(\binom{2n - 2k}{n - 1}\) via Rule E1: since the lower index \(n - 1 \ge 0\) is independent of \(k\), \[\binom{2n - 2k}{n - 1} = [u^{n-1}] (1 + u)^{2n - 2k}.\] Specifically, for all \(0 \le k \le n\), the upper index satisfies \(2n - 2k \ge 0\); even when \(k > \lfloor \frac{n+1}{2} \rfloor\) so that \(0 \le 2n - 2k < n - 1\), the polynomial \((1 + u)^{2n - 2k}\) has degree \(2n - 2k < n - 1\), so \([u^{n-1}](1 + u)^{2n - 2k} = 0 = \binom{2n - 2k}{n - 1}\) holds automatically for every \(0 \le k \le n\) without exception!
  • Single-Step Verbalized Derivation: \[\begin{aligned} T_n &= \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} [u^{n-1}] (1 + u)^{2n - 2k} && \text{(Rule E1; valid for all }0 \le k \le n\text{ since }2n-2k \ge 0\text{)} \\ &= [u^{n-1}] (1 + u)^{2n} \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} \big(-(1 + u)^{-2}\big)^k && \text{(pulling }[u^{n-1}](1+u)^{2n}\text{ outside }\sum_k\text{)} \\ &= [u^{n-1}] (1 + u)^{2n} \left(1 - \frac{1}{(1 + u)^2}\right)^n && \text{(Rule E4: Binomial Theorem over }0 \le k \le n\text{)} \\ &= [u^{n-1}] \Big((1 + u)^2 - 1\Big)^n && \text{(absorbing }(1+u)^{2n}\text{ into the }n\text{-th power)} \\ &= [u^{n-1}] \big(u^2 + 2u\big)^n = [u^{n-1}] u^n (2 + u)^n = 0. && \text{(since }\operatorname{val}(u^n(2+u)^n) = n > n - 1\text{)} \end{aligned}\] Look how clean that is: \((u^2 + 2u)^n = u^n (2 + u)^n\) starts at degree \(u^n\), so its \([u^{n-1}]\) coefficient is \(0\) for every \(n \ge 1\)! (And if the lower index were \(n\) instead of \(n - 1\), \([u^n] u^n(2 + u)^n = [u^0](2 + u)^n = 2^n\).)

6.3 Polynomial Taylor Shift \(F(x+c)\), Generalized \(\alpha\)-Euler Transform & Failure Mode #14

Polynomial Taylor Shift \(\mathcal{T}_c: F(x) \mapsto F(x + c)\) in \(O(d \log d)\)

Plain-Language Introduction & Combinatorial Question

Suppose we have a polynomial of degree \(d\), written in the standard monomial basis: \[F(x) = \sum_{n=0}^d f_n x^n \in K[x].\] Given a shift constant \(c \in K\), the Polynomial Taylor Shift \(\mathcal{T}_c F\) is the polynomial \(G(x) = F(x + c) = \sum_{k=0}^d g_k x^k\) obtained by replacing \(x\) with \(x + c\) and expanding back into powers of \(x\).

Combinatorial Question Answered by \(F(x + c)\): Suppose \(f_n = [x^n]F(x)\) is the total weight of configurations of size \(n\). In each configuration of size \(n\), if we select a subset of \(k\) items to keep as “active” (\(x^k\)) and color each of the remaining \(n - k\) items with one of \(c\) colors (\(c^{n-k}\)), what is the total weight \(g_k = [x^k]F(x+c)\) of configurations leaving exactly \(k\) active items? \[g_k = \sum_{n=k}^d \binom{n}{k} f_n c^{n-k} \qquad (0 \le k \le d).\]

This exact sum appears across competitive programming in three major disguises:

  1. Binomial Inclusion–Exclusion (至少 k 个 \(\leftrightarrow\) 恰好 k 个): If \(f_n\) counts ways to choose \(n\) guaranteed violations and extend arbitrarily, then the number of ways with exactly \(k\) violations is \(g_k = \sum_{n=k}^d (-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k} f_n = [x^k] F(x - 1)\)—which is simply a Taylor shift by \(c = -1\)! (We will exploit this extensively in Chapter 08.)
  2. Eigenbasis Diagonalization of Pascal Transition Matrices: When a Markov chain or linear operator acts on polynomials via \(x \mapsto x + 1\) or \(x \mapsto \frac{x + 1}{2}\), shifting the origin to the fixed point via \(F(x \pm 1)\) diagonalizes the transition matrix into pure scalar powers (as in Adv-1: CF 923E — Perpetual Subtraction in Section 6.5).
  3. Rising Factorial Doubling: To compute \(x^{\overline{2m}} = x^{\overline{m}} \cdot (x + m)^{\overline{m}}\) in \(O(m \log m)\) time, we evaluate \(P_m(x + m)\) from \(P_m(x) = x^{\overline{m}}\) via a Taylor shift by \(c = m\) (Section 6.4).
ImportantLocal Algebraic Hypothesis for \(O(d \log d)\) Taylor Shift

To decouple \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) into a single convolution, we divide by \(k!\) and \((n-k)!\) for \(0 \le k \le n \le d\). Therefore, the \(O(d \log d)\) NTT Taylor shift requires \(d! \in K^\times\) (over \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\), this means \(p > d\)). When \(p \le d\), see the Characteristic-\(p\) Lucas / SOS DP Fallback callout below.

Diagnostic Signal, Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad & Single-Step Derivation

  • Diagnostic Signal: You need \(g_k = \sum_{n=k}^d \binom{n}{k} f_n c^{n-k}\) for all \(0 \le k \le d\) simultaneously.

  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:

    1. Apply the Golden Rule of Index Alignment to \(g_k = \sum_{n=k}^d \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} f_n c^{n-k}\).
    2. Pull \(\frac{1}{k!}\) outside the sum: \(k!\, g_k = \sum_{n=k}^d (n! f_n) \cdot \frac{c^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\).
    3. Notice the indices: \(u_n = n! f_n\) has index \(n\), and \(v_{n-k} = \frac{c^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\) has index \(n - k\). Since \(n - (n - k) = k\), this is a Regime 2 Constant-Difference Cross-Correlation from Section 6.1!
    4. Reverse \(u_n\) into \(\widetilde{U}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^d (n! f_n) x^{d - n}\) and multiply by \(V(x) = \sum_{j=0}^d \frac{c^j}{j!} x^j\).

  • Single-Step Verbalized Derivation: \[\begin{aligned} F(x + c) &= \sum_{n=0}^d f_n (x + c)^n && \text{(definition of }F(x+c)\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{n=0}^d f_n \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} x^k c^{n-k} && \text{(Binomial Theorem on }(x+c)^n\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^d \frac{x^k}{k!} \sum_{n=k}^d (n!\, f_n) \frac{c^{n-k}}{(n - k)!} && \text{(swapping }\sum_n, \sum_k\text{ and splitting }\binom{n}{k}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^d \frac{x^k}{k!} [z^{d - k}] \left(\sum_{n=0}^d (n!\, f_n) z^{d - n}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^d \frac{c^j}{j!} z^j\right). && \text{(reversing }n \mapsto d - n\text{; exponent }(d-n)+(n-k) = d-k\text{)} \end{aligned}\] Note the two equivalent indexing conventions for the reversal:

    • If we reverse the factorial-scaled coefficients \(u_n = n! f_n\) into \(A_{\text{rev}}(z) = \sum_{r=0}^d (d - r)! f_{d - r} z^r\) and convolve with \(B(z) = \sum_{j=0}^d \frac{c^j}{j!} z^j\), then for \(r = d - n\) and \(j = n - k\), the exponent sum is \(r + j = (d - n) + (n - k) = d - k\). Hence: \[g_k = \frac{1}{k!} [z^{d - k}] \big(A_{\text{rev}}(z) B(z)\big) \qquad (0 \le k \le d).\]
TipHand-Checkable Trace (\(d = 3, c = 2\)): Taylor Shift Step-by-Step

Let \(F(x) = 1 + 2x + 3x^2 + 4x^3\) (\(d = 3\)) and \(c = 2\). Directly, \[F(x+2) = 1 + 2(x+2) + 3(x+2)^2 + 4(x+2)^3 = 49 + 62x + 27x^2 + 4x^3.\] Let us verify the convolution formula \(g_k = \frac{1}{k!}[z^{3-k}](A_{\text{rev}}(z)B(z))\):

  1. Compute \(n! f_n\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\): \((0!\cdot 1, 1!\cdot 2, 2!\cdot 3, 3!\cdot 4) = (1, 2, 6, 24)\).
  2. Reverse into \(A_{\text{rev}}(z) = 24 + 6z + 2z^2 + 1z^3\).
  3. Form \(B(z) = \sum_{j=0}^3 \frac{2^j}{j!} z^j = 1 + 2z + 2z^2 + \frac{4}{3}z^3\).
  4. Multiply \(C(z) = A_{\text{rev}}(z) B(z)\) up to \(z^3\):
    • \([z^0]C = 24 \cdot 1 = 24 \implies g_3 = \frac{1}{3!} [z^{3-3}]C = \frac{24}{6} = 4\).
    • \([z^1]C = 24 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 1 = 54 \implies g_2 = \frac{1}{2!} [z^{3-2}]C = \frac{54}{2} = 27\).
    • \([z^2]C = 24 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 = 62 \implies g_1 = \frac{1}{1!} [z^{3-1}]C = \frac{62}{1} = 62\).
    • \([z^3]C = 24 \cdot \frac{4}{3} + 6 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 = 32 + 12 + 4 + 1 = 49 \implies g_0 = \frac{1}{0!} [z^{3-0}]C = 49\). Thus \(F(x+2) = 49 + 62x + 27x^2 + 4x^3\), recovered in one convolution!
NoteFirst Principles: Umbral Calculus as a Linear Functional on \(K[x]\)

In classical combinatorics, umbral calculus ([EN] umbral evaluation / [ZH] 阴算子 / 符号幂代换) is often written via the shorthand \((a + b)^n \mapsto \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} a_k b^{n-k}\), where exponents \(a^k\) are “lowered to subscripts” \(a_k\) after expansion. There is zero mystery in this notation: it is simply the application of a \(K\)-linear functional \(L_a : K[u] \to K\) defined on the basis of monomials by \(L_a(u^k) := a_k\) (or on the falling-factorial basis by \(L(x^{\underline{k}}) := y^k\)). Because \(L_a\) is linear, applying \(L_a\) to any polynomial identity in \(u\)—such as \((u + 1)^n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} u^k\) or \(x^n = \sum_{k=0}^n \left\{ \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right\} x^{\underline{k}}\)—immediately produces a valid scalar identity \(L_a((u+1)^n) = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} a_k\) without any hand-waving.

NoteAlgorithm Contract: poly_taylor_shift(F, c)
  • Input: Polynomial array \(F = [f_0, f_1, \dots, f_d] \in K^{d+1}\) of degree \(d \ge 0\), shift scalar \(c \in K\).
  • Algebraic Precondition: \(d! \in K^\times\) (over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), requires \(p > d\)).
  • Output: Array \(G = [g_0, g_1, \dots, g_d] \in K^{d+1}\) such that \(\sum_{k=0}^d g_k x^k = F(x + c)\).
  • Index & Loop Contract:
    1. If \(c = 0\), return \(F\) immediately.
    2. Precompute factorials \(\text{fact}[i] = i!\) and inverse factorials \(\text{ifact}[i] = (i!)^{-1}\) for \(0 \le i \le d\).
    3. Build \(A_{\text{rev}}[r] = f_{d - r} \cdot \text{fact}[d - r]\) and \(B[j] = c^j \cdot \text{ifact}[j]\) for \(0 \le r, j \le d\) (with \(0^0 := 1\)).
    4. Compute \(C = \text{mul}(A_{\text{rev}}, B)\) (1 NTT of length \(L = 2^{\lceil \log_2(2d+1) \rceil}\)).
    5. Set \(g_k = C[d - k] \cdot \text{ifact}[k]\) for \(0 \le k \le d\).
  • Invariant: Every pair \((n, k)\) with \(0 \le k \le n \le d\) maps bijectively to \(r = d - n \in [0, d]\) and \(j = n - k \in [0, d]\) satisfying \(r + j = d - k\), so \(C[d - k]\) accumulates \((n! f_n)\frac{c^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}\) exactly once for each \(n \in [k, d]\).
  • Complexity: \(O(d \log d)\) time (1 polynomial multiplication of size \(d+1\)) and \(O(d)\) auxiliary space.
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Characteristic \(p \le d\) in Taylor Shift (Lucas / SOS DP Fallback)
  • The Pitfall: When working over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) with a small prime \(p \le d\) (for instance, \(p = 2\) or \(p = 3\)), \(p! = 0 \in \mathbb{F}_p\), so \(\text{ifact}[k]\) does not exist for \(k \ge p\) and the NTT convolution algorithm crashes with division by zero.
  • The Algebraic Remedy: By Lucas’s Theorem, in \(\mathbb{F}_p\) we have \(\binom{n}{k} \equiv \prod_{i \ge 0} \binom{n_i}{k_i} \pmod p\), where \(n = \sum n_i p^i\) and \(k = \sum k_i p^i\) are the base-\(p\) digit expansions of \(n\) and \(k\). Furthermore, \(c^{n - k} = \prod_{i \ge 0} (c^{p^i})^{n_i - k_i}\) whenever \(0 \le k_i \le n_i < p\) for all digits \(i\) (and if \(k_i > n_i\) for any digit \(i\), \(\binom{n_i}{k_i} = 0\)).
  • Therefore, when \(p \le d\), \(g_k = \sum_{n=k}^d \binom{n}{k} f_n c^{n-k} \bmod p\) is a Sum-Over-Subsets (SOS) / Digit DP in base \(p\): iterating over each base-\(p\) digit position \(i = 0, 1, \dots, \lfloor \log_p d \rfloor\) and transitioning \(k_i \le n_i\) in \(O(p)\) per state computes all \(g_0, \dots, g_d\) in \(O(d \cdot p \log_p d) = O(d \log d)\) operations for fixed \(p\) without a single division!

Projective Möbius Transformations & The 4-Step Bruhat Decomposition

More generally, suppose \(g = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & e \end{pmatrix} \in \operatorname{GL}_2(K)\) is a \(2 \times 2\) matrix with \(ae - bc \neq 0\), and we want to compute the degree-\(d\) projective Möbius transformation \[(\mathcal{M}_g F)(x) = (cx + e)^d F\!\left(\frac{ax + b}{cx + e}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^d f_n (ax + b)^n (cx + e)^{d - n}.\] Naive expansion takes \(O(d^2)\) time, and Divide-and-Conquer tree multiplication takes \(O(d \log^2 d)\) time. Can we compute \((\mathcal{M}_g F)(x)\) in \(O(d \log d)\) time using at most 2 NTTs?

Yes! Just as every invertible \(2 \times 2\) matrix decomposes into elementary row/column operations (Bruhat decomposition), any projective Möbius transformation decomposes into three primitive polynomial operations on arrays of length \(d + 1\):

  1. Diagonal Scaling \(\mathcal{D}_\lambda\) (\(\lambda \in K^\times\)): \((\mathcal{D}_\lambda F)(x) = F(\lambda x) = \sum_{n=0}^d (f_n \lambda^n) x^n\) in \(O(d)\) time.
  2. Projective Reversal \(\mathcal{R}\): \((\mathcal{R} F)(x) = x^d F(1/x) = \sum_{n=0}^d f_{d - n} x^n\) (reversing the coefficient array in place) in \(O(d)\) time.
  3. Taylor Shift \(\mathcal{T}_u\) (\(u \in K\)): \((\mathcal{T}_u F)(x) = F(x + u)\) in \(O(d \log d)\) time (1 NTT).

  • Case 1 (\(c = 0\), Affine Transformation): Since \(ae \neq 0\), \(e \in K^\times\), so \[(0\cdot x + e)^d F\!\left(\frac{ax + b}{e}\right) = e^d F\!\left(\frac{a}{e}x + \frac{b}{e}\right) = e^d (\mathcal{D}_{a/e} \circ \mathcal{T}_{b/e} F)(x)\] in 1 Taylor Shift (\(O(d \log d)\)).

  • Case 2 (\(c \neq 0\), General Möbius Transformation): Perform polynomial division of \(ax + b\) by \(cx + e\): \[\frac{ax + b}{cx + e} = \frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc - ae}{c(cx + e)}.\] Putting \(\alpha = \frac{a}{c}\) and \(\beta = \frac{bc - ae}{c}\), we write \((cx + e)^d F\!\left(\alpha + \frac{\beta}{cx + e}\right)\) as a composition of 4 steps:

    1. Shift by \(\alpha = a/c\): Compute \(F_1(y) = F(y + \alpha)\) via 1 Taylor Shift (\(O(d \log d)\)). Now we need \((cx + e)^d F_1\!\left(\frac{\beta}{cx + e}\right)\).
    2. Scale by \(\beta\) and Reverse: Compute \(F_2(z) = z^d F_1(\beta / z) = \sum_{n=0}^d \big([y^n]F_1(y) \cdot \beta^n\big) z^{d - n}\) in \(O(d)\) time.
    3. Substitute \(z = cx + e\): Since \(F_2(z) = z^d F_1(\beta / z)\), evaluating \(F_2\) at \(z = cx + e\) gives \((cx + e)^d F_1\!\left(\frac{\beta}{cx + e}\right)\)! We compute \(F_3(w) = F_2(w + e)\) via 1 Taylor Shift (\(O(d \log d)\), or 0 NTTs if \(e = 0\)!).
    4. Scale \(w = cx\): Return \(F_4(x) = F_3(cx) = \sum_{k=0}^d \big([w^k]F_3(w) \cdot c^k\big) x^k\) in \(O(d)\) time.

Notice that this 4-step pipeline never divides by \(e\), so it works seamlessly even when \(e = 0\)! In particular, the Krawtchouk Transform \((1 - x)^d F\!\left(\frac{1 + x}{1 - x}\right)\) (used in Walsh–Hadamard / hypercube coding theory and Int-2: KUPC 2019 K — One or All in Section 6.5) takes just 2 Taylor shifts: \(F(y) \xrightarrow{y \gets y - 1} F_1(y) \xrightarrow{\text{scale }2\text{ & rev}} z^d F_1(2/z) \xrightarrow{z \gets z + 1} F_3(w) \xrightarrow{w \gets -x} F_3(-x)\).

The Generalized \(\alpha\)-Euler Transform & Canonical Failure Mode #14

Closely related to the projective Möbius action is the Generalized \(\alpha\)-Euler Transform on truncated formal power series: given \(F(y) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} f_k y^k \in K[[y]]/(y^N)\) and parameters \(a, c, \alpha \in K\), compute the first \(N\) coefficients of \[G(x) = \frac{1}{(1 - cx)^\alpha} F\!\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right) \bmod x^N.\]

Let us expand \(G(x)\) in powers of \(x\) using the negative-binomial series \((1 - cx)^{-(k + \alpha)} = \sum_{n \ge k} \binom{n + \alpha - 1}{n - k} c^{n - k} x^{n - k}\): \[\begin{aligned} [x^n] G(x) &= \sum_{k=0}^n f_k a^k [x^{n-k}] \frac{1}{(1 - cx)^{k + \alpha}} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^n f_k a^k c^{n-k} \binom{n + \alpha - 1}{n - k}. \end{aligned}\] How do we decouple \(\binom{n + \alpha - 1}{n - k}\) into a factor of \(n\), a factor of \(k\), and a factor of \(n - k\)? Using the rising factorial \(\alpha^{\overline{m}} = \alpha(\alpha + 1)\cdots(\alpha + m - 1)\): \[\binom{n + \alpha - 1}{n - k} = \frac{(\alpha + k)(\alpha + k + 1)\cdots(\alpha + n - 1)}{(n - k)!} = \frac{\alpha^{\overline{n}}}{\alpha^{\overline{k}} \cdot (n - k)!}.\] Thus, provided \(\alpha^{\overline{k}} \in K^\times\) for all \(0 \le k \le n < N\), we have the 1-NTT Cauchy convolution formula: \[[x^n] G(x) = c^n \alpha^{\overline{n}} \sum_{k=0}^n \left(\frac{f_k (a/c)^k}{\alpha^{\overline{k}}}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{(n - k)!} \qquad (0 \le n < N).\]

WarningCanonical Failure Mode #14: The Negative-Integer \(\alpha = -d\) Pole Trap in the Generalized \(\alpha\)-Euler Transform

  • The Trap: Suppose you call the 1-NTT \(\alpha\)-Euler Transform formula above when \(\alpha = -d\) is a non-positive integer in \(\{-(N-2), \dots, -1, 0\}\). Specifically, this happens whenever you compute the truncated polynomial expansion \[(1 - cx)^d F\!\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right) \bmod x^N \qquad \text{with } 0 \le d \le N - 2\] (or when \(\alpha = 0\), i.e., pure substitution \(F\!\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right) \bmod x^N\)!). Look at the rising factorial \(\alpha^{\overline{k}} = (-d)(-d+1)\cdots(-d + k - 1)\) in the denominator: at \(k = d + 1\), the factor \((-d + d) = 0\) appears, so \(\alpha^{\overline{k}} = 0\) for every \(k \ge d + 1\)! Dividing \(f_k (a/c)^k\) by \(\alpha^{\overline{k}}\) divides by zero at \(k = d + 1\), even though \(\binom{n - d - 1}{n - k}\) is a perfectly well-defined integer!

  • Why the Naive Formula Fails Algebraically: For \(0 \le k \le d\), \((1 - cx)^{d - k}\) is a polynomial of degree \(d - k\), whose coefficients vanish for \(n > d\); whereas for \(k \ge d + 1\), \((1 - cx)^{-(k - d)}\) is an infinite negative-binomial series. The single ratio \(\frac{(-d)^{\overline{n}}}{(-d)^{\overline{k}}}\) attempts to pass through the zero \((-d)^{\overline{d+1}} = 0\) separating the polynomial regime (\(k \le d\)) from the negative-binomial regime (\(k \ge d + 1\)).

  • The Exact Remedy (Head–Tail Split \(F = F_{\le d} + y^{d+1} F_{>d}\)): Whenever \(\alpha = -d \in \{-(N-2), \dots, -1, 0\}\), split \(F(y)\) at degree \(d\) into a head polynomial \(F_{\le d}(y) = \sum_{k=0}^d f_k y^k\) and a shifted tail \(F_{>d}(y) = \sum_{j=0}^{N - d - 2} f_{d + 1 + j} y^j\): \[F(y) = F_{\le d}(y) + y^{d+1} F_{>d}(y).\] Multiplying by \((1 - cx)^d\) and substituting \(y = \frac{ax}{1 - cx}\) splits \(G(x) \bmod x^N\) into two completely pole-free pieces: \[G(x) = \underbrace{(1 - cx)^d F_{\le d}\!\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right)}_{\text{Head } G_{\text{head}}(x):\text{ exact degree-}\le d\text{ polynomial!}} + \underbrace{(ax)^{d+1} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - cx} F_{>d}\!\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right)}_{\text{Tail } G_{\text{tail}}(x):\text{ }\alpha'\text{-Euler Transform with }\alpha' = 1 > 0!}.\]

    1. Head Piece (\(k \le d\)): \(G_{\text{head}}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^d f_k (ax)^k (1 - cx)^{d - k}\) has degree \(\le d\) and is computed in 1 Taylor shift (or directly via \(k! \in K^\times\) since \(\binom{d-k}{n-k} = \frac{(d-k)!}{(n-k)!(d-n)!}\) has no zeros for \(0 \le k \le n \le d\)!): \[[x^n] G_{\text{head}}(x) = \frac{(-c)^n}{(d - n)!} \sum_{k=0}^n \Big(f_k (a / (-c))^k (d - k)!\Big) \frac{1}{(n - k)!} \qquad (0 \le n \le d),\] and \([x^n] G_{\text{head}}(x) = 0\) for all \(n > d\)!
    2. Tail Piece (\(k \ge d + 1\)): In \(G_{\text{tail}}(x)\), the factor \(\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right)^{d+1}\) cancels \((1 - cx)^d\) and leaves \((1 - cx)^{-1}\), shifting the Euler parameter from \(\alpha = -d \le 0\) to \(\alpha' = 1 > 0\), where \((1)^{\overline{m}} = m! \neq 0\) has zero poles! Calling the standard \(\alpha' = 1\) Euler Transform on \(F_{>d}\) and shifting by \((ax)^{d+1}\) computes \(G_{\text{tail}}(x) \bmod x^N\) in 1 NTT!
TipHand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4, d = 1, a = c = 1\)): Watching the Head–Tail Split Defeat Failure Mode #14

Suppose \(N = 4\), \(\alpha = -1\) (\(d = 1\)), \(a = c = 1\), and \(F(y) = f_0 + f_1 y + f_2 y^2 + f_3 y^3\). We want \[G(x) = (1 - x)^1 F\!\left(\frac{x}{1 - x}\right) \bmod x^4.\]

  • Why the naive formula crashes: \(\alpha^{\overline{k}} = (-1)^{\overline{k}}\) has \((-1)^{\overline{0}} = 1\), \((-1)^{\overline{1}} = -1\), and \((-1)^{\overline{2}} = (-1)(0) = 0\)! So \(f_2\) and \(f_3\) cannot be divided by \((-1)^{\overline{k}}\).
  • Applying the Head–Tail Split at \(d = 1\):
    • Head (\(k \le 1\)): \(G_{\text{head}}(x) = (1 - x)\left(f_0 + f_1 \frac{x}{1 - x}\right) = f_0(1 - x) + f_1 x = f_0 + (f_1 - f_0)x + 0x^2 + 0x^3\).
    • Tail (\(k \ge 2\), \(\alpha' = 1\)): \(F_{>1}(y) = f_2 + f_3 y\), and \[G_{\text{tail}}(x) = x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{1 - x} F_{>1}\!\left(\frac{x}{1 - x}\right) = x^2 \left(\frac{f_2}{1 - x} + \frac{f_3 x}{(1 - x)^2}\right) \equiv f_2 x^2 + (f_2 + f_3) x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\]
    • Total \(G(x) \bmod x^4\): \(f_0 + (f_1 - f_0)x + f_2 x^2 + (f_2 + f_3)x^3\), computed with zero divisions by zero!

  • Preconditions: \(\max(d, N - 1)! \in K^\times\) (over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), requires \(p > \max(d, N - 1)\)).
  • Postconditions:
    • poly_moebius_transform returns \([x^k] (cx + e)^d F\!\left(\frac{ax+b}{cx+e}\right)\) for \(0 \le k \le d\).
    • poly_alpha_euler_transform returns \([x^n] (1 - cx)^{-\alpha} F\!\left(\frac{ax}{1 - cx}\right)\) for \(0 \le n < N\).
  • Core Invariant:
    • In poly_moebius_transform when \(c \neq 0\), the 4-step Bruhat factorization \(\frac{ax+b}{cx+e} = \frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc - ae}{c(cx+e)}\) shifts by \(a/c\), scales by \(\frac{bc-ae}{c}\) and reverses, shifts by \(e\), and scales by \(c\), never dividing by \(e\).
    • In poly_alpha_euler_transform, if \(\alpha = -d \in \{-(N-2), \dots, -1, 0\}\), splitting \(F(y) = F_{\le d}(y) + y^{d+1} F_{>d}(y)\) isolates the degree-\(\le d\) head polynomial from the pole-free \(\alpha' = 1\) tail series before any division by \(\alpha^{\overline{k}}\) occurs.
  • Complexity: \(O(d \log d)\) time (at most 2 NTTs) for poly_moebius_transform; \(O(N \log N)\) time (at most 2 NTTs) and \(O(N)\) space for poly_alpha_euler_transform.

6.4 Stirling Numbers (\(S_1, S_2\)), Eulerian Numbers, Falling Factorial Polynomials (FFP) & \(O(d)\) Consecutive Lagrange Interpolation

Plain-Language Introduction: Why Standard Monomials \(k^m\) Fight Binomial Coefficients \(\binom{n}{k}\)

Why is a polynomial-weighted binomial sum like \(\sum_{k=0}^n k^m \binom{n}{k} x^k\) awkward to evaluate directly when \(n = 10^9\) and \(m = 2 \times 10^5\)? Because the monomial \(k^m = k \cdot k \cdots k\) and the binomial denominator \(k! = k(k-1)\cdots 1\) in \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) do not cancel cleanly: canceling one factor of \(k\) leaves \(k^{m-1}\) against \((k-1)!\), where the top is still \(k\) instead of \(k - 1\).

By contrast, the falling factorial of degree \(j\), \[k^{\underline{j}} := k(k - 1)(k - 2)\cdots(k - j + 1) = j! \binom{k}{j},\] is tailor-made to cancel the first \(j\) factors of \(k! = k^{\underline{j}} (k - j)!\). Similarly, the rising factorial of degree \(n\), \[x^{\overline{n}} := x(x + 1)(x + 2)\cdots(x + n - 1),\] encodes cycle decompositions and negative-binomial coefficients \(\binom{x + n - 1}{n} = \frac{x^{\overline{n}}}{n!}\).

The Stirling numbers of the first and second kinds are simply the change-of-basis matrices between the standard monomial basis \(\{1, x, x^2, \dots\}\) and the rising/falling factorial bases \(\{1, x^{\overline{1}}, x^{\overline{2}}, \dots\}\) and \(\{1, x^{\underline{1}}, x^{\underline{2}}, \dots\}\).

Combinatorial & Algebraic Foundations of Stirling Numbers \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) and \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\)

1. Unsigned Stirling Numbers of the First Kind \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right]\) (Cycles \(\leftrightarrow\) Rising Factorials)

  • Combinatorial Objects Counted:
    1. Disjoint Cycles: \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}\) is the number of permutations of \(\{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) having exactly \(k\) disjoint cycles.
    2. Prefix Maxima (Records) via the Rényi–Foata Bijection: Write each cycle of a permutation with its largest element first, and sort the cycles in increasing order of their largest elements; erasing the parentheses produces a bijection between permutations with \(k\) cycles and permutations with exactly \(k\) left-to-right maxima (elements strictly larger than all preceding elements).
  • Recurrence & Polynomial Identity: When inserting element \(n\) into a permutation of \(\{1, \dots, n-1\}\), either \(n\) forms its own \(1\)-cycle (contributing \(\begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k-1 \end{bmatrix}\)) or \(n\) is inserted immediately after any of the \(n - 1\) existing elements inside an existing cycle (contributing \((n - 1)\begin{bmatrix} n-1 \\ k \end{bmatrix}\)): \[\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k - 1 \end{bmatrix} + (n - 1) \begin{bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{bmatrix} \qquad (n \ge 1, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ k \end{bmatrix} = [k = 0]).\] Multiplying by \(x^k\) and summing over \(k\) shows that \(P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix} x^k\) satisfies \(P_n(x) = (x + n - 1) P_{n-1}(x)\), yielding the fundamental basis conversion: \[x^{\overline{n}} = x(x + 1)\cdots(x + n - 1) = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix} x^k, \qquad x^{\underline{n}} = (-1)^n (-x)^{\overline{n}} = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^{n-k} \begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix} x^k.\]

2. Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) (Set Partitions \(\leftrightarrow\) Falling Factorials)

  • Combinatorial Objects Counted:
    1. Unlabeled Set Partitions: \(\begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix}\) is the number of ways to partition a labeled set \(\{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) into \(k\) non-empty, unlabeled (indistinguishable) subsets.
    2. Surjections: Multiplying by \(k!\) labels the \(k\) subsets, so \(k! \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix}\) is the number of surjective functions from \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) onto \(\{1, \dots, k\}\).
  • Recurrence & Polynomial Identity: When placing element \(n\) into a partition of \(\{1, \dots, n-1\}\), either \(\{n\}\) forms a new singleton block (\(\begin{Bmatrix} n-1 \\ k-1 \end{Bmatrix}\) ways) or \(n\) joins one of the \(k\) existing blocks (\(k \begin{Bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{Bmatrix}\) ways): \[\begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} = \begin{Bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k - 1 \end{Bmatrix} + k \begin{Bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{Bmatrix} \qquad (n \ge 1, \quad \begin{Bmatrix} 0 \\ k \end{Bmatrix} = [k = 0]).\] Double-counting all \(x^n\) functions from \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) to \(\{1, \dots, x\}\) by the exact size \(k\) of their image (\(\binom{x}{k}\) ways to choose the \(k\)-element image in \(\{1, \dots, x\}\) times \(k! \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix}\) surjections onto it) proves the basis conversion: \[x^n = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} k! \binom{x}{k} = \sum_{k=0}^n \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} x^{\underline{k}}, \qquad x^n = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^{n-k} \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} x^{\overline{k}}.\]

The 4-Regime \(O(N \log N)\) Stirling Generation Dictionary

In competitive programming (\(N \le 2 \times 10^5\), prime \(p > N\)), you will frequently need an entire row (fixed \(n\), all \(0 \le k \le n\)) or an entire column (fixed \(k\), all \(k \le n \le N\)) of \(\begin{bmatrix} n \\ k \end{bmatrix}\) or \(\begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix}\). Never confuse the four regimes:

Regime Target Sequence Generating Function / Polynomial Identity \(O(N \log N)\) Algorithm & NTT Cost
1. \(S_2\) Row (stirling_2nd_row) \(\begin{Bmatrix} n \\ 0 \end{Bmatrix}, \dots, \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ n \end{Bmatrix}\) (fixed \(n\)) \(\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} = \sum_{j=0}^k \frac{j^n}{j!} \cdot \frac{(-1)^{k-j}}{(k - j)!}\) 1 NTT Convolution of \(A_j = \frac{j^n}{j!}\) and \(B_m = \frac{(-1)^m}{m!}\).
2. \(S_2\) Column (stirling_2nd_col) \(\begin{Bmatrix} k \\ k \end{Bmatrix}, \dots, \begin{Bmatrix} N \\ k \end{Bmatrix}\) (fixed \(k\)) \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^N \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} \frac{x^n}{n!} = \frac{(e^x - 1)^k}{k!}\) Valuation Shift + \(\ln/\exp\): set \(U(x) = \frac{e^x - 1}{x}\) (\([x^0]U = 1\)), compute \(\frac{x^k}{k!} \exp(k \ln U(x)) \bmod x^{N+1}\).

Why Do Regimes 2 and 4 work? (Symbolic Method + Valuation Shift)

By Chapter 04’s Labeled Symbolic Dictionary:

  • A partition of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(k\) non-empty blocks is an unordered SET of size \(k\) of non-empty labeled sets (\(\mathcal{B} = \operatorname{SET}_{\ge 1}(\mathcal{Z})\) with EGF \(B(x) = e^x - 1\)), so its EGF is \(\frac{(e^x - 1)^k}{k!}\). Extracting \([x^n/n!]\) from \((e^x - 1)^k = \sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j} e^{jx}\) also immediately proves the Regime 1 row formula \(\begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j} j^n\)!
  • Similarly, a permutation of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) with \(k\) disjoint cycles is an unordered SET of size \(k\) of directed cycles (\(\mathcal{C} = \operatorname{CYC}(\mathcal{Z})\) with EGF \(C(x) = \ln\frac{1}{1-x}\)), so its EGF is \(\frac{1}{k!}\left(\ln\frac{1}{1-x}\right)^k\).
  • Notice that both \(e^x - 1 = x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \dots\) and \(\ln\frac{1}{1-x} = x + \frac{x^2}{2} + \dots\) have valuation \(1\) (\([x^0] = 0, [x^1] = 1\)), so we cannot take \(\ln(e^x - 1)\) directly. Dividing by \(x\) first produces a unit \(U(x) = 1 + \frac{x}{2!} + \dots\) with \([x^0]U = 1\), allowing \(\exp(k \ln U(x)) \bmod x^{N - k + 1}\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time!

Why Does Regime 3 (stirling_1st_row) Run in \(O(n \log n)\) Instead of \(O(n \log^2 n)\)?

If we computed \(x^{\overline{n}} = x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)\) by a generic divide-and-conquer product tree (Chapter 01), it would take \(O(n \log^2 n)\) time. Instead, we exploit translation symmetry: the second half of the product \((x + m)(x + m + 1)\cdots(x + 2m - 1)\) is simply the first half \(P_m(x) = x(x+1)\cdots(x+m-1)\) shifted by \(c = m\): \[P_{2m}(x) = P_m(x) \cdot P_m(x + m), \qquad P_{2m+1}(x) = P_{2m}(x) \cdot (x + 2m).\] At each doubling step \(m \to 2m\), we perform 1 Taylor shift poly_taylor_shift(P_m, m) (\(O(m \log m)\)) and 1 polynomial multiplication (\(O(m \log m)\)). Because the work geometric-series sums over \(m, m/2, m/4, \dots\), the total time is \(T(n) = T(n/2) + O(n \log n) = O(n \log n)\)!

Falling-Factorial Polynomials (FFP), The Absorption Identity & Point-Value EGF

1. The Falling-Factorial Absorption Identity for Huge-\(n\) Binomial Sums

Combinatorial Question: How do we evaluate \(\sum_{k=0}^n P(k) \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k}\) when \(n \le 10^{18}\) is gigantic and \(P(k)\) is a polynomial of moderate degree \(m\)?

The key is the Falling-Factorial Absorption Identity: for all integers \(n, k, j \ge 0\), \[k^{\underline{j}} \binom{n}{k} = n^{\underline{j}} \binom{n - j}{k - j}.\]

  • Combinatorial Double-Count (Why Zero Divisions Are Needed!): Both sides count the number of ways to choose a committee of \(k\) people from \(n\) people, and then elect an ordered sequence of \(j\) distinct officers (President, VP, \(\dots\)) from within that committee:

    • Left-hand side: First choose the \(k\)-person committee from \(n\) people (\(\binom{n}{k}\) ways), then pick the \(j\) ordered officers from the \(k\) committee members (\(k^{\underline{j}}\) ways).
    • Right-hand side: First pick the \(j\) ordered officers directly from the \(n\) people (\(n^{\underline{j}}\) ways), then choose the remaining \(k - j\) ordinary committee members from the remaining \(n - j\) people (\(\binom{n-j}{k-j}\) ways). Because this is a bijection between finite sets, \(k^{\underline{j}}\binom{n}{k} = n^{\underline{j}}\binom{n-j}{k-j}\) holds in \(\mathbb{Z}\) and therefore modulo any integer \(M\) (even when \(M\) is composite and \(\gcd(j!, M) > 1\)!).

Suppose we expand our degree-\(m\) polynomial \(P(k)\) in the falling-factorial basis: \[P(k) = \sum_{j=0}^m c_j k^{\underline{j}}.\] (If \(P(k) = \sum_{i=0}^m a_i k^i\) is given in the monomial basis, then \(k^i = \sum_{j=0}^i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} k^{\underline{j}}\) immediately gives \(c_j = \sum_{i=j}^m a_i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix}\) with zero divisions!) Substituting \(P(k) = \sum_{j=0}^m c_j k^{\underline{j}}\) into the binomial sum collapses the \(O(n)\) sum over \(k\) into an \(O(m)\) sum over \(j\): \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^n P(k) \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k} &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} c_j \sum_{k=j}^n k^{\underline{j}} \binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k} && \text{(swapping }\sum_k, \sum_j\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} c_j n^{\underline{j}} a^j \sum_{k=j}^n \binom{n - j}{k - j} a^{k - j} b^{(n - j) - (k - j)} && \text{(Absorption Identity)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} c_j n^{\underline{j}} a^j (a + b)^{n - j}. && \text{(Binomial Theorem on }r = k - j\text{)} \end{aligned}\] In particular, when \(a = p\) and \(b = 1 - p\) (the binomial distribution \(\operatorname{Binomial}(n, p)\) where \(a + b = 1\)), the factor \((a + b)^{n - j} = 1^{n - j} = 1\) disappears completely, leaving \(\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} c_j n^{\underline{j}} p^j\)!

2. The Point-Value EGF Theorem for Falling-Factorial Polynomials (下降幂多项式)

What if \(m \le 2 \times 10^5\) is too large for \(O(m^2)\) Stirling triangle precomputation, or \(P\) is given by its consecutive point values \(P(0), P(1), \dots, P(m)\)?

Let \(P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^d c_k x^{\underline{k}}\) be a polynomial in the falling-factorial basis, let \(C(t) = \sum_{k=0}^d c_k t^k\) be the ordinary generating function of its falling-factorial coefficients, and let \(\widehat{P}(t) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty P(m) \frac{t^m}{m!}\) be the exponential generating function of its point evaluations on \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\).

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Falling-Factorial Basis & Point-Value EGF
  • Diagnostic Signal: You are multiplying polynomials expressed in the falling-factorial basis \(P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^d c_k x^{\underline{k}}\), converting between falling-factorial coefficients \(c_k\) and consecutive point evaluations \(P(0), P(1), \dots, P(d)\), or evaluating \(\sum_{m \ge 0} P(m) \frac{t^m}{m!}\) for \(d \le 2 \times 10^5\).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Why do falling factorials \(m^{\underline{k}} = \frac{m!}{(m-k)!}\) pair so naturally with the exponential generating function weight \(\frac{t^m}{m!}\)? Write down the single basis term \(m^{\underline{k}} \frac{t^m}{m!}\): the numerator \(m!\) in \(m^{\underline{k}}\) cancels the EGF denominator \(m!\), leaving \(\frac{t^m}{(m-k)!} = t^k \frac{t^{m-k}}{(m-k)!}\)! Summing over \(m \ge k\) turns the shifted index \(r = m - k\) into a pure factor of \(e^t = \sum_{r \ge 0} \frac{t^r}{r!}\), decoupling the coefficient OGF \(C(t) = \sum_{k=0}^d c_k t^k\) from \(e^t\) as the single product \(\widehat{P}(t) = C(t) e^t\).

Theorem (Point-Value EGF of a Falling-Factorial Polynomial): \[\widehat{P}(t) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty P(m) \frac{t^m}{m!} = C(t) e^t, \qquad \text{and inversely} \qquad C(t) = \widehat{P}(t) e^{-t} \pmod{t^{d+1}}.\]

  • Single-Step Verbalized Proof: \[\begin{aligned} \widehat{P}(t) &= \sum_{m=0}^\infty \left(\sum_{k=0}^d c_k m^{\underline{k}}\right) \frac{t^m}{m!} && \text{(substituting }P(m) = \sum_{k=0}^d c_k m^{\underline{k}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^d c_k t^k \sum_{m=k}^\infty \frac{m^{\underline{k}}}{m!} t^{m - k} && \text{(since }m^{\underline{k}} = 0\text{ for }0 \le m < k\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^d c_k t^k \sum_{m=k}^\infty \frac{t^{m - k}}{(m - k)!} && \text{(canceling }\frac{m^{\underline{k}}}{m!} = \frac{1}{(m-k)!}\text{)} \\ &= C(t) e^t. && \text{(recognizing }\sum_{r \ge 0} \frac{t^r}{r!} = e^t\text{)} \end{aligned}\]

This single identity unlocks fast \(O(d \log d)\) arithmetic on Falling-Factorial Polynomials:

  1. Point Values \(P(0 \dots d) \to\) Falling-Factorial Coefficients \(c_0 \dots d\) (ffp_from_values, 1 NTT): Compute \(C(t) = \left(\sum_{m=0}^d \frac{P(m)}{m!} t^m\right) \left(\sum_{j=0}^d \frac{(-1)^j}{j!} t^j\right) \bmod t^{d+1}\).
  2. Falling-Factorial Coefficients \(c_0 \dots d \to\) Point Values \(P(0 \dots d)\) (ffp_to_values, 1 NTT): Compute \(\widehat{P}(t) = C(t) \left(\sum_{j=0}^d \frac{1}{j!} t^j\right) \bmod t^{d+1}\) and read off \(P(m) = m! [t^m]\widehat{P}(t)\).
  3. Falling-Factorial Polynomial Multiplication (ffp_mul, 3 NTTs): Since \((P \cdot Q)(m) = P(m) Q(m)\) pointwise, convert both \(P\) and \(Q\) to point values up to degree \(d_P + d_Q\) via \(e^t\), multiply their point values \(P(m)Q(m)\) in \(O(d)\) time, and multiply by \(e^{-t}\)!
TipHand-Checkable Trace (\(d = 2\)): Recovering Falling-Factorial Coefficients via \(\widehat{P}(t) e^{-t}\)

Let \(P(x) = x^2\). In the falling-factorial basis, since \(x^{\underline{2}} = x^2 - x\) and \(x^{\underline{1}} = x\), we have \(x^2 = 0 \cdot x^{\underline{0}} + 1 \cdot x^{\underline{1}} + 1 \cdot x^{\underline{2}}\), so \((c_0, c_1, c_2) = (0, 1, 1)\). Let us verify that \(C(t) = \widehat{P}(t) e^{-t} \bmod t^3\) recovers \((0, 1, 1)\) from the point values \((P(0), P(1), P(2)) = (0, 1, 4)\):

  • \(\widehat{P}(t) \bmod t^3 = \frac{0}{0!} + \frac{1}{1!} t + \frac{4}{2!} t^2 = t + 2t^2\).
  • \(e^{-t} \bmod t^3 = 1 - t + \frac{1}{2}t^2\).
  • Multiply \((t + 2t^2)(1 - t + \frac{1}{2}t^2) \bmod t^3 = t + (2 - 1)t^2 = 0 + 1t + 1t^2 \implies (c_0, c_1, c_2) = (0, 1, 1)\)!

Eulerian Numbers \(\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle\) & Worpitzky’s Identity

Closely related to Stirling numbers are the Eulerian numbers \(\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle\) (\(0 \le k < n\), with \(\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle = 1\)), defined combinatorially as the number of permutations \(\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_n)\) of \(\{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) with exactly \(k\) ascents (indices \(1 \le i \le n - 1\) where \(\pi_i < \pi_{i+1}\)).

  • Recurrence: Inserting \(n\) into a permutation of \(\{1, \dots, n-1\}\) with \(j\) ascents either preserves the ascent count if \(n\) is placed at the very beginning or splits an existing ascent pair (\(k + 1\) slots when \(j = k\)), or creates one new ascent if placed at the end or splits a descent pair (\(n - k\) slots when \(j = k - 1\)): \[\left\langle \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right\rangle = (k + 1) \left\langle \begin{matrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{matrix} \right\rangle + (n - k) \left\langle \begin{matrix} n - 1 \\ k - 1 \end{matrix} \right\rangle.\]
TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Worpitzky’s Identity
  • Diagnostic Signal: You need to convert ordinary powers \(m^n\) into a shifted binomial basis \(\binom{m+k}{n}\) (so that summing over \(m\) becomes a clean rational series \(\frac{A_n(x)}{(1-x)^{n+1}}\)), or you need a single row \(\left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle, \dots, \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ n-1 \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle\) of Eulerian numbers in \(O(n \log n)\) time.
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Where does \(m^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle \binom{m+k}{n}\) come from combinatorially? A function \(f : \{1, \dots, n\} \to \{1, \dots, m\}\) (\(m^n\) choices) can be sorted by its values by reading its standardize-permutation \(\pi \in S_n\) (where ties are broken so \(i < j \implies \pi_i < \pi_j\)); if \(\pi\) has \(k\) ascents and \(n - 1 - k\) descents, then the sorted sequence \(1 \le f(\pi_1) \le \dots \le f(\pi_n) \le m\) must be strictly increasing at the \(n - 1 - k\) descent positions of \(\pi^{-1}\). Adding \(0, 1, \dots, n-1-k\) at the strict steps bijects the \(m^n\) functions onto pairs \((\pi, S)\) where \(\pi\) has \(k\) ascents and \(S\) is a multiset of size \(n\) chosen from \(m + k - (n-1)\)… or algebraically, multiplying by \(x^m\) and summing over \(m \ge 0\) places the Eulerian polynomial \(A_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \left\langle\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\rangle x^k\) in the numerator of \(\sum_{m \ge 0} (m+1)^n x^m = \frac{A_n(x)}{(1-x)^{n+1}}\), isolating \(A_n(x) = (1-x)^{n+1} \sum_{m \ge 0} (m+1)^n x^m \bmod x^n\) as a single 1-NTT convolution!
  • Worpitzky’s Identity (Monomials \(\leftrightarrow\) Shifted Binomial Basis): For all \(n \ge 1\) and all \(m\): \[m^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \left\langle \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right\rangle \binom{m + k}{n}.\]
  • Single-Step \(O(n \log n)\) Row Extraction Formula (K1 + Reversal Symmetry): Using the reversal symmetry \(\left\langle \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right\rangle = \left\langle \begin{matrix} n \\ n - 1 - k \end{matrix} \right\rangle\) (reversing a permutation swaps ascents and descents), set \(j = n - 1 - k\) in Worpitzky’s identity so \(\binom{m + k}{n} = \binom{m + n - 1 - j}{n}\). \(O(d)\) Consecutive-Grid Lagrange Interpolation & Faulhaber Power Sums

Finally, suppose we know that a sequence \(P(0), P(1), \dots, P(d)\) is the restriction of a degree-\(d\) polynomial \(P(x)\) to the \(d + 1\) consecutive integers \(\{0, 1, \dots, d\}\), and we want to evaluate \(P(T) \bmod p\) at a single huge integer \(T \le 10^{18}\) (assuming \(p > d\)).

Do we need \(O(d \log d)\) NTT to convert \(P(0 \dots d)\) into falling-factorial coefficients first? No! We can evaluate \(P(T)\) in \(O(d)\) linear time directly from Lagrange’s interpolation formula: \[P(T) = \sum_{i=0}^d P(i) \prod_{\substack{0 \le j \le d \\ j \neq i}} \frac{T - j}{i - j}.\]

Why does this take \(O(d)\) instead of \(O(d^2)\) on the grid \(\{0, 1, \dots, d\}\)?

  1. The Denominator Splits into Factorials: For a fixed \(i \in \{0, \dots, d\}\), the product \(\prod_{j \neq i} (i - j)\) splits into \(j < i\) and \(j > i\): \[\prod_{j=0}^{i-1} (i - j) = i(i-1)\cdots 1 = i!, \qquad \prod_{j=i+1}^d (i - j) = (-1)(-2)\cdots(-(d - i)) = (-1)^{d-i} (d - i)!.\] Thus the denominator is simply \((-1)^{d - i}\, i!\, (d - i)!\), whose inverse is \((-1)^{d-i}\, \text{ifact}[i]\, \text{ifact}[d - i]\).
  2. The Numerator Splits into Prefix and Suffix Products: For \(T \notin \{0, 1, \dots, d\}\) (if \(0 \le T \le d\), simply return \(P(T)\) in \(O(1)\)!), the product \(\prod_{j \neq i} (T - j)\) is the product of a prefix \(\text{pref}[i] = \prod_{j=0}^{i-1} (T - j)\) and a suffix \(\text{suff}[i] = \prod_{j=i+1}^d (T - j)\). Precomputing \(\text{pref}[0 \dots d]\) left-to-right and \(\text{suff}[0 \dots d]\) right-to-left takes \(2d\) multiplications and zero divisions!
NoteAlgorithm Contract: eval_consecutive_lagrange(Y, T)
  • Input: Array \(Y = [P(0), P(1), \dots, P(d)] \in K^{d+1}\) of evaluations of a degree-\(\le d\) polynomial at \(0, 1, \dots, d\); target evaluation point \(T \in K\).
  • Algebraic Precondition: \(d! \in K^\times\) (over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), requires \(p > d\)).
  • Output: \(P(T) \in K\).
  • Index & Loop Contract:
    1. If \(T \in \{0, 1, \dots, d\}\), return \(Y[T]\) directly.
    2. Precompute \(\text{ifact}[0 \dots d]\) in \(O(d)\) time.
    3. Build \(\text{pref}[0] = 1\), \(\text{pref}[i] = \text{pref}[i-1] \cdot (T - (i - 1))\) for \(1 \le i \le d\).
    4. Build \(\text{suff}[d] = 1\), \(\text{suff}[i] = \text{suff}[i+1] \cdot (T - (i + 1))\) for \(d - 1 \ge i \ge 0\).
    5. Return \(\sum_{i=0}^d (-1)^{d - i}\, Y[i] \cdot \text{pref}[i] \cdot \text{suff}[i] \cdot \text{ifact}[i] \cdot \text{ifact}[d - i]\).
  • Invariant: At index \(i\), \(\text{pref}[i] \cdot \text{suff}[i] = \prod_{0 \le j \le d, j \neq i} (T - j)\) avoids dividing by \((T - i)\) (which could be \(0 \bmod p\) if \(T \equiv i \pmod p\)!), making the evaluation safe for every \(T \in \mathbb{F}_p\).
  • Complexity: \(O(d)\) time and \(O(d)\) space.
  • Canonical Application (Faulhaber’s Power Sum): Since \(S_k(n) = \sum_{i=1}^n i^k\) satisfies \(S_k(n) - S_k(n-1) = n^k\), \(S_k(n)\) is a polynomial in \(n\) of degree \(d = k + 1\). Computing \(S_k(0), S_k(1), \dots, S_k(k+1)\) in \(O(k)\) via a linear sieve of \(i^k\) and calling eval_consecutive_lagrange(S_k, N) evaluates \(\sum_{i=1}^N i^k \bmod p\) for \(N \le 10^{18}\) in \(O(k)\) time!

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

The first sum \(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} 2^k \binom{M}{k}\) has only \(N\) terms and can be summed directly in \(O(N)\) time. Look at the double sum: does \(i\) interact with \(j\)? No! Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

1. Direct Algebraic Decoupling (\(O(N + M)\))

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

What is the ordinary generating function of \(\binom{B_i}{A_i}\) over \(B_i \ge 0\)? By Kernel K1 (or Section 6.1 Identity 3), \[\sum_{B_i = 0}^\infty \binom{B_i}{A_i} x^{B_i} = \frac{x^{A_i}}{(1 - x)^{A_i + 1}}.\] Multiply these \(N\) generating functions together to get the generating function for fixed \(\sum_{i=1}^N B_i = K\), and then multiply by \(\frac{1}{1 - x}\) to sum over all \(0 \le K \le M\)!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

1. Generating Function Derivation

Let \(S_A = \sum_{i=1}^N A_i\). By the definition of the Cauchy product of \(N\) formal power series, for a fixed sum \(K = B_1 + \dots + B_N\): \[\begin{aligned} \sum_{B_1 + \dots + B_N = K} \prod_{i=1}^N \binom{B_i}{A_i} &= [x^K] \prod_{i=1}^N \left(\sum_{B_i=0}^\infty \binom{B_i}{A_i} x^{B_i}\right) && \text{(Cauchy product of }N\text{ factors)} \\ &= [x^K] \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{x^{A_i}}{(1 - x)^{A_i + 1}} && \text{(Kernel K1: }\sum_{b \ge 0} \binom{b}{a} x^b = \frac{x^a}{(1-x)^{a+1}}\text{)} \\ &= [x^K] \frac{x^{S_A}}{(1 - x)^{S_A + N}}. && \text{(summing exponents in numerator and denominator)} \end{aligned}\] Now we must sum this coefficient over all \(0 \le K \le M\). Recall from Chapter 01 that taking the prefix sum \(\sum_{K=0}^M [x^K] F(x)\) of a formal power series \(F(x)\) is equivalent to multiplying \(F(x)\) by \(\frac{1}{1 - x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \dots\) and extracting \([x^M]\): \[\begin{aligned} S = \sum_{K=0}^M [x^K] \frac{x^{S_A}}{(1 - x)^{S_A + N}} &= [x^M] \frac{x^{S_A}}{(1 - x)^{S_A + N + 1}} && \text{(prefix-sum operator }(1-x)^{-1}\text{)} \\ &= [x^{M - S_A}] \frac{1}{(1 - x)^{S_A + N + 1}} && \text{(shift rule }[x^M] x^{S_A} G(x) = [x^{M-S_A}] G(x)\text{)} \\ &= \binom{(S_A + N + 1) + (M - S_A) - 1}{M - S_A} = \binom{M + N}{M - S_A} = \binom{M + N}{S_A + N}. \end{aligned}\]

2. Combinatorial Double-Count Verification

  • Use the \((A_1 + 1)\)-st, \((A_1 + A_2 + 2)\)-nd, \(\dots\), \((S_A + N)\)-th chosen balls as dividers that partition the remaining \((M + N) - N = M\) non-divider balls into \(N + 1\) boxes of sizes \(B_1, B_2, \dots, B_{N+1}\).
  • Then inside box \(i\) (\(1 \le i \le N\)), exactly \(A_i\) balls were chosen in \(\binom{B_i}{A_i}\) ways!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Because \(\begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} = \begin{Bmatrix} i-1 \\ j-1 \end{Bmatrix} + j \begin{Bmatrix} i-1 \\ j \end{Bmatrix}\) uses only additions and multiplications, we can precompute \(\begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} \bmod p\) for \(0 \le j \le i \le m\) in \(O(m^2)\) time!
  2. Then \(f(k) = \sum_{j=0}^m b_j k^{\underline{j}}\) where \(b_j = \sum_{i=j}^m a_i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} \bmod p\).
  3. Apply the Falling-Factorial Absorption Identity \(k^{\underline{j}} \binom{n}{k} = n^{\underline{j}} \binom{n - j}{k - j}\), which holds in \(\mathbb{Z}\) with zero divisions!
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

After absorbing \(k^{\underline{j}} \binom{n}{k} = n^{\underline{j}} \binom{n-j}{k-j}\), swap the sums over \(k\) and \(j\), and collapse the inner sum over \(k\) using \(\sum_{k=j}^n \binom{n-j}{k-j} x^k = x^j (1 + x)^{n - j}\). Notice that \(n^{\underline{j}} = n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)\) is a product of \(j\) integers and never divides by \(j!\)!

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Division-Free Basis Conversion via \(\begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix}\)

Because \(p\) is not prime, we cannot use NTT or divide by factorials. Instead, recall from Section 6.4 that for every integer \(i \ge 0\), \[k^i = \sum_{j=0}^i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} k^{\underline{j}} \in \mathbb{Z}[k].\] Substituting this into \(f(k) = \sum_{i=0}^m a_i k^i\) and changing the order of summation gives: \[f(k) = \sum_{i=0}^m a_i \sum_{j=0}^i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} k^{\underline{j}} = \sum_{j=0}^m \left(\sum_{i=j}^m a_i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix}\right) k^{\underline{j}} = \sum_{j=0}^m b_j k^{\underline{j}},\] where \(b_j = \sum_{i=j}^m a_i \begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix} \bmod p\).

2. Collapsing the Binomial Sum via Absorption

Now substitute \(f(k) = \sum_{j=0}^m b_j k^{\underline{j}}\) into the target sum \(S = \sum_{k=0}^n f(k) x^k \binom{n}{k}\): \[\begin{aligned} S &= \sum_{k=0}^n \left(\sum_{j=0}^m b_j k^{\underline{j}}\right) \binom{n}{k} x^k && \text{(expanding }f(k)\text{ in the falling-factorial basis)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} b_j \sum_{k=j}^n k^{\underline{j}} \binom{n}{k} x^k && \text{(swapping sums; }k^{\underline{j}} = 0\text{ for }k < j\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} b_j n^{\underline{j}} x^j \sum_{k=j}^n \binom{n - j}{k - j} x^{k - j} && \text{(Absorption Identity }k^{\underline{j}}\binom{n}{k} = n^{\underline{j}}\binom{n-j}{k-j}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, m)} b_j n^{\underline{j}} x^j (1 + x)^{n - j} \pmod p. && \text{(Binomial Theorem on }r = k - j \in [0, n - j]\text{)} \end{aligned}\]

3. Hand-Checkable Trace & Algorithm Contract

    • For \(j = 1\): \(b_1 \cdot 3^{\underline{1}} \cdot 2^1 \cdot (1+2)^{3-1} = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 9 = 54\).
    • For \(j = 2\): \(b_2 \cdot 3^{\underline{2}} \cdot 2^2 \cdot (1+2)^{3-2} = 1 \cdot 6 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 72\).
    • Total \(S = 54 + 72 = 126 \equiv 26 \pmod{100}\), with zero divisions!
  • Input & Preconditions: Integers \(n, x, p \in [1, 10^9]\) (\(p\) is an arbitrary positive integer, not necessarily prime) and polynomial coefficients \(a_0, \dots, a_m\) (\(m \le 10^3\)).
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(\left(\sum_{k=0}^n f(k) x^k \binom{n}{k}\right) \bmod p\) using zero divisions:
    1. Stirling Precomputation: Initialize \(S_2[0][0] = 1 \bmod p\), and for \(i = 1 \dots m\), \(j = 1 \dots i\), compute \(S_2[i][j] = (S_2[i-1][j-1] + j \cdot S_2[i-1][j]) \bmod p\).
    2. Coefficient Transformation: Compute \(b_j = \sum_{i=j}^m a_i S_2[i][j] \bmod p\) for \(0 \le j \le m\).
    3. Final Evaluation: Maintain \(\text{ff}_j = n^{\underline{j}} \bmod p\), \(x^j \bmod p\), and \((1 + x)^{n - j} \bmod p\) for \(0 \le j \le \min(n, m)\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(m^2 + \log n)\) time and \(O(m)\) space (using a 1D rolling array for \(S_2\)).

Intermediate 2 (#080) — AtCoder KUPC 2019 K: One or All

  • Source: AtCoder KUPC 2019 K — One or All
  • Prerequisite Tags: Ch 06 (§6.3 Projective Möbius / Krawtchouk Transform via Taylor Shift)
  • Target Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time, \(O(N)\) space

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Given \(N\) non-negative integers \(A_0, A_1, \dots, A_{N-1}\) (\(1 \le N \le 10^5\), \(0 \le A_i < 998244353\)), compute for all \(1 \le j \le N\) the Krawtchouk linear combinations \[S_j = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} A_i [x^i] \big((1 + x)^{N - j} (1 - x)^{j - 1}\big) \bmod 998244353.\]

  • Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\)): For \(N = 3\) (\(d = 2\)) and \(A = (1, 2, 3)\):
    • \(j = 1\) (\(k = 0\)): \((1+x)^2 = 1 + 2x + x^2 \implies S_1 = 1(1) + 2(2) + 3(1) = 8\).
    • \(j = 2\) (\(k = 1\)): \((1+x)(1-x) = 1 - x^2 \implies S_2 = 1(1) + 2(0) + 3(-1) = -2 \equiv 998244351\).
    • \(j = 3\) (\(k = 2\)): \((1-x)^2 = 1 - 2x + x^2 \implies S_3 = 1(1) + 2(-2) + 3(1) = 0\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Look at the basis polynomials \(K_{j-1}(x) = (1 + x)^{N - 1 - (j - 1)} (1 - x)^{j - 1}\) for \(k = j - 1 \in \{0, 1, \dots, N - 1\}\). We want to dualize the inner product \(S_{k+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} A_i [x^i] \big((1 + x)^{N - 1 - k} (1 - x)^k\big)\) for all \(0 \le k \le N - 1\). Notice that \((1 + x)^{N - 1 - k} (1 - x)^k = (1 + x)^{N - 1} \left(\frac{1 - x}{1 + x}\right)^k\) is the exact Projective Möbius / Krawtchouk Transform of Section 6.3!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Let \(d = N - 1\) and let \(M_{k, i} = [x^i] \big((1 + x)^{d - k} (1 - x)^k\big)\) be the Krawtchouk matrix entry. Why can’t we sum \(\sum_{k=0}^d M_{k, i} y^k\) directly into a single power? Because the Binomial Theorem requires a factor of \(\binom{d}{k}\) in front of \((1 + x)^{d - k} (1 - x)^k\)! Once we multiply \(M_{k, i}\) by \(\binom{d}{k}\), the bivariate generating function over \(x\) and \(y\) becomes completely symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\): \[\sum_{k=0}^d \binom{d}{k} M_{k, i} y^k = [x^i] \sum_{k=0}^d \binom{d}{k} (1 + x)^{d - k} \big(y(1 - x)\big)^k = [x^i] \big((1 + x) + y(1 - x)\big)^d = [x^i] \big((1 + y) + x(1 - y)\big)^d = \binom{d}{i} (1 + y)^{d - i} (1 - y)^i.\] Extracting \([y^k]\) from both sides yields the Krawtchouk Reciprocity Identity: \[\binom{d}{k} [x^i] \big((1 + x)^{d - k} (1 - x)^k\big) = \binom{d}{i} [y^k] \big((1 + y)^{d - i} (1 - y)^i\big).\]

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

By the Krawtchouk reciprocity identity \(\binom{d}{k} M_{k, i} = \binom{d}{i} M_{i, k}\), our target sum \(S_{k+1} = \sum_{i=0}^d A_i M_{k, i}\) becomes \[S_{k+1} = \frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}} \sum_{i=0}^d A_i \binom{d}{i} M_{i, k} = \frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}} [y^k] \sum_{i=0}^d \left(A_i \binom{d}{i}\right) (1 + y)^{d - i} (1 - y)^i = \frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}} [y^k] (1 + y)^d F\!\left(\frac{1 - y}{1 + y}\right),\] where \(F(z) = \sum_{i=0}^d A_i \binom{d}{i} z^i\)! And \((1 + y)^d F\!\left(\frac{1 - y}{1 + y}\right)\) is computed in \(O(N \log N)\) time using just 2 Taylor shifts via the Bruhat decomposition of Section 6.3!

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Reverse-Engineering the Krawtchouk Reciprocity Identity

Let \(d = N - 1\) and \(k = j - 1 \in \{0, 1, \dots, d\}\). We need to compute, for every \(0 \le k \le d\), \[S_{k+1} = \sum_{i=0}^d A_i [x^i] \big((1 + x)^{d - k} (1 - x)^k\big).\] In this raw form, \(i\) is the extracted exponent \([x^i]\) while \(k\) is in the power \((1 + x)^{d-k}(1 - x)^k\). To swap the roles of \(i\) and \(k\), consider the bivariate generating function \[\Phi(x, y) = \big((1 + x) + y(1 - x)\big)^d = \big((1 + y) + x(1 - y)\big)^d.\]

  • Expanding the left-hand side in powers of \(y\) via the Binomial Theorem gives: \[[x^i y^k] \Phi(x, y) = \binom{d}{k} [x^i] \big((1 + x)^{d - k} (1 - x)^k\big).\]
  • Expanding the right-hand side in powers of \(x\) via the Binomial Theorem gives: \[[x^i y^k] \Phi(x, y) = \binom{d}{i} [y^k] \big((1 + y)^{d - i} (1 - y)^i\big).\] Equating the two expressions proves Krawtchouk Reciprocity: \[[x^i] \big((1 + x)^{d - k} (1 - x)^k\big) = \frac{\binom{d}{i}}{\binom{d}{k}} [y^k] \big((1 + y)^{d - i} (1 - y)^i\big).\]

2. Reducing All \(S_{k+1}\) to a Single Projective Möbius Transform

Define the scaled polynomial \(F(z) = \sum_{i=0}^d f_i z^i\) with \(f_i = A_i \binom{d}{i} \bmod 998244353\). Substituting Krawtchouk Reciprocity into \(S_{k+1}\) pulls \([y^k]\) outside the sum over \(i\): \[\begin{aligned} S_{k+1} &= \frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}} [y^k] \sum_{i=0}^d f_i (1 + y)^{d - i} (1 - y)^i \\ &= \frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}} [y^k] \left((1 + y)^d F\!\left(\frac{1 - y}{1 + y}\right)\right). \end{aligned}\] Now apply the 4-Step Bruhat Decomposition from Section 6.3 to \(\frac{1 - y}{1 + y} = -1 + \frac{2}{1 + y}\):

  1. Taylor Shift by \(c = -1\): Compute \(F_1(u) = F(u - 1)\) using poly_taylor_shift(F, -1) in 1 NTT.
  2. Scale by \(2\) and Reverse: Form \(F_2(v) = v^d F_1(2/v) = \sum_{i=0}^d \big([u^i]F_1(u) \cdot 2^i\big) v^{d - i}\) in \(O(d)\) time.
  3. Taylor Shift by \(c = 1\): Substitute \(v = 1 + y\) by computing \(F_3(y) = F_2(y + 1)\) using poly_taylor_shift(F_2, 1) in 1 NTT.
  4. Extract and Unscale: For each \(0 \le k \le d\), \(S_{k+1} = \binom{d}{k}^{-1} [y^k] F_3(y) \bmod 998244353\).

3. Hand-Checkable Trace & Algorithm Contract

  • Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 3, d = 2, A = (1, 2, 3)\)):
    • Scale \(f_i = A_i \binom{2}{i}\): \(F(z) = 1\binom{2}{0} + 2\binom{2}{1}z + 3\binom{2}{2}z^2 = 1 + 4z + 3z^2\).
    • Step 1 (\(F_1(u) = F(u - 1)\)): \(1 + 4(u-1) + 3(u-1)^2 = 0 - 2u + 3u^2\).
    • Step 2 (\(F_2(v) = v^2 F_1(2/v)\)): \(0\cdot v^2 + (-2 \cdot 2)v + (3 \cdot 4) = 12 - 4v + 0v^2\).
    • Step 3 (\(F_3(y) = F_2(y + 1)\)): \(12 - 4(y + 1) = 8 - 4y + 0y^2\).
    • Step 4 (Divide by \(\binom{2}{k}\)): \((S_1, S_2, S_3) = (8/1, -4/2, 0/1) = (8, -2, 0)\). Exact match!
  • Input & Preconditions: Array \(A_0, \dots, A_{N-1} \in \mathbb{F}_p\) (\(N \le 10^5\), \(p = 998244353 > N\) so \(0!, \dots, (N-1)!\) are invertible).
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(S_1, \dots, S_N \bmod 998244353\), where \(S_{k+1} = \binom{d}{k}^{-1} [y^k] (1+y)^d F\!\left(\frac{1-y}{1+y}\right)\) for \(d = N - 1\) and \(0 \le k \le d\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: Computing \(F_3(y) = (1 + y)^d F\!\left(\frac{1 - y}{1 + y}\right)\) requires 2 NTTs of size \(2N\), running in \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: After the 4-step Bruhat decomposition (poly_taylor_shift(F, -1), power-of-2 scaling + coefficient reversal, and poly_taylor_shift(F_2, 1)), \([y^k] F_3(y) = \binom{d}{k} S_{k+1} \bmod p\) for every \(0 \le k \le d\).
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: When reversing \(F_1(u)\) to form \(F_2(v) = v^d F_1(2/v)\), always pad \(F_1\) to exact length \(d + 1 = N\) before reversing so trailing zero high-degree coefficients are not dropped; for \(N = 1\) (\(d = 0\)), return \(S_1 = A_0 \bmod p\) directly.

Advanced 1 (#094) — Codeforces 923E: Perpetual Subtraction

  • Source: Codeforces 923E — Perpetual Subtraction
  • Prerequisite Tags: Ch 06 (§6.3 Polynomial Taylor Shift F(x \pm 1), Eigenbasis Diagonalization of Upper-Triangular Transition Matrices)
  • Target Complexity: \(O(N \log N + N \log M)\) time, \(O(N)\) space

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given an integer \(N \le 10^5\), a step count \(M \le 10^{18}\), and an initial probability distribution \(p_0, p_1, \dots, p_N\) over \(\{0, 1, \dots, N\}\) (given modulo \(998244353\)). Initially, a random integer \(x\) is chosen with \(\Pr[x = i] = p_i\). Then the following operation is repeated \(M\) times independently:

  • Replace \(x\) with an integer chosen uniformly at random from \(\{0, 1, \dots, x\}\).

Compute the final probability distribution \(p_0^{(M)}, p_1^{(M)}, \dots, p_N^{(M)}\) modulo \(998244353\) after \(M\) steps.

  • Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\)): For \(N = 2, M = 1\), and \(P_0 = (0, 0, 1)\) (starts at \(x = 2\) with probability \(1\)): after \(M = 1\) step, \(x\) is uniform on \(\{0, 1, 2\}\), so \(P_1 = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)\). Via the eigenbasis \(y = x - 1\): \(P_0(x) = x^2 = (y + 1)^2 = 1 + 2y + y^2\), scaling by \((k+1)^{-1}\) gives \(Q_1(y) = 1 + \frac{2}{2}y + \frac{1}{3}y^2 = 1 + y + \frac{1}{3}y^2\), and shifting back \(y = x - 1\) gives \(P_1(x) = 1 + (x - 1) + \frac{1}{3}(x - 1)^2 = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3}x + \frac{1}{3}x^2\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

In a single step, if the current state is \(j \in \{0, \dots, N\}\), it transitions to each \(i \in \{0, \dots, j\}\) with probability \(\frac{1}{j + 1}\): \[p_i^{(t+1)} = \sum_{j=i}^N \frac{p_j^{(t)}}{j + 1}.\] Because \(M \le 10^{18}\) is huge, we cannot apply this transition \(M\) times directly. Instead, form the probability generating function \(P_t(x) = \sum_{i=0}^N p_i^{(t)} x^i\). How does the linear operator \(\mathcal{L}: P_t(x) \mapsto P_{t+1}(x)\) act on a polynomial \(P(x)\)?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Write out \(\mathcal{L}(x^j) = \frac{1}{j+1} \sum_{i=0}^j x^i = \frac{1}{j+1} \frac{x^{j+1} - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{1}{x - 1} \int_1^x t^j\, dt\). What is the eigenbasis of the operator \(\mathcal{L}\)? Instead of monomials \(x^j\), test the shifted monomials \((x - 1)^k\) around \(x = 1\): \[\mathcal{L}\big((x - 1)^k\big) = \frac{1}{x - 1} \int_1^x (t - 1)^k\, dt = \frac{1}{x - 1} \cdot \frac{(x - 1)^{k+1}}{k + 1} = \frac{1}{k + 1} (x - 1)^k.\] Every shifted monomial \((x - 1)^k\) is an exact eigenvector of \(\mathcal{L}\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_k = \frac{1}{k + 1}\)!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

This gives a 3-step algorithm for any \(M \le 10^{18}\):

  1. Change to Eigenbasis (\(x = y + 1\), Taylor Shift by \(+1\)): Compute \(Q_0(y) = P_0(y + 1) = \sum_{k=0}^N q_k^{(0)} y^k\) via poly_taylor_shift(P_0, +1) in 1 NTT.
  2. Apply \(M\) Steps in \(O(N \log M)\): Since \(y^k = (x - 1)^k\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\frac{1}{k + 1}\), after \(M\) steps \(q_k^{(M)} = q_k^{(0)} \cdot (k + 1)^{-M} \bmod 998244353\).
  3. Return to Monomial Basis (\(y = x - 1\), Taylor Shift by \(-1\)): Compute \(P_M(x) = Q_M(x - 1)\) via poly_taylor_shift(Q_M, -1) in 1 NTT!
NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Reverse-Engineering the Operator \(\mathcal{L}\) on \(K[x]\)

Let \(P_t(x) = \sum_{i=0}^N p_i^{(t)} x^i\). A single step of the Markov chain maps \(P_t(x)\) to: \[\begin{aligned} (\mathcal{L} P_t)(x) &= \sum_{i=0}^N x^i \sum_{j=i}^N \frac{p_j^{(t)}}{j + 1} && \text{(definition of single-step transition)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^N \frac{p_j^{(t)}}{j + 1} \sum_{i=0}^j x^i && \text{(swapping }\sum_i\text{ and }\sum_j\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^N p_j^{(t)} \frac{x^{j+1} - 1}{(j + 1)(x - 1)} && \text{(summing geometric series }\sum_{i=0}^j x^i\text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{x - 1} \int_1^x P_t(u)\, du. && \text{(recognizing }\int_1^x u^j du = \frac{x^{j+1}-1}{j+1}\text{)} \end{aligned}\] Why does \(\int_1^x\) have lower limit \(1\)? Because at \(x = 1\), total probability is conserved. This lower limit \(u = 1\) is our Diagnostic Signal to expand \(P_t(u)\) around \(u = 1\) in powers of \(y = u - 1\)!

2. Diagonalizing \(\mathcal{L}\) via Taylor Shift

Substitute \(u = 1 + y\) (so \(x = 1 + y\) and \(P_t(x) = P_t(1 + y) =: Q_t(y) = \sum_{k=0}^N q_k^{(t)} y^k\)). Then: \[(\mathcal{L} P_t)(1 + y) = \frac{1}{y} \int_0^y Q_t(v)\, dv = \frac{1}{y} \sum_{k=0}^N q_k^{(t)} \frac{y^{k+1}}{k + 1} = \sum_{k=0}^N \left(\frac{q_k^{(t)}}{k + 1}\right) y^k.\] In the \(y\)-basis (\(y = x - 1\)), the entire upper-triangular transition matrix becomes diagonal: \[q_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{q_k^{(t)}}{k + 1} \implies q_k^{(M)} = \frac{q_k^{(0)}}{(k + 1)^M} \qquad (0 \le k \le N).\] Equivalently, in matrix language, let \(B_{i, r} = \binom{r}{i}\) be the Pascal matrix (Taylor shift by \(+1\), mapping \(p \mapsto q = B p\)) and \(B^{-1}_{r, j} = (-1)^{r-j}\binom{r}{j}\) be its inverse (Taylor shift by \(-1\)). For the single-step transition matrix \(M_{r, j} = \frac{1}{j + 1}\) (\(0 \le r \le j\)), the Hockey-Stick identity \(\sum_{r=i}^j \binom{r}{i} = \binom{j+1}{i+1}\) and Absorption identity \(\frac{1}{j+1}\binom{j+1}{i+1} = \frac{1}{i+1}\binom{j}{i}\) immediately verify that every entry \((B M)_{i, j}\) for \(0 \le i \le j\) satisfies: \[(B M)_{i, j} = \sum_{r=i}^j \binom{r}{i} \frac{1}{j + 1} = \frac{1}{j + 1} \binom{j + 1}{i + 1} = \frac{1}{i + 1} \binom{j}{i} = (\Lambda B)_{i, j},\] where \(\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(1, \frac{1}{2}, \dots, \frac{1}{N+1}\right)\), confirming \(B M = \Lambda B \iff M = B^{-1} \Lambda B\)!

3. Hand-Checkable Trace & Algorithm Contract

  • Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 2, M = 2, P_0(x) = x^2\)):
    • \(Q_0(y) = P_0(y + 1) = 1 + 2y + y^2\).
    • After \(M = 2\) steps, divide \([y^k]\) by \((k+1)^2\): \(Q_2(y) = 1 + \frac{2}{4}y + \frac{1}{9}y^2 = 1 + \frac{1}{2}y + \frac{1}{9}y^2\).
    • Shift back \(P_2(x) = Q_2(x - 1) = 1 + \frac{1}{2}(x - 1) + \frac{1}{9}(x^2 - 2x + 1) = \frac{11}{18} + \frac{5}{18}x + \frac{2}{18}x^2\).
  • Input & Preconditions: Initial distribution array \(P_0 = [p_0, \dots, p_N] \in \mathbb{F}_p^{N+1}\) (\(N \le 10^5\)), step count \(M \le 10^{18}\), and prime \(p = 998244353 > N + 1\) so \(1, \dots, N + 1\) are invertible.
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(P_M = [p_0^{(M)}, \dots, p_N^{(M)}] \bmod 998244353\) via 3 steps:
    1. Forward Taylor Shift (\(c = +1\)): Compute \(Q_0 = \text{poly\_taylor\_shift}(P_0, 1)\) in 1 NTT: \[q_k^{(0)} = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{j=k}^N (j!\, p_j) \frac{1^{j-k}}{(j - k)!}.\]
    2. Diagonal Eigenvalue Scaling (\(M\) steps): For each \(0 \le k \le N\), set \(q_k^{(M)} = q_k^{(0)} \cdot ((k + 1)^{-1})^{M \bmod (p - 1)} \bmod 998244353\).

1. Decomposition Around the Global Maximum \(n\)

First handle boundary cases: if \(A = 0\) or \(B = 0\) or \(A + B - 2 > n - 1\) or \(A + B - 2 < 0\), the answer is \(0\) (since \(n\) itself contributes \(1\) to both \(A\) and \(B\), and each of the remaining \(n - 1\) elements can contribute to at most one of \(A\) or \(B\)). If \(n = 1\), the answer is \(1\) if \(A = 1, B = 1\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Now assume \(n \ge 2\) and \(A, B \ge 1\). Suppose the global maximum \(n\) stands at position \(i + 1\) (\(0 \le i \le n - 1\)).

  • Any prefix maximum after position \(i + 1\) would have to exceed \(n\), which is impossible. Thus the \(A\) prefix maxima consist of \(A - 1\) prefix maxima among the \(i\) elements to the left of \(n\), plus \(n\) itself.
  • Symmetrically, the \(B\) suffix maxima consist of \(n\) itself, plus \(B - 1\) suffix maxima among the \(n - 1 - i\) elements to the right of \(n\).
  • Choosing which \(i\) elements of \(\{1, \dots, n - 1\}\) go to the left of \(n\) takes \(\binom{n - 1}{i}\) ways. 2. Collapsing the Binomial Convolution of Stirling Columns

We can prove the identity \[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \binom{n - 1}{i} \begin{bmatrix} i \\ A - 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} n - 1 - i \\ B - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \binom{A + B - 2}{A - 1} \begin{bmatrix} n - 1 \\ A + B - 2 \end{bmatrix}\] in two complementary ways:

  • Combinatorial Proof: By the Rényi–Foata correspondence, each record-block between consecutive prefix maxima (and suffix maxima) is a directed cycle rooted at its maximum element. 1. Polynomial Degree Proof via Finite Calculus & Falling Factorials

We give two complementary proofs that \(P_u(x) := \text{dp}[u][x]\) is a polynomial in \(x\) of degree \(\text{sz}(u)\):

    1. For each child \(v\) of \(u\), \(P_v(y)\) is a polynomial of degree \(\text{sz}(v)\).

Let \(p = m^{-1} \bmod 998244353\) (well-defined since \(1 \le m < 998244353\)) and \(q = 1 - p\). The random variable \(X\) takes value \(i \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}\) with probability \(\binom{n}{i} p^i q^{n - i}\). Expanding \(i^k\) in the falling-factorial basis via Section 6.4: \[i^k = \sum_{j=0}^k \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} i^{\underline{j}}.\] Substituting this into \(\mathbb{E}[X^k]\) and applying the Falling-Factorial Absorption Identity \(i^{\underline{j}}\binom{n}{i} = n^{\underline{j}}\binom{n - j}{i - j}\): \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X^k] &= \sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i} p^i q^{n - i} \sum_{j=0}^k \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} i^{\underline{j}} && \text{(basis conversion }i^k = \sum_{j=0}^k \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} i^{\underline{j}}\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} \sum_{i=j}^n i^{\underline{j}} \binom{n}{i} p^i q^{n - i} && \text{(swapping }\sum_i, \sum_j\text{; }i^{\underline{j}} = 0\text{ for }i < j\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} p^j \sum_{i=j}^n \binom{n - j}{i - j} p^{i - j} q^{(n - j) - (i - j)} && \text{(Absorption Identity)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} p^j (p + q)^{n - j} && \text{(Binomial Theorem on }r = i - j\text{)} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} \left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^j. && \text{(since }p + q = 1\text{ and }1^{n-j} = 1\text{)} \end{aligned}\]

2. Indicator-Variable Combinatorial Double-Count (Bonus Verification)

Why does \(\mathbb{E}[X^k] = \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} m^{-j}\) hold combinatorially? Write \(X = I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n\), where \(I_r \in \{0, 1\}\) is the indicator that round \(r\) draws the Joker (\(\mathbb{E}[I_r] = 1/m\)). Expanding \(X^k = (I_1 + \dots + I_n)^k\) gives \(n^k\) terms \(I_{r_1} I_{r_2} \cdots I_{r_k}\) corresponding to functions \(f: \{1, \dots, k\} \to \{1, \dots, n\}\).

  • If the image of \(f\) has size \(|\operatorname{Im}(f)| = j\), then since \(I_r^c = I_r\) for any \(c \ge 1\) and different rounds are independent, \(\mathbb{E}[I_{r_1}\cdots I_{r_k}] = (1/m)^j\)!
  • How many functions \(f: \{1, \dots, k\} \to \{1, \dots, n\}\) have image size \(j\)? There are \(\begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix}\) ways to partition the domain \(\{1, \dots, k\}\) into \(j\) non-empty blocks, and \(n^{\underline{j}}\) ways to assign distinct rounds in \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) to those \(j\) blocks! Summing over \(j\) gives \(\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} m^{-j}\) immediately!

3. Hand-Checkable Trace & Algorithm Contract

  • Hand-Checkable Trace (\(n = 3, m = 2, k = 3\)):
    • Using \(\begin{Bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{Bmatrix} = 1, \begin{Bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{Bmatrix} = 3, \begin{Bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{Bmatrix} = 1\): \[\mathbb{E}[X^3] = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^1 + 3 \cdot 6 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 + 1 \cdot 6 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{3}{2} + \frac{9}{2} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{27}{4},\] matching \(\frac{1}{8}(0^3 \binom{3}{0} + 1^3 \binom{3}{1} + 2^3 \binom{3}{2} + 3^3 \binom{3}{3}) = \frac{3 + 24 + 27}{8} = \frac{54}{8} = \frac{27}{4}\).
  • Input & Preconditions: Integers \(1 \le n, m < 998244353\), moment exponent \(k \ge 1\) (\(k \le 5000\) in standard version, \(k \le 2 \times 10^5\) in NTT version), and prime modulus \(p = 998244353 > k\) (with \(m \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\)).
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(\mathbb{E}[X^k] = \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} (m^{-1})^j \bmod 998244353\):
    1. Compute row \(k\) of the Stirling numbers of the second kind via 1 NTT (stirling_2nd_row, Section 6.4): \[\begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} = [x^j] \left(\sum_{r=0}^k \frac{r^k}{r!} x^r\right) \left(\sum_{s=0}^k \frac{(-1)^s}{s!} x^s\right) \qquad (0 \le j \le k).\]
    2. Accumulate \(\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} \begin{Bmatrix} k \\ j \end{Bmatrix} n^{\underline{j}} (m^{-1})^j \bmod 998244353\) in \(O(k)\) time.
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(k \log k)\) time and \(O(k)\) space via 1 NTT (or \(O(k^2)\) DP when \(k \le 5000\)).
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: At index \(j \in [0, \min(n, k)]\), the running term maintains \(\mathbb{E}[X^{\underline{j}}] = n^{\underline{j}} m^{-j} \bmod p\) via \(\text{term}_0 = 1\) and \(\text{term}_{j+1} = \text{term}_j \cdot (n - j) \cdot m^{-1} \bmod p\).
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: Cap the summation at \(\min(n, k)\) (though \(\text{term}_j = 0\) automatically for \(j > n\) since \(n^{\underline{j}}\) hits the factor \(n - n = 0\)); do not precompute factorials up to \(n \le 998244352\)—compute \(n^{\underline{j}}\) incrementally in \(O(k)\) multiplications.

Fast Diagnostic Routing Checklist for Chapter 06

When faced with a binomial sum, polynomial shift, or power-weighted combinatorial expression, run through this 6-question diagnostic checklist in order:

  1. Can the sum be aligned into a 1-NTT Cauchy or Cross-Correlation convolution?
    • If the summand factors into \(\alpha(n)\beta(k)\gamma(n-k)\), use Cauchy convolution (mul).
    • If the summand factors into \(\alpha(n)\beta(i)\gamma(i+n)\) (\(j - i = n\)), reverse \(\beta\) into \(\widetilde{B}[r] = \beta(N - r)\) and read \([x^{N+n}](\widetilde{B} \cdot G)\) (§6.1).
  2. Is it a pure binomial product with a standard Vandermonde structure?
    • Sum of lower indices fixed (\(\binom{R}{k}\binom{S}{n-k}\)) \(\to\) Chu–Vandermonde \(\binom{R+S}{n}\) (§6.1).
    • Both lower indices carry \(+k\) (\(\binom{R}{a+k}\binom{S}{b+k}\) with \(R \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\)) \(\to\) flip \(\binom{R}{a+k} = \binom{R}{R-a-k}\) (§6.1).
    • Sum of upper indices fixed (\(\binom{k}{a}\binom{n-k}{b}\)) \(\to\) Parallel summation \(\binom{n+1}{a+b+1}\) via \((1-x)^{-(a+1)}(1-x)^{-(b+1)}\) (§6.1).
  3. Does the free external parameter \(n\) appear in only ONE binomial factor?
    • Apply Engine 1 (Wilf’s Snake Oil): form \(\sum_{n \ge 0} S_n x^n\), swap \(\sum_n\) inside \(\sum_k\), collapse the \(n\)-sum via Master Kernels K1–K7, sum the \(k\)-series, and extract \([x^n]\) (§6.2).
  4. Do multiple binomial factors depend on both \(n\) and \(k\)?
    • Apply Engine 2 (Egorychev Extraction): keep one \(\binom{n}{k}\) intact, replace the other binomials via \(\binom{A}{B} = [u^B](1+u)^A\) (Rule E1) or \([u^{A-B}](1-u)^{-B-1}\) (Rule E2), shift \(k\) out of \([u^{\dots}]\) as \(u^{ck}\), and collapse \(\sum_k \binom{n}{k} W(u)^k = (1 + W(u))^n\) (§6.2).
  5. Do you need \(F(x + c)\), \((cx + e)^d F\!\left(\frac{ax+b}{cx+e}\right)\), or \((1 - cx)^{-\alpha} F\!\left(\frac{ax}{1-cx}\right) \bmod x^N\)?
    • Use poly_taylor_shift in 1 NTT (\(O(d \log d)\) when \(p > d\); Lucas/SOS digit DP when \(p \le d\)) and the 4-step Bruhat decomposition for Möbius transforms (§6.3).
    • Watch Failure Mode #14: When \(\alpha = -d \in \{-(N-2), \dots, 0\}\) in the \(\alpha\)-Euler Transform, split \(F(y) = F_{\le d}(y) + y^{d+1}F_{>d}(y)\) to shift the tail to \(\alpha' = 1 > 0\) and avoid dividing by \((-d)^{\overline{d+1}} = 0\) (§6.3).
  6. Is a polynomial \(P(k)\) (or monomial \(k^m\)) weighting a huge-\(n\) binomial sum \(\sum_{k=0}^n P(k)\binom{n}{k} a^k b^{n-k}\), or is a DP polynomial in \(x \le 10^9\)?
    • Convert \(P(k) = \sum_{j=0}^m c_j k^{\underline{j}}\) into the falling-factorial basis (via \(\begin{Bmatrix} i \\ j \end{Bmatrix}\) when given monomials, or via \(\widehat{P}(t)e^{-t}\) in 1 NTT when given point values \(P(0 \dots m)\)) and apply the division-free Absorption Identity \(k^{\underline{j}}\binom{n}{k} = n^{\underline{j}}\binom{n-j}{k-j}\) (§6.4).
    • If a degree-\(d\) polynomial \(P(x)\) is known at consecutive points \(0, 1, \dots, d\), evaluate \(P(T)\) at \(T \le 10^{18}\) in \(O(d)\) time via eval_consecutive_lagrange (§6.4).
