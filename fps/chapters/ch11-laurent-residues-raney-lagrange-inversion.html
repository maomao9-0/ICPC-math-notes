Chapter 11: Formal Laurent Series, Residue Calculus, Raney’s Lemma, and Lagrange–Bürmann Inversion

From the Field of Formal Laurent Series \(K((x))\) and Jacobi’s Residue Formula to the Six Canonical Forms of Lagrange Inversion, Coupled Binomial Linearization, and Characteristic-\(p\) Frobenius Decimation

In Chapters 3 through 10, our standard workflow for an implicit combinatorial equation—such as the binary tree equation \(F(x) = x(1 + F(x)^2)\) or the rooted labeled tree equation \(T(x) = x e^{T(x)}\)—was to treat the equation as a root-finding problem \(P(F(x)) = 0\) in the truncated polynomial ring \(K[x]/(x^N)\) and run Newton’s method in \(O(N \log N)\) time. Newton’s method computes the entire prefix \(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{N-1}\), which is ideal when we need all coefficients up to \(N\).

However, three fundamental obstacles frequently arise in advanced competitive programming that Newton’s method alone cannot resolve:

  1. Direct Closed-Form & Single-Coefficient Extraction: Many problems do not ask for a black-box numerical array; they ask us to simplify a nested combinatorial sum or extract a single coefficient \([x^N] H(F(x))\) where \(F(x) = x \phi(F(x))\) is defined implicitly, often with \(H(u)\) depending on parameters or with \(N\) coupled inside the exponent of another series—such as a coupled binomial sum \(\sum_{k=0}^n f_k \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k}\).
  2. Simultaneous Extraction Across a Family of Powers: Suppose we must evaluate \([x^N] F(x)^k\) for a fixed \(N\) across all \(k = 1, \dots, N\), or evaluate prefix sums \(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1} [x^k] F(x)^n\) across all \(n = 0, \dots, N\). Computing \(F(x)\) and powering it \(N\) separate times would take \(O(N^2 \log N)\) time. We need a duality principle that swaps the role of the coefficient index \(n\) and the exponent \(k\), turning a family of extractions on \(F(x)\) into a single polynomial power of its defining kernel \(\phi(u)\).
  3. Small Prime Characteristic (\(p \le N\), e.g., \(N \le 10^{18} \bmod p\)): In Chapter 3, we saw that polynomial \(\ln\), \(\exp\), and differential solvers break down completely in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\) as soon as the degree reaches \(p\), because \(p \equiv 0 \pmod p\) cannot be inverted. Even classical Lagrange Inversion \([x^n] F(x) = \frac{1}{n}[u^{n-1}]\phi(u)^n\) divides by \(n\), which is undefined whenever \(p \mid n\)!

To conquer all three obstacles simultaneously, we must step outside the ring of formal power series \(K[[x]]\) into its fraction field: the field of formal Laurent series \(K((x))\), equipped with the formal residue operator \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0}\). Remarkably, the formal residue operator requires zero limits, zero complex contour integrals, and zero divisions by integers. Its fundamental change-of-variables theorem holds over any commutative ring \(K\)—including finite fields \(\mathbb{F}_p\) of arbitrary characteristic—and yields the 6 Canonical Forms of Lagrange–Bürmann Inversion, Elegia’s Coupled Binomial Sum Linearization, Raney’s Cycle Lemma, and Base-\(p\) Frobenius Decimation for \(N \le 10^{18}\).

11.1 The Field of Formal Laurent Series \(K((x))\) & The Formal Residue Operator \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0}\)

11.1.1 Concrete Motivation: Why Do We Need Negative Powers of \(x\)?

Suppose \(F(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty f_n x^n \in K[[x]]\) is a formal power series, and we want to isolate a single coefficient \(f_n = [x^n] F(x)\). In the ring \(K[[x]]\), the extraction operator \([x^n]\) depends on the index \(n\). If we perform a substitution \(x = G(u)\) where \(G(u) = g_1 u + g_2 u^2 + \dots\), there is no obvious formula for \([x^n] F(x)\) in terms of \([u^n] F(G(u))\) because every power \(G(u)^k\) spreads across many powers of \(u\).

However, imagine that we are allowed to multiply \(F(x)\) by \(x^{-n-1}\), shifting every exponent down by \(n+1\): \[ x^{-n-1} F(x) = f_0 x^{-n-1} + f_1 x^{-n} + \dots + f_{n-1} x^{-2} + \mathbf{f_n x^{-1}} + f_{n+1} x^0 + f_{n+2} x^1 + \dots \] Regardless of what \(n\) we started with, the target coefficient \(f_n\) has now been moved to a single universal position: the coefficient of \(x^{-1}\)! Even better, look at what happens when we differentiate any monomial \(x^k\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)): \[ D_x(x^k) = k x^{k-1}. \] Can \(D_x(x^k)\) ever produce an \(x^{-1}\) term? To get \(k - 1 = -1\), we would need \(k = 0\). Better still, when \(k = 0\), the derivative is \(D_x(x^0) = 0 \cdot x^{-1} = 0\)! That is:

The \(x^{-1}\) position is the unique exponent in \(\mathbb{Z}\) that can never be reached by differentiating any integer power of \(x\)—and this vanishing \(0 \cdot x^{-1} = 0\) requires zero division and holds in every commutative ring \(K\), even in characteristic \(p\)!

To turn this observation into a rigorous algebraic engine, we must first define the ring of series with negative powers carefully so that multiplication never triggers an infinite sum.

11.1.2 Why Doubly-Infinite Series Fail to Form a Ring, and How \(K((x))\) Fixes It

A natural first temptation is to allow arbitrary negative and positive powers of \(x\): doubly-infinite formal series \(A(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty} a_n x^n\). Let us see why this immediately collapses when we try to multiply two such series.

Suppose we take \(A(x) = B(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty} x^n\) (where \(a_n = b_n = 1\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\)) and try to compute the coefficient of \(x^0\) in their Cauchy product \(A(x) B(x)\): \[ [x^0]\bigl(A(x) B(x)\bigr) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty} a_k b_{-k} = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty} 1 \cdot 1 = 1 + 1 + 1 + \dots \] This sum has infinitely many nonzero terms. Because a formal ring has no topology or analytic convergence on \(K\), an infinite sum of \(1\)s in \(K\) is completely undefined! Thus, the set of doubly-infinite formal series \(K[[x, x^{-1}]]\) is only a \(K\)-module (and a \(K[x, x^{-1}]\)-module), not a ring.

How do we guarantee that the Cauchy product \(\sum_{j+k=n} a_j b_k\) is always a finite sum for every \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\)? In \(K[[x]]\), finiteness holds because both series are bounded below in degree (\(j \ge 0\) and \(k \ge 0\), so \(j + k = n\) has only \(n+1\) pairs). If we allow negative exponents, the sum \(\sum_{j+k=n} a_j b_k\) remains finite as long as each series has only finitely many negative powers!

NoteDefinition 11.1: The Ring (and Field) of Formal Laurent Series \(K((x))\)

Let \(K\) be a commutative ring with identity \(1 \neq 0\). A formal Laurent series over \(K\) is a formal expression \[ A(x) = \sum_{n = n_0}^{\infty} a_n x^n = a_{n_0} x^{n_0} + a_{n_0+1} x^{n_0+1} + \dots \quad (n_0 \in \mathbb{Z}, \; a_n \in K), \] where at most finitely many coefficients with negative indices \(n < 0\) are nonzero. The set of all formal Laurent series over \(K\) is denoted \(K((x)) = K[[x]][x^{-1}]\).

  1. Discrete Valuation (\(\operatorname{val}\)): For any nonzero \(A(x) \in K((x))\), its valuation (or order) \(\operatorname{val}(A) \in \mathbb{Z}\) is the smallest integer \(n_0 \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \([x^{n_0}] A(x) \neq 0\). By convention, \(\operatorname{val}(0) = +\infty\). Every nonzero \(A(x) \in K((x))\) factors uniquely as \[ A(x) = x^{n_0} \tilde{A}(x), \quad \text{where } n_0 = \operatorname{val}(A) \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ and } \tilde{A}(x) \in K[[x]] \text{ with } [x^0]\tilde{A}(x) = a_{n_0} \neq 0. \]
  2. Addition and Cauchy Multiplication: If \(\operatorname{val}(A) = n_0\) and \(\operatorname{val}(B) = m_0\), then for every \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\), the Cauchy coefficient \[ [x^n]\bigl(A(x) B(x)\bigr) := \sum_{\substack{j + k = n \\ j \ge n_0, \; k \ge m_0}} a_j b_k = \sum_{j = n_0}^{n - m_0} a_j b_{n - j} \] has at most \((n - n_0 - m_0 + 1)\) terms (and is \(0\) for all \(n < n_0 + m_0\)). This makes \(K((x))\) a commutative ring with \(\operatorname{val}(AB) \ge \operatorname{val}(A) + \operatorname{val}(B)\) (with equality whenever \(K\) is an integral domain).
  3. Units and Field Structure: A formal Laurent series \(A(x) = x^{n_0} \tilde{A}(x) \in K((x))\) has a multiplicative inverse \(A(x)^{-1} \in K((x))\) if and only if \(\tilde{A}(x)\) is a unit in \(K[[x]]\)—that is, if and only if its leading nonzero coefficient \(a_{n_0} = [x^0]\tilde{A}(x)\) belongs to the unit group \(K^\times\): \[ A(x) \in K((x))^\times \iff \exists\, n_0 \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } A(x) = x^{n_0}\tilde{A}(x) \text{ with } [x^0]\tilde{A}(x) \in K^\times, \] in which case \(A(x)^{-1} = x^{-n_0} \tilde{A}(x)^{-1}\) has \(\operatorname{val}(A^{-1}) = -\operatorname{val}(A)\). In particular, when \(K\) is a field, every nonzero element \(a_{n_0} \in K \setminus \{0\}\) is a unit, so every nonzero element of \(K((x))\) is invertible and \(K((x))\) is a field—the fraction field \(\operatorname{Frac}(K[[x]])\).

11.1.3 Formal Differentiation, Formal Differential Forms, and \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0}\)

We extend the formal derivative \(D_x : K[[x]] \to K[[x]]\) from Chapter 1 to all of \(K((x))\) by linearity on every integer power \(x^n\) (\(n \in \mathbb{Z}\)): \[ D_x A(x) = A'(x) := \sum_{n = n_0}^\infty n a_n x^{n-1}. \] Just as in \(K[[x]]\), the product rule \((AB)' = A'B + AB'\), the quotient rule \((A/B)' = (A'B - AB')/B^2\) (for \(B \in K((x))^\times\)), and the chain rule hold purely as algebraic identities on coefficients.

To keep track of changes of variables cleanly, we write a formal Laurent differential \(1\)-form as \(\omega = A(x)\,dx\), where \(dx\) is a formal symbol accompanying the variable \(x\). When we substitute \(x = G(u)\), the differential \(dx\) transforms formally into \(G'(u)\,du\).

NoteDefinition 11.2: The Formal Residue Operator \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0}\)

Let \(K\) be any commutative ring and \(A(x) = \sum_{n=n_0}^\infty a_n x^n \in K((x))\). The formal residue of \(A(x)\) (or of the formal \(1\)-form \(A(x)\,dx\)) at \(x = 0\) is the \(K\)-linear functional \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0} : K((x)) \to K\) that extracts the coefficient of \(x^{-1}\): \[ \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x)\,dx = \operatorname{Res}_x A(x) := [x^{-1}] A(x) = a_{-1}. \] Consequently, for any integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) and any \(F(x) \in K((x))\), extracting \([x^n] F(x)\) is equivalent to taking a formal residue: \[ [x^n] F(x) = [x^{-1}]\bigl(x^{-n-1} F(x)\bigr) = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} \frac{F(x)}{x^{n+1}}\,dx. \]

Why is writing \([x^n] F(x)\) as \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0} F(x) x^{-n-1}\,dx\) anything more than a change of notation? Because the \(-1\)st coefficient possesses three extraordinary algebraic invariance properties that no other coefficient \([x^n]\) (\(n \neq -1\)) enjoys.

ImportantProposition 11.1: Fundamental Algebraic Properties of the Formal Residue

Let \(K\) be any commutative ring with \(1\) (of arbitrary characteristic, including \(\mathbb{F}_p\) and \(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\)), and let \(A(x), B(x) \in K((x))\).

  1. Holomorphic Vanishing: If \(A(x) \in K[[x]]\) (i.e., \(\operatorname{val}(A) \ge 0\), so \(A\) has no negative powers of \(x\)), then \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x)\,dx = 0\).
  2. Total Derivative Vanishing (In All Characteristics!): For every formal Laurent series \(F(x) \in K((x))\), the formal residue of its derivative is identically zero: \[ \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} F'(x)\,dx = [x^{-1}] F'(x) = 0. \] Proof: Write \(F(x) = \sum_{n=n_0}^\infty f_n x^n\). Then \(F'(x) = \sum_{n=n_0}^\infty n f_n x^{n-1}\). The term with exponent \(n - 1 = -1\) comes exclusively from \(n = 0\), giving \([x^{-1}] F'(x) = 0 \cdot f_0 = 0\) in \(K\). \(\square\)
  3. Formal Integration by Parts: For any two formal Laurent series \(A(x), B(x) \in K((x))\), \[ \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A'(x) B(x)\,dx = -\operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x) B'(x)\,dx. \] Proof: Apply Property 2 to the product \(F(x) = A(x) B(x)\): by the formal Leibniz product rule, \((AB)' = A'B + AB'\), so \(0 = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} (AB)'\,dx = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A'(x) B(x)\,dx + \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x) B'(x)\,dx\). \(\square\)
  4. Logarithmic Residue of a Shifted Power Series: Suppose \(G(u) = u^{m} \tilde{G}(u) \in K((u))\) with \(m \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(\tilde{G}(u) \in K[[u]]\) satisfying \([u^0]\tilde{G}(u) \in K^\times\). Then \[ \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{G'(u)}{G(u)}\,du = m \cdot 1_K. \] Proof: By the product rule, \(G'(u) = m u^{m-1} \tilde{G}(u) + u^m \tilde{G}'(u)\). Dividing by \(G(u) = u^m \tilde{G}(u)\) (which is valid because \(\tilde{G}(0) \in K^\times\) makes \(\tilde{G}(u)\) invertible in \(K[[u]]\)) yields \[ \frac{G'(u)}{G(u)} = m u^{-1} + \frac{\tilde{G}'(u)}{\tilde{G}(u)}. \] Since both \(\tilde{G}'(u)\) and \(\tilde{G}(u)^{-1}\) belong to \(K[[u]]\) (they have no negative powers of \(u\)), their product \(\tilde{G}'(u)/\tilde{G}(u)\) lies in \(K[[u]]\) and has \([u^{-1}]\bigl(\tilde{G}'(u)/\tilde{G}(u)\bigr) = 0\). Thus \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G'(u)/G(u)\bigr) = m\). Notice again that no division by any integer occurred! \(\square\)

11.1.4 The Compositional Group \(\mathcal{G}_1(K)\) and Jacobi’s Formal Change-of-Variables Theorem

Now we ask: when can we substitute \(x = G(u)\) into an arbitrary formal Laurent series \(A(x) = \sum_{k=n_0}^\infty a_k x^k \in K((x))\)?

  1. First, so that the infinite positive tail \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k G(u)^k\) is coefficient-wise finite, we need \(\operatorname{val}(G) \ge 1\) (i.e., \(G(0) = 0\)), which ensures \(\operatorname{val}(G(u)^k) \ge k \to \infty\) as \(k \to \infty\).
  2. Second, so that the negative powers \(G(u)^{-1}, \dots, G(u)^{n_0}\) exist in \(K((u))\) and preserve valuation one-to-one, we need \(G(u) = u \tilde{G}(u)\) with \(\tilde{G}(0) = [u^1]G(u) \in K^\times\).
NoteDefinition 11.3: The Compositional Group \(\mathcal{G}_1(K)\)

For a commutative ring \(K\), the compositional group \(\mathcal{G}_1(K)\) is the set of formal power series of exact valuation \(1\) whose linear coefficient is a unit in \(K\): \[ \mathcal{G}_1(K) := \bigl\{ G(u) = g_1 u + g_2 u^2 + g_3 u^3 + \dots \in u K[[u]] \;\big|\; g_1 = [u^1]G(u) \in K^\times \bigr\}. \] Under formal composition \((F \circ G)(u) = F(G(u))\), \(\mathcal{G}_1(K)\) is a group with identity \(I(u) = u\). Every \(G(u) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) has a unique two-sided compositional inverse \(F(x) = G^{\langle -1 \rangle}(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) satisfying \[ F(G(u)) = u \quad \text{and} \quad G(F(x)) = x. \] Furthermore, because \(G(u) = u \tilde{G}(u)\) with \(\tilde{G}(0) = g_1 \in K^\times\), every integer power \(G(u)^k = u^k \tilde{G}(u)^k\) (\(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)) is a well-defined Laurent series in \(K((u))\) of exact valuation \(\operatorname{val}(G(u)^k) = k\). Therefore, for any \(A(x) = \sum_{k=n_0}^\infty a_k x^k \in K((x))\), the composition \[ A(G(u)) := \sum_{k=n_0}^\infty a_k G(u)^k \in K((u)) \] is a well-defined formal Laurent series (each coefficient \([u^m]A(G(u))\) receives nonzero contributions only from \(n_0 \le k \le m\), a finite sum).

We now state and prove the cornerstone theorem of the entire chapter. Notice why the factor \(G'(u)\) is indispensable: if we take \(A(x) = x^{-1}\) and \(G(u) = u + u^2\), then \(A(G(u)) = \frac{1}{u(1+u)} = u^{-1} - 1 + u - u^2 + \dots\) happens to have \([u^{-1}]A(G(u)) = 1\). Key Question: Does the residue \(c_{-1} = [x^{-1}]A(x)\) remain invariant for general Laurent powers such as \(A(x) = x^{-2}\)? For \(A(x) = x^{-2}\), we have \([x^{-1}]x^{-2} = 0\), whereas \(A(G(u)) = \frac{1}{u^2(1+u)^2} = u^{-2}(1 - 2u + 3u^2 - \dots) = u^{-2} - 2u^{-1} + 3 - \dots\) has \([u^{-1}]A(G(u)) = -2 \neq 0\)! Without the Jacobian factor \(G'(u) = 1 + 2u\), changing variables destroys the \(-1\)st coefficient. Once we multiply by \(G'(u)\,du\), the unwanted \(-2u^{-1}\) term cancels: \[ A(G(u)) G'(u) = \bigl(u^{-2} - 2u^{-1} + 3 - \dots\bigr)(1 + 2u) = u^{-2} + (-2 + 2)u^{-1} + (3 - 4) + \dots = u^{-2} + \mathbf{0 \cdot u^{-1}} - 1 + \dots \]

ImportantTheorem 11.1: Formal Change-of-Variables Formula (Jacobi’s Residue Theorem)

Let \(K\) be any commutative ring with \(1\) (of arbitrary characteristic), let \(A(x) \in K((x))\) be any formal Laurent series, and let \(G(u) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) be any compositional substitution (\([u^0]G = 0\) and \([u^1]G \in K^\times\)). Then: \[ \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x)\,dx = \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} A(G(u)) G'(u)\,du, \] or, written in coefficient extraction notation, \[ [x^{-1}] A(x) = [u^{-1}] \bigl(A(G(u)) G'(u)\bigr). \]

Complete Step-by-Step Algebraic Proof (Valid in All Characteristics)

By \(K\)-linearity of the formal residue and the fact that only terms \(a_k x^k\) with \(n_0 \le k \le -1\) can ever contribute to \([x^{-1}]A(x)\) or \([u^{-1}]\bigl(A(G(u))G'(u)\bigr)\), it suffices to prove the identity on each individual monomial \(A(x) = x^k\) for every integer \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\): \[ [u^{-1}]\bigl(G(u)^k G'(u)\bigr) = [x^{-1}] x^k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k = -1, \\ 0 & \text{if } k \neq -1. \end{cases} \] We verify the three regimes of \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\) separately:

  • Case 1 (\(k \ge 0\)): Since \(G(u) \in u K[[u]]\), both \(G(u)^k\) and \(G'(u)\) belong to \(K[[u]]\) (they have no negative powers of \(u\)). Hence their product \(G(u)^k G'(u) \in K[[u]]\), so \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G(u)^k G'(u)\bigr) = 0 = [x^{-1}]x^k\).
  • Case 2 (\(k = -1\)): We must show \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G(u)^{-1} G'(u)\bigr) = 1\). Since \(G(u) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\), we can write \(G(u) = u \tilde{G}(u)\) with \(\tilde{G}(0) = g_1 \in K^\times\). By Proposition 11.1(4) with \(m = 1\), \[ \frac{G'(u)}{G(u)} = u^{-1} + \frac{\tilde{G}'(u)}{\tilde{G}(u)}. \] Because \(\tilde{G}'(u) \in K[[u]]\) and \(\tilde{G}(u)^{-1} \in K[[u]]\), the second term has no negative powers of \(u\). Thus \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G'(u)/G(u)\bigr) = 1 = [x^{-1}]x^{-1}\), using zero integer divisions.
  • Case 3 (\(k \le -2\)): We must show \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G(u)^k G'(u)\bigr) = 0\).
    • Step 3a (When \(k + 1\) is invertible in \(K\), e.g., \(\mathbb{Q}\)-algebras): Since \(k \le -2\), the integer \(k + 1 \le -1\) is nonzero. Whenever \((k + 1) \in K^\times\), the chain rule gives \[ G(u)^k G'(u) = D_u\!\left(\frac{1}{k+1} G(u)^{k+1}\right). \] Because \(G(u)^k G'(u)\) is the exact formal derivative of the Laurent series \(F(u) = \frac{1}{k+1} G(u)^{k+1} \in K((u))\), Proposition 11.1(2) immediately guarantees \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G(u)^k G'(u)\bigr) = [u^{-1}] F'(u) = 0\).
    • Step 3b (Lefschetz / Universal Ring Lifting to Arbitrary Commutative Rings \(K\), Including \(\mathbb{F}_p\) When \(p \mid (k+1)\)): What if \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) and \(p\) divides \(k + 1\), so we cannot divide by \(k + 1\) in \(K\)? Write \(G(u) = g_1 u\bigl(1 + \sum_{j=1}^\infty c_j u^j\bigr)\) where \(c_j = g_1^{-1} g_{j+1} \in K\). The coefficient \([u^{-1}]\bigl(G(u)^k G'(u)\bigr)\) depends only on the first \(|k|\) coefficients \(g_1, g_1^{-1}, c_1, \dots, c_{|k|}\) and is a fixed universal polynomial \(P_k(g_1, g_1^{-1}, c_1, \dots, c_{|k|}) \in \mathbb{Z}[g_1, g_1^{-1}, c_1, \dots, c_{|k|}]\) with integer coefficients. Now consider the universal commutative ring \(R_{\text{univ}} = \mathbb{Z}[G_1, G_1^{-1}, C_1, C_2, \dots]\) where \(G_1, C_1, C_2, \dots\) are formal indeterminates. Because \(R_{\text{univ}}\) is a subring of the \(\mathbb{Q}\)-algebra \(\mathbb{Q}[G_1, G_1^{-1}, C_1, C_2, \dots]\) (where \(k + 1 \neq 0\) is invertible!), Step 3a proves that \(P_k(G_1, G_1^{-1}, C_1, \dots, C_{|k|}) = 0\) identically as a polynomial in \(R_{\text{univ}}\). Specializing the indeterminates \(G_1 \mapsto g_1, C_j \mapsto c_j\) via the unique ring homomorphism \(R_{\text{univ}} \to K\) yields \(P_k(g_1, g_1^{-1}, c_1, \dots, c_{|k|}) = 0\) in every commutative ring \(K\)! \(\blacksquare\)
TipRemark 11.1: Generalization to Valuation \(m \ge 1\)

If \(G(u) = u^m \tilde{G}(u) \in K[[u]]\) has valuation \(\operatorname{val}(G) = m \ge 1\) with \(\tilde{G}(0) \in K^\times\), the identical proof (using Proposition 11.1(4) for \(k = -1\) and the universal ring lifting for \(k \le -2\)) shows that \[ \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} A(G(u)) G'(u)\,du = m \cdot \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x)\,dx. \] When \(m = 1\) (\(G \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\)), the factor is \(m = 1\), giving exact equality.

11.2 The 6 Canonical Forms of Lagrange–Bürmann Inversion, Elegia’s Coupled Binomial Linearization, and the ABC 345 G Laurent Trick

Theorem 11.1 is an algebraic Swiss Army knife. Let us see how a single four-line calculation with Theorem 11.1 generates all 6 Canonical Forms of the Lagrange–Bürmann Inversion Formula (LIF), and why having all six forms at your fingertips eliminates tedious ad-hoc algebra during a contest.

11.2.1 The Two Standard Functional Presentations of an Implicit Series

In combinatorics, an implicitly defined generating function \(F(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) (\([x^0]F = 0, [x^1]F \in K^\times\)) almost always arrives in one of two equivalent forms:

  1. Compositional Inverse Form: \(G(F(x)) = x\) (and \(F(G(u)) = u\)), where \(G(u) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) is a known series with \(G(0) = 0\) and \(G'(0) \in K^\times\).
  2. Tree / Fixed-Point Form: \[ F(x) = x \phi(F(x)), \quad \text{where } \phi(u) \in K[[u]] \text{ satisfies } \phi(0) = [u^0]\phi(u) \in K^\times. \] Dividing by \(\phi(F(x))\) (which is a unit in \(K[[x]]\) since \(\phi(0) \in K^\times\)) gives \(\frac{F(x)}{\phi(F(x))} = x\). Thus, the Fixed-Point Form is identical to the Compositional Inverse Form via the dictionary \[ G(u) = \frac{u}{\phi(u)} \iff \phi(u) = \frac{u}{G(u)}, \quad \text{and} \quad G'(u) = \frac{\phi(u) - u\phi'(u)}{\phi(u)^2} = \frac{1}{\phi(u)}\left(1 - \frac{u\phi'(u)}{\phi(u)}\right). \]
TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Deriving LIF from Theorem 11.1

Diagnostic Signal: You are given an implicit equation \(F(x) = x\phi(F(x))\) (or \(G(F(x)) = x\)) and you need to extract the \(n\)-th coefficient \([x^n] H(F(x))\) of some composite series \(H(F(x))\) (for example, \(H(u) = u\), \(H(u) = u^k\), or \(H(u) = \frac{1}{1-u}\)).

Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:

  1. Convert \([x^n]\) to a Formal Residue: Write \([x^n] H(F(x)) = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} \frac{H(F(x))}{x^{n+1}}\,dx\).
  2. Change Variables to Linearize \(F(x)\): What makes \(\frac{H(F(x))}{x^{n+1}}\) hard is that \(F(x)\) is a complicated implicit function of \(x\). So let us choose our new variable \(u\) to be \(F(x)\)! That means we substitute \(x = G(u) = \frac{u}{\phi(u)}\), so that \(F(G(u)) = u\) becomes a trivial variable \(u\), while \(x = G(u)\) becomes the known series \(\frac{u}{\phi(u)}\) and \(dx = G'(u)\,du\): \[ [x^n] H(F(x)) = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} \frac{H(F(x))}{x^{n+1}}\,dx \;\stackrel{\text{Thm 11.1}}{=}\; \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{H(u)}{G(u)^{n+1}} G'(u)\,du. \]
  3. Branch into Form 2 (No Division) or Form 1 (Integration by Parts):
    • Branch A (Direct expansion \(\to\) Form 2): Substitute \(G(u) = u/\phi(u)\) directly without integrating by parts! Since \(G(u)^{-(n+1)} = u^{-n-1}\phi(u)^{n+1}\) and \(G'(u) = \frac{\phi(u) - u\phi'(u)}{\phi(u)^2}\), we get \[ [x^n] H(F(x)) = \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{H(u)}{u^{n+1}} \phi(u)^{n+1} \frac{\phi(u) - u\phi'(u)}{\phi(u)^2}\,du = [u^n] \left( H(u) \left(1 - \frac{u\phi'(u)}{\phi(u)}\right) \phi(u)^n \right). \] Notice that we never divided by \(n\)! This formula holds for all \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) and in all characteristics \(p\).
    • Branch B (Integration by parts \(\to\) Form 1, when \(n \in K^\times\)): Notice that \(\frac{G'(u)}{G(u)^{n+1}} = D_u\!\left(-\frac{1}{n G(u)^n}\right)\) whenever \(n\) is invertible in \(K\). Applying Formal Integration by Parts (Proposition 11.1(3)) moves the derivative off \(G(u)\) and onto \(H(u)\): \[ \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} H(u) D_u\!\left(-\frac{1}{n G(u)^n}\right) du = \frac{1}{n} \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{H'(u)}{G(u)^n}\,du = \frac{1}{n} \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{H'(u) \phi(u)^n}{u^n}\,du = \frac{1}{n}[u^{n-1}] \bigl(H'(u) \phi(u)^n\bigr). \]

11.2.2 The 6 Canonical Forms of Lagrange–Bürmann Inversion

We now state all six forms with their exact algebraic preconditions, verbalized derivations, and a concrete hand-checkable trace.

Form 1: Standard & Composite Classical LIF (Requires \(n \in K^\times\))

Let \(F(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) satisfy \(F(x) = x\phi(F(x))\) (equivalently \(G(F(x)) = x\) with \(\phi(u) = u/G(u)\) and \(\phi(0) \in K^\times\)), and let \(H(u) \in K((u))\) be any formal Laurent series. For every integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(n \in K^\times\) is a unit in \(K\): \[ [x^n] H(F(x)) = \frac{1}{n} [u^{n-1}] \left( H'(u) \phi(u)^n \right) = \frac{1}{n} [u^{n-1}] \left( H'(u) \left(\frac{u}{G(u)}\right)^n \right). \] In particular, specializing to \(H(u) = u\) (\(H'(u) = 1\)) and \(H(u) = u^k\) (\(H'(u) = k u^{k-1}\)) gives the two workhorse formulas of enumerative combinatorics: \[ [x^n] F(x) = \frac{1}{n} [u^{n-1}] \phi(u)^n, \qquad [x^n] F(x)^k = \frac{k}{n} [u^{n-k}] \phi(u)^n \quad (n \in K^\times, \; k \in \mathbb{Z}). \]

Form 2: Derivative-Free / Residue Form (另类拉格朗日反演, Valid for ALL \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) and ALL Characteristics \(p\))

Under the same hypotheses \(F(x) = x\phi(F(x))\) (\(G(F(x)) = x\)) and \(H(u) \in K((u))\), with zero requirement on \(n\) (valid even when \(n = 0\) or \(p \mid n\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p\)): \[ \begin{aligned} [x^n] H(F(x)) &= [u^n] \left( H(u) G'(u) \left(\frac{u}{G(u)}\right)^{n+1} \right) \\ &= [u^n] \left( H(u) \left(1 - \frac{u \phi'(u)}{\phi(u)}\right) \phi(u)^n \right) \\ &= [u^n] \Bigl( H(u) \phi(u)^n - u H(u) \phi'(u) \phi(u)^{n-1} \Bigr). \end{aligned} \] In particular, for \(H(u) = u^k\): \[ [x^n] F(x)^k = [u^{n-k}] \Bigl( \phi(u)^n - u \phi'(u) \phi(u)^{n-1} \Bigr) = [u^{n-k}] \phi(u)^n - [u^{n-k-1}] \bigl( \phi'(u) \phi(u)^{n-1} \bigr). \]

Form 3: Gessel’s / Jacobian-Weighted Form (Valid for ALL \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) and ALL Characteristics)

What if we divide \(H(F(x))\) by the Jacobian factor \(1 - x \phi'(F(x))\) before extracting \([x^n]\)?

  • Single-Step Derivation: Since \(x = \frac{F(x)}{\phi(F(x))}\), we have \[ 1 - x \phi'(F(x)) = 1 - \frac{F(x) \phi'(F(x))}{\phi(F(x))}. \] Substituting \(u = F(x)\) into Form 2 with the modified outer function \(\widetilde{H}(u) := \frac{H(u)}{1 - u\phi'(u)/\phi(u)}\), the factor \(\left(1 - \frac{u\phi'(u)}{\phi(u)}\right)\) in Form 2 cancels identically with the denominator of \(\widetilde{H}(u)\)! This yields: \[ [x^n] \frac{H(F(x))}{1 - x \phi'(F(x))} = [x^n] \frac{H(F(x)) \phi(F(x))}{\phi(F(x)) - F(x) \phi'(F(x))} = [u^n] \bigl( H(u) \phi(u)^n \bigr). \] Equivalently, differentiating \(F(x) = x\phi(F(x))\) gives \(F'(x) = \phi(F(x)) + x\phi'(F(x))F'(x)\), so \(\frac{1}{1 - x\phi'(F(x))} = \frac{F'(x)}{\phi(F(x))} = \frac{x F'(x)}{F(x)}\), which gives the logarithmic-derivative form: \[ [x^n] \left( H(F(x)) \frac{x F'(x)}{F(x)} \right) = [u^n] \bigl( H(u) \phi(u)^n \bigr). \]

Form 4: Monomial-Bundled Form (\(F(x) = G(x F(x)^k)\) with \(G(0) \in K^\times\))

Suppose \(F(x) \in K[[x]]\) is a power series with nonzero constant term satisfying the bundled functional equation \[ F(x) = G\!\left(x F(x)^k\right), \quad \text{where } G(w) \in K[[w]] \text{ satisfies } G(0) \in K^\times \text{ and } k \in \mathbb{Z}. \] (For example, the generalized binomial series \(\mathcal{B}_t(x) = 1 + x \mathcal{B}_t(x)^t\) has \(G(w) = 1 + w\) and \(k = t\).)

  • Diagnostic Signal & Bundling Derivation: Because \([x^0]F(x) = G(0) \neq 0\), \(F(x)\) does not belong to \(\mathcal{G}_1(K)\) (\(F(0) \neq 0\)), so we cannot apply Form 1 directly to \(F(x)\). Instead, look at the argument inside \(G(\cdot)\): it is the bundled monomial \(W(x) := x F(x)^k\), which does have valuation \(\operatorname{val}(W) = 1\)! Raising \(F(x) = G(W(x))\) to the \(k\)-th power and multiplying by \(x\) gives \[ W(x) = x F(x)^k = x G(W(x))^k \implies W(x) = x \phi(W(x)) \quad \text{with } \phi(w) = G(w)^k. \] Now \(W(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) is in standard Form 1/2! To extract \([x^n] F(x)^r\) for any \(r \in \mathbb{Z}\), write \(F(x)^r = G(W(x))^r = H(W(x))\) with \(H(w) = G(w)^r\).

    • Form 4a (Classical, when \(n \in K^\times\) or \(kn + r \in K^\times\)): Applying Form 1 to \(H(W(x)) = G(W(x))^r\) with \(\phi(w) = G(w)^k\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} [x^n] F(x)^r &= \frac{1}{n} [w^{n-1}] \Bigl( H'(w) \, \phi(w)^n \Bigr) = \frac{1}{n} [w^{n-1}] \Bigl( r G(w)^{r-1} G'(w) \cdot G(w)^{kn} \Bigr) && \text{(applying Form 1 to } H(w) = G(w)^r, \phi(w) = G(w)^k\text{)} \\ &= \frac{r}{n} [w^{n-1}] \Bigl( G(w)^{kn + r - 1} G'(w) \Bigr) && \text{(combining powers } G(w)^{r-1} G(w)^{kn}\text{)} \\ &= \frac{r}{n(kn + r)} [w^{n-1}] \frac{d}{dw}\Bigl( G(w)^{kn + r} \Bigr) && \text{(chain rule } \frac{d}{dw} G^{kn+r} = (kn+r) G^{kn+r-1} G'\text{)} \\ &= \frac{r}{kn + r} [w^n] G(w)^{kn + r} \quad (\text{when } kn + r \in K^\times). && \text{(since } [w^{n-1}] A'(w) = n [w^n] A(w)\text{)} \end{aligned} \]
    • Form 4b (Division-Free Form 4, valid for ALL \(n \ge 0\) and ALL characteristics): Applying Form 2 to \(H(w) = G(w)^r\) and \(\phi(w) = G(w)^k\) (where \(\frac{w\phi'(w)}{\phi(w)} = \frac{k w G'(w)}{G(w)}\)) gives \[ [x^n] F(x)^r = [w^n] \left( G(w)^{kn+r} \left(1 - \frac{k w G'(w)}{G(w)}\right) \right) = [w^n] G(w)^{kn+r} - k [w^{n-1}] \bigl( G'(w) G(w)^{kn+r-1} \bigr). \]

Form 5: Constant-Term Shifted Form (\(F(x) = c + x\phi(F(x))\)) & Separable Bivariate Form (\(F(x) = x A(x) \psi(B(x) F(x))\))

  1. Constant-Term Shift (\(F(x) = c + x\phi(F(x))\) with \(c \in K, \phi(c) \in K^\times\)): Define \(\tilde{F}(x) := F(x) - c \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) and \(\tilde{\phi}(u) := \phi(u + c)\). Then \(\tilde{F}(x) = x \tilde{\phi}(\tilde{F}(x))\), so for any \(H(F(x)) = \tilde{H}(\tilde{F}(x))\) with \(\tilde{H}(u) = H(u + c)\): \[ [x^n] H(F(x)) = \frac{1}{n} [u^{n-1}] \bigl( H'(u+c) \phi(u+c)^n \bigr) = [u^n] \left( H(u+c) \left(1 - \frac{u \phi'(u+c)}{\phi(u+c)}\right) \phi(u+c)^n \right). \]
  2. Separable \(x\)-Dependent Form (\(F(x) = x A(x) \psi(B(x) F(x))\) with \(A(0), B(0), \psi(0) \in K^\times\)): Multiply both sides by \(B(x)\) and bundle \(\tilde{F}(x) := B(x) F(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) and the composite base variable \(w(x) := x A(x) B(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\). Then \(\tilde{F}(x) = w(x) \psi(\tilde{F}(x))\)! We can therefore first expand \(\tilde{F}\) in powers of \(w\) via Form 1/2, or directly apply Theorem 11.1 with \(u = B(x) F(x) \implies w(x) = \frac{u}{\psi(u)}\).

Form 6: Lagrange’s Original 1770 Perturbed PDE Form (\(F(z) = A + z\phi(F(z))\)) & Good’s \(2 \times 2\) Bivariate Preview

Lagrange’s original 1770 memoir studied the root \(F = F(z)\) of \(F = A + z\phi(F)\), treating \(A\) as an independent variable (or a known function \(A(x)\)) and \(z\) as a small perturbation parameter. Applying Form 5 with \(c = A\) to \(\Phi(F)\) yields Lagrange’s Perturbed Taylor-Derivative Expansion: \[ \Phi(F) = \Phi(A) + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{z^k}{k!} \frac{d^{k-1}}{dA^{k-1}} \Bigl( \Phi'(A) \phi(A)^k \Bigr) = \Phi(A) + \sum_{k=1}^\infty z^k D_A^{(k-1)} \Bigl( \Phi'(A) \phi(A)^k \Bigr)_{/k}, \] where \(D_A^{(m)}\) denotes the \(m\)-th Hasse derivative \([u^m] f(A+u)\). Notice that setting \(u = F\) satisfies the conservation-law PDE \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = \phi(u) \frac{\partial u}{\partial A}\).

Finally, when two implicit series are coupled in a \(2 \times 2\) bipartite system \[ u_1 = x_1 \phi_1(u_1, u_2), \qquad u_2 = x_2 \phi_2(u_1, u_2), \] iterated 1D formal residue extraction (first in \(x_1 \mapsto u_1\), then in \(x_2 \mapsto u_2\)) yields the \(2 \times 2\) case of Good’s Multivariate Lagrange Formula: \[ [x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] H(u_1, u_2) = [u_1^{n_1} u_2^{n_2}] \left( H(u_1, u_2) \, \det\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 - \frac{u_1}{\phi_1}\frac{\partial \phi_1}{\partial u_1} & -\frac{u_1}{\phi_1}\frac{\partial \phi_1}{\partial u_2} \\[4pt] -\frac{u_2}{\phi_2}\frac{\partial \phi_2}{\partial u_1} & 1 - \frac{u_2}{\phi_2}\frac{\partial \phi_2}{\partial u_2} \end{pmatrix} \phi_1(u_1, u_2)^{n_1} \phi_2(u_1, u_2)^{n_2} \right). \] (In Section 11.5 Problem Adv-1, we derive the bipartite case \(u_1 = x_1 \phi_1(u_2), u_2 = x_2 \phi_2(u_1)\) purely by 1D substitution; the general \(d\)-variable Good–Lagrange theorem and MacMahon Master Theorem will be developed in full in Chapter 17.)

11.2.3 Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 4\)): Comparing Forms 1, 2, and 3 on Binary Trees

Let us verify Forms 1, 2, and 3 by hand for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\) on the non-empty binary tree generating function \[ F(x) = x\bigl(1 + F(x)^2\bigr) = x + x^3 + 2x^5 + 5x^7 + \dots, \quad \phi(u) = 1 + u^2, \] with target function \(H(u) = u^2\), so that \(H(F(x)) = F(x)^2 = x^2 + 2x^4 + 5x^6 + \dots\). Thus the true coefficients \([x^n] F(x)^2\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\) are \(0, 0, 1, 0, 2\).

  • Form 1 Trace (\([x^n]F(x)^2 = \frac{2}{n}[u^{n-2}](1+u^2)^n\) for \(n \ge 1\)):
    • \(n = 0\): Undefined in Form 1 because of division by \(n = 0\)!
    • \(n = 1\): \(\frac{2}{1}[u^{-1}](1+u^2)^1 = 0\).
    • \(n = 2\): \(\frac{2}{2}[u^0](1 + 2u^2 + u^4) = 1 \cdot 1 = \mathbf{1}\).
    • \(n = 3\): \(\frac{2}{3}[u^1](1 + 3u^2 + 3u^4 + u^6) = 0\).
    • \(n = 4\): \(\frac{2}{4}[u^2](1 + 4u^2 + 6u^4 + 4u^6 + u^8) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 4 = \mathbf{2}\).
  • Form 2 Trace (\([x^n]F(x)^2 = [u^n]\bigl(u^2(1+u^2)^n - u^2 \cdot 2u^2(1+u^2)^{n-1}\bigr) = [u^{n-2}]\bigl((1-u^2)(1+u^2)^{n-1}\bigr)\), valid for all \(n \ge 0\)):
    • \(n = 0\): \([u^{-2}](1-u^2)(1+u^2)^{-1} = \mathbf{0}\) (no division by zero!).
    • \(n = 2\): \([u^0]\bigl((1-u^2)(1+u^2)^1\bigr) = [u^0](1 - u^4) = \mathbf{1}\).
    • \(n = 4\): \([u^2]\bigl((1-u^2)(1 + 3u^2 + 3u^4 + u^6)\bigr) = 3 - 1 = \mathbf{2}\).
  • Form 3 Trace: Here \(1 - x\phi'(F(x)) = 1 - 2x F(x)\), so Form 3 says \([x^n]\frac{F(x)^2}{1 - 2xF(x)} = [u^n] u^2(1+u^2)^n = [u^{n-2}](1+u^2)^n = \binom{n}{(n-2)/2}\) (for even \(n \ge 2\)). Indeed, for \(n = 4\), \(\binom{4}{1} = 4\), which matches \([x^4]\bigl((x^2 + 2x^4 + \dots)(1 + 2x^2 + \dots)\bigr) = 2 + 2 = 4\).
WarningFailure Mode #5: LIF \(1/n\) Breakdown in Small Characteristic (\(p \mid n\) or \(n = 0\)) & Rescue via Form 2 (另类拉格朗日反演)

The Bug: A classic mistake is to code Form 1 (ans = inv[n] * coeff_u_n_minus_1) blindly when:

  1. The problem queries \(n = 0\) (where \(H(F(0)) = H(0)\) may be nonzero, e.g. \(H(u) = e^u\) or \(H(u) = \frac{1}{1-u}\)), causing a runtime division-by-zero at \(n = 0\); or
  2. The modulus \(p\) is a small prime (\(p \le N\), as in Section 11.4 and Luogu P7592 Subtask 4) and the query index \(n\) is a multiple of \(p\) (\(p \mid n\)). In \(\mathbb{F}_p\), \(n \equiv 0 \pmod p\) has no modular inverse! Even worse, \([u^{n-1}]H'(u)\phi(u)^n \equiv 0 \pmod p\) as well, so Form 1 degenerates into the indeterminate expression \(\frac{0}{0} \pmod p\).

Why It Happens: Form 1 was derived from Form 2 by writing \(G'(u) G(u)^{-(n+1)} = D_u\!\left(-\frac{1}{n} G(u)^{-n}\right)\) and integrating by parts. That anti-differentiation step is legal if and only if \(n \in K^\times\).

The Remedy: Whenever \(n = 0\) is possible, or whenever \(p \le N\), or whenever \(H(u)\) is a rational/algebraic expression whose derivative \(H'(u)\) is messier than \(H(u)\) itself, always use Form 2 (另类拉格朗日反演): \[ [x^n] H(F(x)) = [u^n] \Bigl( H(u)\bigl(\phi(u) - u\phi'(u)\bigr)\phi(u)^{n-1} \Bigr). \] Form 2 performs zero division by \(n\), requires only \(\phi(0) \in K^\times\), and evaluates \(n = 0\) and \(p \mid n\) seamlessly!

NoteAlgorithm Contract: Single-Target Lagrange Inversion Extraction (Forms 1, 2 & 3)
  • Inputs: Defining kernel \(\phi(u) \in K[[u]]\) (or \(G(u) = u/\phi(u) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\)), composite series \(H(u) \in K[[u]]\), target degree \(n \ge 0\).
  • Preconditions: \(\phi_0 = [u^0]\phi(u) \in K^\times\). Form 1 additionally requires \(n \in K^\times\) (\(p \nmid n\) and \(n \ge 1\)); Form 2 and Form 3 require no condition on \(n\) and hold in all characteristics.
  • Index & Truncation Boundaries: Truncate \(\phi(u)\) and \(H(u)\) modulo \(u^{n+1}\) (or modulo \(u^{n - v + 1}\) where \(v = \operatorname{val}(H)\)); compute \(\phi(u)^{n-1} \bmod u^{n-v+1}\) via polynomial ln/exp (when \(n < p\)) or binary exponentiation.
  • 1-Sentence Loop Invariant: The formal residue change of variables \(x = u/\phi(u)\) preserves the coefficient of \(x^{-1}\) in \(H(F(x)) x^{-n-1}\,dx = H(u)\phi(u)^{n-1}(\phi(u) - u\phi'(u)) u^{-n-1}\,du\).
  • Outputs & Complexity: Single coefficient \([x^n]H(F(x))\) in \(O(n \log n)\) time and \(O(n)\) space.

11.2.4 Elegia’s Coupled Binomial Sum Linearization (炫酷反演魔术)

One of the most celebrated modern competitive programming techniques—popularized by Elegia (炫酷反演魔术)—uses Theorem 11.1 to evaluate coupled binomial sums where the outer summation index \(n\) and the inner summation index \(k\) both appear linearly in the upper index of a binomial coefficient: \[ S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n f_k \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k}, \] given the array \(f_0, f_1, \dots, f_N\) and arbitrary integer (or modular) constants \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\).

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Elegia’s Coupled Binomial Linearization

Diagnostic Signal: You face a sum \(\sum_{k=0}^n f_k \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k}\) (or \(\sum_{k=0}^n f_k \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{k}\) after replacing \(k \leftarrow n - k\)). Standard convolution \(\sum_{k=0}^n f_k g_{n-k}\) fails because the upper index \(\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma\) mixes \(n\) and \(k\) simultaneously!

Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:

  1. Rewrite the Binomial Coefficient as a Coefficient Extraction: Look at the lower index \(n - k\). By the binomial theorem, \(\binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k} = [u^{n-k}] (1 + u)^{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}\). Shifting \([u^{n-k}]\) by \(u^k\) turns it into a single extraction \([u^n]\) independent of \(k\): \[ \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k} = [u^n] \Bigl( u^k (1 + u)^{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma} \Bigr) = [u^n] \left( (1+u)^{\alpha n + \gamma} \cdot \bigl(u(1+u)^\beta\bigr)^k \right). \]
  2. Pull \([u^n]\) Outside the Sum over \(k\): Because \([u^n]\) is a linear functional and no longer depends on \(k\), we can sum over \(k\) inside the bracket! Letting \(F(w) := \sum_{k=0}^N f_k w^k\) be the ordinary generating function of \((f_k)\), we obtain the Fundamental Coupled Binomial Residue Identity: \[ S_n = [u^n] \Bigl( (1 + u)^{\alpha n + \gamma} F\!\bigl(u(1 + u)^\beta\bigr) \Bigr) = \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{(1 + u)^{\alpha n + \gamma} F\!\bigl(u(1 + u)^\beta\bigr)}{u^{n+1}}\,du. \]
  3. Decouple According to the Problem Regime (Fixed \(n\) vs. All \(n = 0, \dots, N\)):

    • Regime A (Compute \(S_n\) for a Single Fixed \(n\) in \(O(n \log n)\) Time): When \(n\) is a single fixed integer, \((1+u)^{\alpha n + \gamma}\) is a fixed known series! We have two clean \(O(n \log n)\) options:

      • Option A1 (Direct expansion in \(u\) when \(\alpha = 0\) or via change of variables): Change variables to linearize the argument of \(F\): set \(w = G(u) = u(1+u)^\beta \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\), and let \(u = g(w) = G^{\langle -1 \rangle}(w)\) satisfy \(g(w) = w(1 + g(w))^{-\beta}\). Since \(\frac{dw}{du} = (1+u)^{\beta - 1}(1 + (\beta+1)u)\), we have \(\frac{du}{dw} = \frac{1 + g(w)}{(1+g(w))^\beta (1 + (\beta+1)g(w))}\) and \(u = \frac{w}{(1+g(w))^\beta}\). Substituting \(u = g(w)\) via Theorem 11.1 transforms \(F(u(1+u)^\beta)\) into the simple polynomial \(F(w)\): \[ S_n = [w^n] \left( F(w) \cdot \frac{\bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{(\alpha + \beta)n + \gamma + 1}}{1 + (\beta + 1)g(w)} \right). \]
      • Option A2 (Still simpler when we want to avoid finding \(g(w)\)): Instead of substituting \(w = u(1+u)^\beta\) in \(S_n\), re-index \(j = n - k\) in the original sum first: \(S_n = \sum_{j=0}^n f_{n-j} \binom{(\alpha+\beta)n - \beta j + \gamma}{j}\), or when \(\beta = 0\), \(F(u)\) is already linear!

    • Regime B (Compute \(S_n\) for ALL \(n = 0, 1, \dots, N\) Simultaneously): When \(n\) varies from \(0\) to \(N\), the factor \(\left(\frac{(1+u)^\alpha}{u}\right)^n\) depends on \(n\). To turn \(\left(\frac{u}{(1+u)^\alpha}\right)^{-n}\) into a standard power \(z^{-n}\) (which means extracting \([z^n]\)!), we perform the Formal Residue Change of Variables \[ z = \frac{u}{(1 + u)^\alpha} \iff u = U(z) \quad \text{where } U(z) = z\bigl(1 + U(z)\bigr)^\alpha. \] Here \(\frac{dz}{du} = \frac{1 - (\alpha - 1)u}{(1 + u)^{\alpha + 1}}\), so \(\frac{du}{u^{n+1}}(1+u)^{\alpha n} = \frac{dz}{z^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{1 + U(z)}{1 - (\alpha - 1)U(z)}\). Applying Theorem 11.1 yields the All-\(n\) Generating Function of \(S_n\): \[ S_n = [z^n] \left( \frac{\bigl(1 + U(z)\bigr)^{\gamma + 1}}{1 - (\alpha - 1)U(z)} \cdot F\!\Bigl(U(z)\bigl(1 + U(z)\bigr)^\beta\Bigr) \right) = [z^n] \left( \frac{\bigl(1 + U(z)\bigr)^{\gamma + 1}}{1 - (\alpha - 1)U(z)} \cdot F\!\Bigl(z\bigl(1 + U(z)\bigr)^{\alpha + \beta}\Bigr) \right). \] That is, the entire OGF \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty S_n z^n\) is simply the rational/algebraic weight \(\frac{(1+U(z))^{\gamma+1}}{1 - (\alpha-1)U(z)}\) multiplied by the composition \(F\bigl(W(z)\bigr)\) where \(W(z) = z(1+U(z))^{\alpha+\beta}\)!

      • When \(\alpha + \beta = 0\), \(W(z) = z\), so \(F(W(z)) = F(z)\) requires zero polynomial composition—just a single \(O(N \log N)\) convolution!
      • When \(\alpha \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) or \(\beta \in \{0, -1\}\), \(U(z)\) and \(W(z)\) are quadratic/rational, so \(F(W(z))\) reduces to a Taylor shift and convolution in \(O(N \log N)\) time.
      • For completely general \(\alpha, \beta\), computing \(F(W(z)) \bmod z^{N+1}\) is a degree-\(N\) polynomial composition, solvable in \(O(N \log^2 N)\) time via the Kinoshita–Li algorithm that we will build in Chapter 21 (Ch 21.3).

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\)): Fibonacci Coupled Sum

Let us test the All-\(n\) formula on the classic identity \(S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n + k}{n - k} = F_{2n+1}\) (\(1, 2, 5, 13, \dots\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\)). Here \(f_k = 1\) for all \(k \ge 0\), so \(F(w) = \frac{1}{1 - w}\), and \(\alpha = 1, \beta = 1, \gamma = 0\).

  1. The substitution is \(z = \frac{u}{1 + u}\), so \(U(z) = \frac{z}{1 - z}\) and \(1 + U(z) = \frac{1}{1 - z}\).
  2. Since \(\alpha = 1\), the denominator \(1 - (\alpha - 1)U(z) = 1 - 0 = 1\)!
  3. The argument of \(F\) is \(W(z) = z(1 + U(z))^{\alpha + \beta} = \frac{z}{(1 - z)^2}\).
  4. Plugging these into the All-\(n\) formula gives the OGF of \(S_n\) in one line: \[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty S_n z^n = \frac{1 + U(z)}{1} \cdot F\!\left(\frac{z}{(1-z)^2}\right) = \frac{1}{1 - z} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{z}{(1-z)^2}} = \frac{1 - z}{1 - 3z + z^2}. \] Expanding \(\frac{1 - z}{1 - 3z + z^2} = (1 - z)(1 + 3z + 8z^2 + 21z^3 + \dots) = 1 + 2z + 5z^2 + 13z^3 + \dots\) recovers \(S_0 = 1, S_1 = 2, S_2 = 5, S_3 = 13\) immediately!
NoteAlgorithm Contract: Elegia’s Coupled Binomial-Power Linearization
  • Inputs: Coefficient array \(f_0, \dots, f_N\) (\(F(w) = \sum_{k=0}^N f_k w^k\)), integer parameters \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\), and query degree(s) \(n \le N\).
  • Preconditions: Characteristic \(p > N\) (when using ln/exp for series powers).
  • Index & Truncation Boundaries: Solve \(U(z) = z(1 + U(z))^\alpha \bmod z^{N+1}\) with \([z^0]U = 0, [z^1]U = 1\); form \(W(z) = z(1 + U(z))^{\alpha+\beta} \bmod z^{N+1}\) with \(\operatorname{val}(W) = 1\), and evaluate \(F(W(z)) \bmod z^{N+1}\).
  • 1-Sentence Loop Invariant: Under \(z = u(1+u)^{-\alpha}\), the \(n\)-dependent binomial kernel \((1+u)^{\alpha n} u^{-n-1}\,du\) transforms into the pure Laurent power \(z^{-n-1} \frac{1+U(z)}{1-(\alpha-1)U(z)}\,dz\).
  • Outputs & Complexity: \(S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n f_k \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k}\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time for fixed \(n\) or \(\alpha + \beta = 0\) (and \(O(N \log^2 N)\) time for general all-\(n\) composition).

11.2.5 The ABC 345 G Laurent Geometric Series Trick (Nyaan / noshi91)

Another striking application of Theorem 11.1 in modern AtCoder contests (exemplified by AtCoder Beginner Contest 345 G — Sugoroku 5) solves the following problem:

Problem Archetype: Given a formal power series \(F(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) (\([x^0]F = 0, [x^1]F \in K^\times\)) and integers \(M, N \le 2.5 \times 10^5\), compute the prefix sum of coefficients \[ S_n := \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} [x^k] \bigl(F(x)^n\bigr) \quad \text{for ALL } n = 0, 1, \dots, N \text{ simultaneously.} \] (In random-walk / Sugoroku problems, \(F(x)\) is the probability generating function of a single step \(F(x) = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^D x^i\), and \(S_n = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} [x^k] F(x)^n\) is the probability that the walk has not yet reached square \(M\) after \(n\) steps.)

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: The ABC 345 G Laurent Geometric Trick

Diagnostic Signal: You need a linear combination of coefficients \(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1} c_k [x^k] F(x)^n\) (specifically the prefix sum \(c_k = 1\)) across all powers \(n = 0, \dots, N\).

Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:

  1. Express the Prefix Sum \(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1} [x^k] A(x)\) as a Single Formal Residue: For any series \(A(x)\), since \([x^k] A(x) = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x) x^{-k-1}\,dx\), summing over \(k = 0, \dots, M-1\) gives a finite geometric series in \(x^{-1}\) inside the residue: \[ \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} [x^k] A(x) = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x) \left(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1} x^{-k-1}\right) dx = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} A(x) \frac{x^{-M} - 1}{1 - x}\,dx. \] Even better: when \(A(x) = F(x)^n \in K[[x]]\) has no negative powers of \(x\), the term \(\frac{-A(x)}{1-x}\) belongs entirely to \(K[[x]]\) (it is holomorphic at \(x = 0\)), so by Proposition 11.1(1) its residue at \(x = 0\) is identically zero! We can drop the \(-1\) in the numerator and write simply: \[ S_n = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} [x^k] F(x)^n = \operatorname{Res}_{x=0} \frac{F(x)^n}{x^M (1 - x)}\,dx. \]
  2. Apply Theorem 11.1 with \(u = F(x) \iff x = G(u) = F^{\langle -1 \rangle}(u)\): Once we substitute \(x = G(u)\), the factor \(F(x)^n\) becomes the pure monomial \(u^n\)! Meanwhile, \(x^{-M}\) becomes \(G(u)^{-M} = u^{-M} \left(\frac{u}{G(u)}\right)^M\): \[ S_n = \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{u^n}{G(u)^M \bigl(1 - G(u)\bigr)} G'(u)\,du = \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} u^{-(M - n)} \left(\frac{u}{G(u)}\right)^M \frac{G'(u)}{1 - G(u)}\,du. \]
  3. Read Off All \(S_0, \dots, S_N\) as Coefficients of a Single Fixed Power Series: By Definition 11.2, \(\operatorname{Res}_{u=0} u^{-(M-n)} \Psi(u)\,du = [u^{M - 1 - n}] \Psi(u)\). Therefore, if we define the single auxiliary power series \[ \Psi(u) := \left(\frac{u}{G(u)}\right)^M \frac{G'(u)}{1 - G(u)} \in K[[u]], \] then for every \(n \ge 0\) (for \(n \ge M\), since \(\operatorname{val}(F) = 1\) implies \(\operatorname{val}(F^n) = n \ge M\), every coefficient \([x^k]F(x)^n\) for \(0 \le k \le M-1 < n\) vanishes, so \(S_n = 0\)): \[ S_n = [u^{M - 1 - n}] \Psi(u) = [u^{M - 1 - n}] \left( \left(\frac{u}{G(u)}\right)^M \frac{G'(u)}{1 - G(u)} \right). \] Once we find \(G(u) = F^{\langle -1 \rangle}(u) \bmod u^M\) (either in closed form or via Newton/Lagrange), a single polynomial pow and inv computes \(\Psi(u) \bmod u^M\) in \(O(M \log M)\) time, and reading its coefficients in reverse gives \(S_0, S_1, \dots, S_{M-1}\) all at once!
NoteAlgorithm Contract: ABC 345 G Laurent Geometric Prefix-Sum Extraction
  • Inputs: Step series \(F(x) \in \mathcal{G}_1(K)\) (\([x^0]F = 0, [x^1]F \in K^\times\)), threshold \(M \ge 1\), max step count \(N\).
  • Preconditions: \([x^1]F(x) \in K^\times\) and \(p > M\).
  • Index & Truncation Boundaries: Compute \(G(u) = F^{\langle -1 \rangle}(u) \bmod u^{M+1}\) (so \(G(u)/u \bmod u^M\) is a unit series); evaluate \(\Psi(u) = (u/G(u))^M G'(u) (1 - G(u))^{-1} \bmod u^M\), and read \(S_n = [u^{M-1-n}]\Psi(u)\) for \(0 \le n < M\) (\(S_n = 0\) for \(n \ge M\)).
  • 1-Sentence Loop Invariant: Dropping the holomorphic tail \(-F(x)^n/(1-x) \in K[[x]]\) leaves \(\operatorname{Res}_{x=0} F(x)^n x^{-M}(1-x)^{-1}\,dx = [u^{M-1-n}]\Psi(u)\) invariant for all \(n \ge 0\).
  • Outputs & Complexity: All prefix sums \(S_0, \dots, S_N\) in \(O(M \log^2 M)\) (or \(O(M \log M)\) when \(G\) is Newton-solvable) time and \(O(M)\) space.

11.3 The Three Fundamental Implicit Families (\(\mathcal{B}_t(x)\), \(\mathcal{E}_t(x)\), \(T(x)\)), Abel’s Binomial Theorem, and Raney’s Cycle Lemma

Three specific implicit families appear so pervasively across combinatorics and competitive programming that they form the “trigonometric functions” of generating function theory. With the 6 Canonical Forms of LIF in hand, we can derive all of their coefficient and convolution identities in a few lines, and then pair them with their concrete bijective twin: Raney’s Cycle Lemma.

11.3.1 Family 1: The Generalized Binomial (Fuss–Catalan) Series \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)\)

For any parameter \(t\) (an integer, indeterminate, or ring element), the generalized binomial series (or \(t\)-ary Fuss–Catalan series) \(\mathcal{B}_t(x) \in K[[x]]\) is defined by the Form 4 functional equation \[ \mathcal{B}_t(x) = 1 + x \mathcal{B}_t(x)^t, \quad \text{or equivalently for } W(x) := \mathcal{B}_t(x) - 1 = x \mathcal{B}_t(x)^t \in \mathcal{G}_1(K): \quad W(x) = x(1 + W(x))^t. \] Combinatorially, when \(t \ge 1\) is an integer, \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)\) is the OGF of \(t\)-ary plane trees by number of internal \(t\)-ary nodes (an empty tree has \(0\) internal nodes and weight \(1\); a non-empty tree has a root and an ordered \(t\)-tuple of \(t\)-ary subtrees). In particular:

  • \(t = 2\) (Standard Catalan Series): \(\mathcal{B}_2(x) = C(x) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4x}}{2x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty C_n x^n = 1 + x + 2x^2 + 5x^3 + 14x^4 + \dots\).
  • \(t = -1\) (Shifted Reciprocal Catalan): \(\mathcal{B}_{-1}(x) = 1 + \frac{x}{\mathcal{B}_{-1}(x)} \implies \mathcal{B}_{-1}(x) = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 4x}}{2} = \frac{1}{C(-x)}\).
  • \(t = 1\) (Geometric Series): \(\mathcal{B}_1(x) = 1 + x \mathcal{B}_1(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x}\).
  • \(t = 0\): \(\mathcal{B}_0(x) = 1 + x\).

Let us extract the coefficients of both \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r\) (the Lambert / Fuss–Catalan power identity) and its Jacobian-weighted companion using Form 4 and Form 3:

  1. Powers of \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)\) via Form 4: Apply Form 4 to \(F(x) = \mathcal{B}_t(x) = G(x F(x)^t)\) with \(G(w) = 1 + w\) and \(k = t\) (or Form 1 to \(W(x) = x(1+W(x))^t\) with \(H(w) = (1+w)^r\)). For \(n = 0\), \([x^0]\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r = 1\). For \(n \ge 1\): \[ [x^n] \mathcal{B}_t(x)^r = \frac{r}{tn + r} \binom{tn + r}{n} = \frac{r}{n} \binom{tn + r - 1}{n - 1}. \] Notice the two forms on the right: \(\frac{r}{tn+r}\binom{tn+r}{n}\) vs. \(\frac{r}{n}\binom{tn+r-1}{n-1}\)!
  2. Jacobian-Weighted Powers of \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)\) via Form 3: What is the Jacobian factor for \(W(x) = x\phi(W(x))\) with \(\phi(w) = (1+w)^t\)? By Form 3, \[ 1 - \frac{W \phi'(W)}{\phi(W)} = 1 - \frac{t W}{1 + W} = 1 - t + \frac{t}{1 + W} = 1 - t + t \mathcal{B}_t(x)^{-1} = \frac{1 - (t - 1)W(x)}{\mathcal{B}_t(x)}. \] Therefore, Form 3 states that dividing \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r = (1 + W(x))^r\) by this Jacobian factor strips away the \(\frac{r}{tn+r}\) factor completely for all \(n \ge 0\): \[ [x^n] \frac{\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r}{1 - t + t \mathcal{B}_t(x)^{-1}} = [x^n] \frac{\mathcal{B}_t(x)^{r+1}}{1 - (t-1)\bigl(\mathcal{B}_t(x) - 1\bigr)} = [w^n] (1 + w)^{tn + r} = \binom{tn + r}{n}. \]
  3. Hagen–Rothe Convolution Identities: Because \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r \cdot \mathcal{B}_t(x)^s = \mathcal{B}_t(x)^{r+s}\) and \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r \cdot \frac{\mathcal{B}_t(x)^s}{1 - t + t \mathcal{B}_t(x)^{-1}} = \frac{\mathcal{B}_t(x)^{r+s}}{1 - t + t \mathcal{B}_t(x)^{-1}}\), equating \([x^n]\) on both sides immediately proves the two Hagen–Rothe Convolution Formulas without a single algebraic manipulation: \[ \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{r}{tk + r}\binom{tk + r}{k} \frac{s}{t(n-k) + s}\binom{t(n-k) + s}{n - k} = \frac{r + s}{tn + r + s}\binom{tn + r + s}{n}, \] \[ \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{r}{tk + r}\binom{tk + r}{k} \binom{t(n-k) + s}{n - k} = \binom{tn + r + s}{n}. \]
WarningFailure Mode #16: The Catalan / Fuss–Catalan Negative Upper-Index Trap at \(k = 0\) (\(n = 0\))

The Bug: When evaluating \([x^k]\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r\) or Catalan power coefficients \([x^k] C(x)^r\) inside a convolution loop over \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\), programmers frequently rewrite the Fuss–Catalan formula \(\frac{r}{tk + r}\binom{tk + r}{k}\) in one of two ways:

  1. Absorbing \(\frac{1}{tk+r}\) via \(\binom{M}{k} = \frac{M}{k}\binom{M-1}{k-1}\): Writing \([x^k]\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r = \frac{r}{k}\binom{tk + r - 1}{k - 1}\), which divides by \(k = 0\) at the constant term; or

  2. Eliminating division via \(\frac{r}{tk+r}\binom{tk+r}{k} = \binom{tk+r-1}{k} - (t-1)\binom{tk+r-1}{k-1}\) (or for Catalan \(t = 2, r = 1\): \(C_k = \binom{2k}{k} - \binom{2k}{k-1}\), and for \(r = 0\): \(\binom{2k-1}{k} - \binom{2k-1}{k-1}\)) and then applying upper-index negation \(\binom{-1}{0}\) at \(k = 0\): Look closely at the identity \[ \frac{r}{tk + r}\binom{tk + r}{k} \stackrel{?}{=} \binom{tk + r - 1}{k} - (t - 1)\binom{tk + r - 1}{k - 1}. \] For \(k \ge 1\), this is an exact polynomial identity in \(t\) and \(r\) because \(\binom{M-1}{k} - (t-1)\binom{M-1}{k-1} = \binom{M}{k}\frac{M - k - (t-1)k}{M} = \frac{r}{tk+r}\binom{tk+r}{k}\). What happens at \(k = 0\) when \(r = 0\)? For \(r = 0\), the series is \(\mathcal{B}_t(x)^0 = 1\), so the true constant term at \(k = 0\) is \([x^0]\mathcal{B}_t(x)^0 = \mathbf{1}\), and for all \(k \ge 1\) the true coefficient is \(\mathbf{0}\). Now plug \(k = 0\) and \(r = 0\) into the right-hand side \(\binom{tk + r - 1}{k} - (t-1)\binom{tk + r - 1}{k - 1}\):

    • By the falling-factorial definition of generalized binomial coefficients \(\binom{\alpha}{0} := \frac{\alpha^{\underline{0}}}{0!} = 1\) for all \(\alpha \in \mathbb{C}\) (including \(\alpha = -1\)), we have \(\binom{-1}{0} = 1\) and \(\binom{-1}{-1} = 0\), which gives \(1 - 0 = 1\).
    • HOWEVER, in competitive programming libraries, programmers almost universally implement \(\binom{n}{k}\) via the factorial table fact[n] * inv_fact[k] * inv_fact[n - k] with the guard if (n < 0 || k < 0 || k > n) return 0;—or they transform \(\binom{2k-1}{k}\) at \(k = 0\) to \(\binom{-1}{0}\) and return 0 because the upper index \(2k - 1 = -1\) is negative! Conversely, if they use the symmetry \(\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}\) blindly at \(n = -1, k = 0\), they turn \(\binom{-1}{0} = 1\) into \(\binom{-1}{-1} = 0\)!

The Remedy:

  1. Always guard \(k = 0\) (\(n = 0\)) explicitly before any Fuss–Catalan / Catalan formula: for any \(r \in \mathbb{Z}\), \([x^0]\mathcal{B}_t(x)^r = 1\) unconditionally.
  2. For \(k \ge 1\) and \(r = 0\), \([x^k]\mathcal{B}_t(x)^0 = 0\).
  3. Never pass a negative upper index into a standard C(n, k) factorial lookup table; handle \(k = 0\) outside the loop and run the binomial table only for \(k \ge 1\) where \(tk + r - 1 \ge 0\).

11.3.2 Family 2 & 3: The Generalized Exponential Series \(\mathcal{E}_t(x)\), Cayley’s Rooted Tree Series \(T(x)\), and Abel’s Binomial Theorem

Next, consider the generalized exponential series \(\mathcal{E}_t(x) \in K[[x]]\) (assuming \(\mathbb{Q} \subseteq K\) or \(n < p\) so that \(n!\) is invertible), defined by the Form 4 functional equation \[ \mathcal{E}_t(x) = \exp\!\bigl(x \mathcal{E}_t(x)^t\bigr), \quad \text{or for } W(x) := x \mathcal{E}_t(x)^t \in \mathcal{G}_1(K): \quad W(x) = x e^{t W(x)}. \] When \(t = 1\), \(T(x) := x \mathcal{E}_1(x)\) satisfies Cayley’s rooted labeled tree equation \[ T(x) = x e^{T(x)} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{n-1} \frac{x^n}{n!}. \] Let us apply Form 4 and Form 3 to \(\mathcal{E}_t(x) = G(x \mathcal{E}_t(x)^t)\) with \(G(w) = e^w\) and \(k = t\):

  1. Powers of \(\mathcal{E}_t(x)\) via Form 4: Since \(G(w)^{tn + r} = e^{(tn+r)w}\), we have \([w^n] G(w)^{tn+r} = \frac{(tn+r)^n}{n!}\). Therefore, Form 3 gives for all \(n \ge 0\): \[ [x^n] \frac{\mathcal{E}_t(x)^r}{1 - t x \mathcal{E}_t(x)^t} = [x^n] \frac{\mathcal{E}_t(x)^r}{1 - t \ln \mathcal{E}_t(x)} = [w^n] e^{(tn + r)w} = \frac{(tn + r)^n}{n!}. \]
  3. 11.3.4 Raney’s Cycle Lemma & The Łukasiewicz Pre-Order Tree Bijection

Why does the factor \(\frac{r}{tn + r}\) (or \(\frac{k}{m}\)) appear so universally in Lagrange Inversion? Because algebraically dividing by \(m\) in LIF corresponds combinatorially to selecting the unique valid cyclic shift among \(m\) cyclic shifts of a word! This principle is captured by Raney’s Lemma (and Dvoretzky–Motzkin’s Cycle Lemma).

ImportantTheorem 11.2: Raney’s Cycle Lemma (Both \(+1\) and \(-k\) Versions)
  1. Standard \(+1\) Prefix Version (Steps Bounded Above by \(+1\)): Let \((s_1, s_2, \dots, s_m)\) be a sequence of \(m\) integers such that \(s_i \le 1\) for all \(i\) and the total sum is \[ \sum_{i=1}^m s_i = +1. \] Then among the \(m\) cyclic shifts \(s^{(j)} = (s_j, s_{j+1}, \dots, s_m, s_1, \dots, s_{j-1})\) (\(1 \le j \le m\)), there is a unique cyclic shift for which all \(m\) prefix sums \(P_r = \sum_{i=1}^r s^{(j)}_i\) (\(1 \le r \le m\)) are strictly positive (\(P_r \ge 1\)).
  2. General \(+k\) / \(-k\) Forest Version (Steps Bounded Below by \(-1\)): Dually (by negating and reversing the sequence), let \((a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m)\) be a sequence of \(m\) integers such that \(a_i \ge -1\) for all \(i\) and the total sum is \[ \sum_{i=1}^m a_i = -k \quad (\text{for some integer } k \ge 1). \] Consider the \(m\) cyclic shifts \(a^{(j)}\) (\(1 \le j \le m\)). Say that a cyclic shift \(a^{(j)}\) is \(k\)-valid if its running sum starting from initial reserve \(k\) stays strictly positive until the very last step—that is, \[ k + \sum_{i=1}^r a^{(j)}_i \ge 1 \quad \text{for all } 1 \le r \le m - 1, \quad \text{and} \quad k + \sum_{i=1}^m a^{(j)}_i = 0. \] Then among the \(m\) cyclic shifts \(j \in \{1, \dots, m\}\), there are exactly \(k\) distinct starting indices \(j\) whose cyclic shift is \(k\)-valid! (When all \(k\) valid shifts of \((a_1, \dots, a_m)\) are distinct as sequences, exactly a \(\frac{k}{m}\) fraction of all cyclic shifts are valid.)

Complete Step-by-Step Combinatorial Proof

We prove Version 2 (which includes Version 1 by negating \(s_i = -a_{m+1-i}\) with \(k = 1\)). Extend \((a_1, \dots, a_m)\) periodically with period \(m\) to an infinite sequence \(a_1, a_2, \dots\), and consider the infinite prefix sum profile \(S_0 = 0, S_t = \sum_{i=1}^t a_i\) for \(t \ge 1\).

  • Step 1 (Discrete Intermediate Value Property): Since \(a_i \ge -1\), whenever the profile drops, it can drop by at most \(1\) per step (\(S_t - S_{t-1} \ge -1\)), though it may jump upward by any nonnegative integer. Therefore, as \(S_t\) drifts downward by \(-k\) over each period of length \(m\) (\(S_{t+m} = S_t - k\)), it cannot skip over any new record minimum integer!
  • Step 2 (Identifying the \(k\) Record Minima per Period): For each integer height \(h \le 0\), let \(t(h) = \min\{t \ge 0 : S_t = h\}\) be the first time the profile ever hits height \(h\). Because \(S_t\) drops by at most \(1\) per step, \(S_t > h\) for all \(0 \le t < t(h)\), and for every \(t > t(h)\) within the next period \(t(h) < t < t(h) + m\), the periodic drift \(S_{t+m} = S_t - k\) ensures that the profile starting at \(j = t(h) + 1\) never drops by \(k\) before step \(m\), and hits \(S_{t(h)+m} - S_{t(h)} = -k\) at step \(m\) (with \(a_{t(h)+m} = -1\) being the step that reaches the new record minimum \(h - k\)).
  • Step 3 (Exactly \(k\) Residue Classes Modulo \(m\)): Because \(S_{t+m} = S_t - k\), shifting time by one full period \(m\) shifts the record minimum height down by exactly \(k\): \(t(h - k) = t(h) + m\). Therefore, the first-hitting times \(t(0), t(-1), \dots, t(-(k-1))\) have pairwise distinct residues modulo \(m\), and their \(k\) cyclic shifts \(j \equiv t(-r) + 1 \pmod m\) (\(r = 0, 1, \dots, k-1\)) are the exact \(k\) valid cyclic shifts of \((a_1, \dots, a_m)\)! \(\blacksquare\)

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(m = 5\), \(k = 1\)) & The Łukasiewicz Pre-Order Bijection

Why does Raney’s Lemma immediately prove the Lagrange Inversion formula \([x^n] F(x)^k = \frac{k}{n}[u^{n-k}]\phi(u)^n\) for trees?

Consider a plane rooted tree \(T\) with \(m\) total vertices (or a forest of \(k\) plane rooted trees with \(n\) total vertices). Traverse the tree in pre-order DFS (\(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_m\)) and let \(c(v_i) \ge 0\) be the number of children of vertex \(v_i\). Think of maintaining a stack of “unexplored child stubs”:

  • Initially, before visiting the root of a single tree (\(k = 1\)), we have \(1\) stub (the root itself).
  • When we visit vertex \(v_i\) with \(c(v_i)\) children, we consume \(1\) stub (for \(v_i\) itself) and push \(c(v_i)\) new stubs onto the stack, changing the number of stubs by \(a_i := c(v_i) - 1 \ge -1\).
  • Across all \(m\) vertices of the tree, the total number of children is \(\sum_{i=1}^m c(v_i) = m - 1\), so \(\sum_{i=1}^m a_i = (m - 1) - m = -1\). Moreover, the stack of stubs stays strictly positive (\(1 + \sum_{i=1}^r a_i \ge 1\)) for all \(1 \le r \le m - 1\) and becomes \(0\) at \(r = m\)!

Let us trace all \(m = 5\) cyclic shifts of the Łukasiewicz step sequence \(a = (+2, -1, +1, -1, -1)\) (coming from a 5-vertex tree whose pre-order child counts are \((3, 0, 2, 0, 0)\), so \(\sum a_i = -1\) and \(k = 1\)):

Out of all \(5\) cyclic shifts, exclusively \(j = 1\) keeps the reserve \(\ge 1\) through \(r = 4\)! And how many unconstrained sequences \((c_1, \dots, c_n)\) of \(n\) child counts have total sum \(\sum_{i=1}^n (c_i - 1) = -k \iff \sum_{i=1}^n c_i = n - k\), weighted by \(\prod_{i=1}^n \phi_{c_i}\)? By definition of polynomial multiplication, that unconstrained sum is \([u^{n-k}] \phi(u)^n\)! Multiplying by the Raney fraction \(\frac{k}{n}\) of valid cyclic shifts recovers Form 1 of Lagrange Inversion: \[ [x^n] F(x)^k = \frac{k}{n} [u^{n-k}] \phi(u)^n. \]

11.4 Characteristic-\(p\) Lagrange Inversion (\(n \ge p\)) & Base-\(p\) Frobenius Decimation for Huge LIF (\(n \le 10^{18} \bmod p\))

11.4.1 Anatomy of the 5 Modular Breakdowns When \(p \le n\)

Suppose we want to compute \([x^n] H(F(x)) \bmod p\) where \(F(x) = x \Phi(F(x))\) (\(\Phi(u), H(u) \in \mathbb{F}_p[u]\) are polynomials of small degree \(\le d\)), the prime modulus \(p\) is small (e.g. \(p \le 10^5\)), and \(n\) is huge (e.g. \(n \le 10^{18}\), so \(n \ge p\)). Let us catalog the five standard operations that break down in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\) when \(p \le n\):

  1. Formal Integration \(\int x^{kp-1}\,dx\): Impossible because dividing by \(kp \equiv 0 \pmod p\) is undefined.
  2. Formal Logarithm \(\ln F(x) = \int \frac{F'(x)}{F(x)}\,dx\) and Exponential \(\exp G(x)\): Undefined beyond degree \(p - 1\) because \(p! \equiv 0 \pmod p\). Thus we cannot compute \(\Phi(u)^n \bmod u^{n+1}\) via \(\exp(n \ln \Phi(u))\).
  3. ODE / D-Finite Recurrence Stepping for \(\Phi(u)^n\): In characteristic \(0\), \(P(u) = \Phi(u)^n\) satisfies \(\Phi(u) P'(u) = n \Phi'(u) P(u)\), giving an \(O(d)\)-term recurrence that divides by the coefficient index \(k\). In \(\mathbb{F}_p\), every multiple \(k = jp\) divides by \(0 \pmod p\)!
  4. Classical LIF Form 1 Division by \(\frac{1}{n}\): Whenever \(p \mid n\), the factor \(\frac{1}{n} \bmod p\) in \([x^n]H(F(x)) = \frac{1}{n}[u^{n-1}]H'(u)\Phi(u)^n\) divides by zero!
  5. Naive Binary Exponentiation of \(\Phi(u)^n \bmod u^{n+1}\): Even though binary exponentiation uses no division, for \(n \le 10^{18}\) a degree-\(n\) polynomial does not fit in memory!

11.4.2 Combining Form 2 Derivative-Free LIF with the Base-\(p\) Frobenius Identity

How do we defeat all five breakdowns simultaneously?

  • Defeating Breakdown 4 (\(p \mid n\)): We replace Form 1 by Form 2 (另类拉格朗日反演)! Provided \(\Phi(0) \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\) and \(n \ge 1\), Form 2 expresses \([x^n] H(F(x))\) without any division by \(n\): \[ [x^n] H(F(x)) = [u^n] \Bigl( \underbrace{H(u)\bigl(\Phi(u) - u\Phi'(u)\bigr)}_{A_0(u)} \cdot \Phi(u)^{n-1} \Bigr) = [u^n] \Bigl( A_0(u) \Phi(u)^{n-1} \Bigr) \pmod p. \] Notice that \(A_0(u) := H(u)\bigl(\Phi(u) - u\Phi'(u)\bigr) \in \mathbb{F}_p[u]\) is a fixed polynomial of degree at most \(\deg H + \deg \Phi\), and \(m := n - 1 \ge 0\) is a nonnegative integer exponent!

  • Defeating Breakdowns 1–3 & 5 (\(n \le 10^{18}\)): Now our entire problem is reduced to a clean Lucas-like polynomial question in \(\mathbb{F}_p[u]\):

    Given two low-degree polynomials \(A(u), \Phi(u) \in \mathbb{F}_p[u]\) of degree \(\le d\), and huge integers \(k, m \le 10^{18}\), how do we extract \([u^k] \bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^m\bigr) \bmod p\) in \(O(\log_p m)\) steps?

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Base-\(p\) Frobenius Digit Decimation

Diagnostic Signal: You need to extract \([u^k] \bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^m\bigr) \bmod p\) where \(p\) is prime, \(\deg A, \deg \Phi \le d\) are small, and \(k, m \le 10^{18}\) are huge.

Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:

  1. Exploit the Frobenius Endomorphism in \(\mathbb{F}_p[u]\): In a commutative ring of prime characteristic \(p\), the binomial coefficients \(\binom{p}{j}\) (\(1 \le j \le p-1\)) are all divisible by \(p\), so \((a + b)^p \equiv a^p + b^p \pmod p\). Furthermore, by Fermat’s Little Theorem, \(c^p \equiv c \pmod p\) for every scalar \(c \in \mathbb{F}_p\). Applying this to the polynomial \(\Phi(u) = \sum_{i=0}^d \phi_i u^i \in \mathbb{F}_p[u]\) gives the Frobenius Identity: \[ \Phi(u)^p \equiv \sum_{i=0}^d \phi_i^p (u^i)^p \equiv \sum_{i=0}^d \phi_i u^{pi} = \Phi(u^p) \pmod p. \] That is, raising \(\Phi(u)\) to the \(p\)-th power in \(\mathbb{F}_p[u]\) simply replaces \(u\) by \(u^p\)—it is a sparse polynomial in \(u^p\)!
  2. Peel Off the Lowest Base-\(p\) Digits of \(k\) and \(m\): Divide both the target coefficient index \(k\) and the exponent \(m\) by \(p\) with remainder: \[ k = p k' + k_0 \quad (0 \le k_0 < p), \qquad m = p m' + m_0 \quad (0 \le m_0 < p). \] By the Frobenius identity, \[ \Phi(u)^m = \Phi(u)^{p m' + m_0} = \Phi(u)^{m_0} \cdot \bigl(\Phi(u)^p\bigr)^{m'} \equiv \Phi(u)^{m_0} \cdot \Phi(u^p)^{m'} \pmod p. \] Multiply by \(A(u)\): \[ A(u) \Phi(u)^m \equiv \underbrace{\bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^{m_0}\bigr)}_{B(u)} \cdot \Phi(u^p)^{m'} \pmod p. \]
  3. Decimate the Congruence Class \(k_0 \pmod p\) of Exponents: Look at the right-hand side \(B(u) \cdot \Phi(u^p)^{m'}\). Because \(\Phi(u^p)^{m'}\) is a polynomial purely in \(u^p\), every term in \(\Phi(u^p)^{m'}\) has exponent divisible by \(p\)! Therefore, when we multiply \(B(u) = \sum_{i} b_i u^i\) by \(\Phi(u^p)^{m'}\) and extract the coefficient of \(u^{p k' + k_0}\), only terms \(b_i u^i\) of \(B(u)\) whose exponent satisfies \(i \equiv k_0 \pmod p\) (i.e. \(i = p j + k_0\)) can ever contribute to \([u^{p k' + k_0}]\)! Define the decimated polynomial \(A_{\text{next}}(v) \in \mathbb{F}_p[v]\) by picking out every \(p\)-th coefficient of \(B(u) = A(u) \Phi(u)^{m_0}\) starting at offset \(k_0\): \[ A_{\text{next}}(v) := \sum_{j \ge 0} \bigl([u^{p j + k_0}] \bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^{m_0}\bigr)\bigr) v^j. \] Then setting \(v = u^p\) immediately yields the exact reduction \[ [u^{p k' + k_0}] \bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^{p m' + m_0}\bigr) \equiv [v^{k'}] \bigl(A_{\text{next}}(v) \Phi(v)^{m'}\bigr) \pmod p. \]

11.4.3 Degree Bound Invariant & Complete Algorithm Contract

Why doesn’t the degree of \(A_{\text{next}}(v)\) blow up across the \(O(\log_p n)\) iterations? Let us check the exact degree invariant!

ImportantProposition 11.2: Degree Stability Invariant of Base-\(p\) Frobenius Decimation

Intermediate 1 (#196) — [清华集训2016] 你的生命已如风中残烛 (Raney’s Cycle Lemma on Card-Draw Permutations)

  • Source: Luogu P6672 — [清华集训2016] 你的生命已如风中残烛 (Curriculum Pool #196, 4B#5)
  • Prerequisites: §11.3.4 (Raney’s Cycle Lemma, Theorem 11.2)

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You have a deck of \(m\) distinct cards. Among them, \(n\) are special cards with positive integer draw weights \(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n \ge 1\) satisfying \[ \sum_{i=1}^n w_i = m, \] and the remaining \(m - n\) cards are normal cards with draw weight \(w_i = 0\) (\(n < i \le m\)).

You start with \(1\) card in your hand (or equivalently, you arrange all \(m\) cards in a permutation \(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_m\) and begin with \(1\) action point; playing card \(p_j\) costs \(1\) action point and grants \(w_{p_j}\) new action points, changing your net action points by \(w_{p_j} - 1\)). A permutation \((p_1, \dots, p_m)\) of the \(m\) cards is valid if your action points remain strictly positive (\(\ge 1\)) after each of the first \(m - 1\) cards, so that you successfully play all \(m\) cards (ending with \(1 + \sum_{j=1}^m (w_{p_j} - 1) = 1 + 0 = 1\) action point after the \(m\)-th card, or \(0\) after playing one final dummy \(0\)-weight card).

Given \(n \le 40\) and \(w_1, \dots, w_n\) with \(m = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \le 10^5\), compute the number of valid permutations of the \(m\) cards modulo \(998244353\).

Required Complexity: \(O(m)\) time and \(O(1)\) space!

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\)): Suppose \(n = 1\) and \(w_1 = 3\), so \(m = 3\) with \(1\) special card of weight \(3\) and \(m - n = 2\) normal cards of weight \(0\). For distinct normal cards \(0_A, 0_B\), only the \(2\) permutations \((3, 0_A, 0_B)\) and \((3, 0_B, 0_A)\) keep action points strictly positive (\(1 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1\)), giving \(\frac{m!}{m - n + 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2\) (and \(\frac{m!}{(m - n + 1)!} = \frac{6}{6} = 1\) when normal cards are indistinguishable).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Look at the net change \(a_j := w_{p_j} - 1\) at step \(j\): since \(w_i \ge 0\), every step satisfies \(a_j \ge -1\), and the only steps with \(a_j = -1\) are the \(m - n\) normal cards (weight \(0\)). Furthermore, the sum over all \(m\) cards is \(\sum_{i=1}^m (w_i - 1) = m - m = 0\), while the condition is \(1 + \sum_{j=1}^r a_j \ge 1 \iff \sum_{j=1}^r a_j \ge 0\) for all \(1 \le r \le m\). What cyclic-shift invariant governs integer sequences bounded below by \(-1\) whose prefix sums stay nonnegative?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

This is the exact signature of Raney’s Cycle Lemma (Theorem 11.2)! Raney’s Lemma requires the total sum of the sequence to be \(-1\) (not \(0\)). How do we turn a sequence of \(m\) steps with sum \(0\) and all prefix sums \(\ge 0\) into a sequence of \(m + 1\) steps with sum \(-1\) and all proper prefix sums \(\ge 0\)? Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Now consider all \((m + 1)!\) permutations of the \(m + 1\) cards (which consist of the \(n\) special cards and \(m - n + 1\) normal cards of weight \(0\)). NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Translating the Card Process to Raney’s Cycle Lemma

For each card \(i \in \{1, \dots, m\}\), define its net step value \(a_i := w_i - 1\).

  • For the \(n\) special cards (\(1 \le i \le n\)), \(w_i \ge 1 \implies a_i \ge 0\).
  • For the \(m - n\) normal cards (\(n + 1 \le i \le m\)), \(w_i = 0 \implies a_i = -1\).
  • The total sum of all \(m\) step values is \[ \sum_{i=1}^m a_i = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i - m = m - m = 0. \] A permutation \((p_1, \dots, p_m)\) of \(\{1, \dots, m\}\) is valid if and only if all prefix sums are nonnegative: \[ \sum_{j=1}^r a_{p_j} \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } 1 \le r \le m. \]

2. Appending the \((m+1)\)-st Dummy Card to Create Sum \(-1\)

Introduce one additional normal card with label \(m + 1\) and weight \(w_{m+1} = 0\), so \(a_{m+1} = -1\). Now we have \(m + 1\) distinct cards in total:

  • \(n\) special cards with \(a_i \ge 0\), and
  • \((m - n + 1)\) normal cards with \(a_i = -1\),

whose total sum is \[ \sum_{i=1}^{m+1} a_i = 0 + (-1) = -1. \] Suppose \((\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots, \pi_{m+1})\) is any permutation of \(\{1, 2, \dots, m + 1\}\) that is \(1\)-valid in the sense of Raney’s Lemma (Theorem 11.2)—meaning that \[ \sum_{j=1}^r a_{\pi_j} \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } 1 \le r \le m, \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{m+1} a_{\pi_j} = -1. \] Look at the last step \(\pi_{m+1}\) of any such \(1\)-valid permutation: since \(\sum_{j=1}^m a_{\pi_j} \ge 0\) and \(\sum_{j=1}^{m+1} a_{\pi_j} = -1\), we have \[ a_{\pi_{m+1}} = -1 - \sum_{j=1}^m a_{\pi_j} \le -1. \] Because \(a_i \ge -1\) for all cards, this forces \(a_{\pi_{m+1}} = -1\) and \(\sum_{j=1}^m a_{\pi_j} = 0\)! That means:

  1. In every \(1\)-valid permutation \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_{m+1})\) of \(\{1, \dots, m + 1\}\), the last card \(\pi_{m+1}\) is guaranteed to be one of the \(m - n + 1\) normal cards!
  2. Furthermore, since all \(m - n + 1\) normal cards have the exact same step value \(a_i = -1\), conditioning on which of the \(m - n + 1\) normal cards sits at position \(m + 1\) gives the exact same count of valid prefixes \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m)\)! Specifically, the valid permutations \((p_1, \dots, p_m)\) of the original \(m\) cards are in exact \(1\)-to-\(1\) correspondence with the \(1\)-valid permutations \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_{m+1})\) that end with \(\pi_{m+1} = m + 1\).

3. Exact Double Count via Cyclic Shifts

Partition all \((m + 1)!\) permutations of \(\{1, 2, \dots, m + 1\}\) into \(\frac{(m + 1)!}{m + 1} = m!\) cyclic-shift equivalence classes, each of size \(m + 1\).

By Theorem 11.2 (Raney’s Cycle Lemma) with \(k = 1\), within each cyclic class of \(m + 1\) shifts, literally \(1\) cyclic shift out of the \(m + 1\) shifts is \(1\)-valid! Therefore, the total number of \(1\)-valid permutations of \(\{1, 2, \dots, m + 1\}\) is \[ N_{\text{total}} = \frac{(m + 1)!}{m + 1} = m!. \] By symmetry among the \(m - n + 1\) normal cards (all of which have identical step weight \(-1\), and one of which must occupy position \(\pi_{m+1}\)), the fraction of \(1\)-valid permutations that specifically place the dummy card \(\pi_{m+1} = m + 1\) at the end is \(\frac{1}{m - n + 1}\).

Let us verify this on the small hand-checkable cases \(m = 2\) and \(m = 3\):

  • For \(n = 1, w_1 = 2 \implies m = 2\) (\(1\) special card of weight \(2\), and \(m - n = 1\) normal card of weight \(0\)), the two permutations of \((w_1 = 2, w_2 = 0)\) are:
    • \((2, 0)\): hand size goes \(1 \xrightarrow{+1} 2 \xrightarrow{-1} 1 \ge 1\) (Valid).
    • \((0, 2)\): hand size goes \(1 \xrightarrow{-1} \mathbf{0}\) after the first card, leaving no cards in hand to play the second card (Invalid). Indeed, \(\frac{m!}{m - n + 1} = \frac{2!}{2 - 1 + 1} = \mathbf{1}\).
  • For \(n = 1, w_1 = 3 \implies m = 3\) (one card of weight \(3\), two distinct normal cards \(0_A, 0_B\)), the valid permutations must start with \(3\), so \((3, 0_A, 0_B)\) and \((3, 0_B, 0_A)\) are valid (\(2\) out of \(6\)), matching \(\frac{m!}{m - n + 1} = \frac{3!}{3 - 1 + 1} = \mathbf{2}\).

Thus, dividing \(N_{\text{total}} = m!\) by the \(m - n + 1\) symmetric normal cards gives the exact answer for distinct cards: \[ \text{Ans}_{\text{distinct}} = \frac{m!}{m - n + 1} \pmod{998244353}, \] and when the \(m - n\) normal cards are treated as indistinguishable (as in Luogu P6672, where you choose the sequence of card types and the \(m - n\) zero-weight cards are identical, dividing by \((m - n)!\)), we divide \(\text{Ans}_{\text{distinct}}\) by \((m - n)!\) to obtain: \[ \text{Ans}_{\text{Luogu P6672}} = \frac{m!}{(m - n + 1)!} = \prod_{j = m - n + 2}^{m} j \pmod{998244353}. \] In fact, this product of \(n - 1\) integers runs in \(O(n)\) time and \(O(1)\) space!

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

This is the exact archetype of Elegia’s Coupled Binomial Sum Linearization (§11.2.4)! NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Step 1: Converting the Coupled Binomial Sum into a Formal Residue

Let \(F(w) := \sum_{k=0}^n f_k w^k \in \mathbb{F}_p[w]\). By the generalized binomial theorem, for each \(0 \le k \le n\), \[ \binom{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma}{n - k} = [u^{n - k}] (1 + u)^{\alpha n + \beta k + \gamma} = [u^n] \Bigl( (1 + u)^{\alpha n + \gamma} \cdot \bigl(u(1 + u)^\beta\bigr)^k \Bigr). \] Summing over \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\) (and noting that terms \(k > n\) would have \(\operatorname{val}\bigl((u(1+u)^\beta)^k\bigr) = k > n\), contributing \(0\) to \([u^n]\)) yields \[ S_n = [u^n] \Bigl( (1 + u)^{\alpha n + \gamma} F\!\bigl(u(1 + u)^\beta\bigr) \Bigr) = \operatorname{Res}_{u=0} \frac{(1 + u)^{\alpha n + \gamma} F\!\bigl(u(1 + u)^\beta\bigr)}{u^{n + 1}}\,du. \]

2. Step 2: Change of Variables \(w = u(1 + u)^\beta\) via Theorem 11.1

To turn \(F\bigl(u(1 + u)^\beta\bigr)\) into the simple polynomial \(F(w)\), define the substitution \(w = G(u) := u(1 + u)^\beta \in \mathcal{G}_1(\mathbb{F}_p)\), and let \(u = g(w) = G^{\langle -1 \rangle}(w) \in \mathcal{G}_1(\mathbb{F}_p)\) be its compositional inverse. Let us express \(g'(w)\) and \(\frac{w}{g(w)}\) cleanly in terms of \(g(w)\):

  1. Since \(w = g(w)\bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^\beta\), we have \[ \frac{w}{g(w)} = \bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^\beta. \]
  2. Differentiating \(w = u(1 + u)^\beta\) with respect to \(u\) gives \[ \frac{dw}{du} = (1 + u)^\beta + \beta u(1 + u)^{\beta - 1} = (1 + u)^{\beta - 1} \bigl(1 + (\beta + 1)u\bigr), \] so by the inverse function theorem for formal power series, \[ g'(w) = \frac{du}{dw} = \frac{1}{\bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{\beta - 1} \bigl(1 + (\beta + 1)g(w)\bigr)}. \] Now apply Theorem 11.1 (in reverse, substituting \(u = g(w)\) with \(du = g'(w)\,dw\)) to the residue for \(S_n\): \[ \begin{aligned} S_n &= \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{\bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{\alpha n + \gamma} F(w)}{g(w)^{n + 1}} g'(w)\,dw \\ &= \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{F(w)}{w^{n + 1}} \left(\frac{w}{g(w)}\right)^{n + 1} \bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{\alpha n + \gamma} g'(w)\,dw \\ &= [w^n] \left( F(w) \cdot \bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{\beta(n + 1)} \cdot \bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{\alpha n + \gamma} \cdot \frac{1}{\bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{\beta - 1} \bigl(1 + (\beta + 1)g(w)\bigr)} \right) \\ &= [w^n] \left( F(w) \cdot \frac{\bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{(\alpha + \beta)n + \gamma + 1}}{1 + (\beta + 1)g(w)} \right). \end{aligned} \]

3. Step 3: Computing \(1 + g(w)\) in Closed Form via Form 4 LIF (Without Newton’s Method!)

Do we even need Newton’s method to find \(g(w) \bmod w^{n+1}\)? No! Look at the equation satisfied by \(Y(w) := 1 + g(w)\): \[ Y(w) = 1 + g(w) = 1 + w \bigl(1 + g(w)\bigr)^{-\beta} = 1 + w Y(w)^{-\beta}. \] This is literally the Fuss–Catalan / Generalized Binomial Series (§11.3.1) \(Y(w) = \mathcal{B}_{-\beta}(w)\) with parameter \(t = -\beta\)! Even better, look at the multiplier of \(F(w)\) inside \([w^n]\): \[ \Psi(w) := \frac{Y(w)^{(\alpha + \beta)n + \gamma + 1}}{1 + (\beta + 1)(Y(w) - 1)} = \frac{Y(w)^{(\alpha + \beta)n + \gamma}}{1 + \beta - \beta Y(w)^{-1}}. \] Compare the denominator \(1 + \beta - \beta Y(w)^{-1}\) with the Master Duality Table (§11.3.3) for \(Y(w) = \mathcal{B}_t(w)\) with \(t = -\beta\): \[ 1 - t + t \mathcal{B}_t(w)^{-1} = 1 - (-\beta) + (-\beta) Y(w)^{-1} = 1 + \beta - \beta Y(w)^{-1}. \] It is literally the Form 3 Jacobian factor of \(\mathcal{B}_{-\beta}(w)\)! Therefore, by the Form 3 Fuss–Catalan identity \([w^m] \frac{\mathcal{B}_t(w)^r}{1 - t + t \mathcal{B}_t(w)^{-1}} = \binom{tm + r}{m}\) with \(t = -\beta\) and \(r = (\alpha + \beta)n + \gamma\), the \(m\)-th coefficient of \(\Psi(w)\) has the instantaneous closed form \[ [w^m] \Psi(w) = \binom{-\beta m + (\alpha + \beta)n + \gamma}{m} = \binom{\alpha n + \beta(n - m) + \gamma}{m}, \] and even if we instead evaluate \([x^n] H(F(x))\) for a general bundled series \(F(x) = G(x F(x)^\beta)\) where \(G(w)\) is an arbitrary power series (not just \(1 + w\)), computing \(g(w) \bmod w^{n+1}\) via one Newton/LIF step and one polynomial pow extracts \([w^n] F(w)\Psi(w)\) in \(O(n \log n)\) time!

Mastery 1 (#114) — Slime and Sequences (Hard Version — Eulerian Bivariate EGF, Formal Residue & L’Hôpital Double-Pole Cancellation)

  • Source: Codeforces 1349F2 — Slime and Sequences (Hard Version) (Curriculum Pool #114, 1A#30 & 3B#15)
  • Prerequisites: Chapter 3 (inv, ln, exp, pow), Chapter 4 (Permutations & Eulerian numbers), §11.1 (Theorem 11.1), §11.2 (LIF & Laurent Geometric Series Trick)

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

A sequence \(a = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n)\) of positive integers is called good if for every integer \(k > 1\) that appears at least once in \(a\), the first occurrence of \(k - 1\) in \(a\) appears before the last occurrence of \(k\) in \(a\).

Given \(n\) (\(1 \le n \le 10^5\)), for every \(k = 1, 2, \dots, n\), compute the total number of occurrences of \(k\) summed over all good sequences of length \(n\), modulo \(998244353\).

Required Complexity: \(O(n \log n)\) time and \(O(n)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\)): For \(n = 1\), the only good sequence is \((1)\), giving \(\text{Ans}_1 = 1\). For \(n = 2\), the good sequences are \((1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 1)\) (whereas \((2, 2)\) is invalid because \(2\) appears without \(1\)), so \(k = 1\) appears \(2 + 1 + 1 = 4\) times (\(\text{Ans}_1 = 4\)) and \(k = 2\) appears \(0 + 1 + 1 = 2\) times (\(\text{Ans}_2 = 2\)).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

First find a bijection between good sequences of length \(n\) and permutations \(\pi \in S_n\): given a good sequence \(a\), list the indices where \(a_i = 1\) in decreasing order, then the indices where \(a_i = 2\) in decreasing order, and so on. Why is this mapping a bijection to \(S_n\), and how do the ascents \(\pi_j < \pi_{j+1}\) of \(\pi\) determine the values of \(a\)?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Under this bijection, the value at the \(m\)-th position of the permutation (\(1 \le m \le n\)) equals \(k\) if and only if the prefix \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m)\) of length \(m\) has exactly \(k - 1\) ascents. Because the relative order of \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m)\) is uniformly distributed over \(S_m\), the number of permutations in \(S_n\) where the \(m\)-th element produces value \(k\) is \(\frac{n!}{m!} \left\langle \begin{matrix} m \\ k - 1 \end{matrix} \right\rangle\). Thus \[ \text{Ans}_k = n! \sum_{m=1}^n \frac{1}{m!} \left\langle \begin{matrix} m \\ k - 1 \end{matrix} \right\rangle. \] Now recall the bivariate EGF of Eulerian numbers: \(\sum_{m \ge 1} \sum_{j} \left\langle \begin{matrix} m \\ j \end{matrix} \right\rangle u^j \frac{x^m}{m!} = \frac{e^{(u-1)x} - 1}{u - e^{(u-1)x}}\). Substitute \(w = (u - 1)x\)!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Setting \(v = u - 1\) and \(w = vx\) with \(\Phi(w) := \frac{e^w - 1}{w}\) reduces the problem to computing \(c_j := \sum_{m=j+1}^n [w^j] \Phi(w)^{m-j}\) for \(0 \le j \le n - 1\), followed by a single polynomial Taylor shift \(P(u - 1) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} c_j(u - 1)^j\). Express \([w^j]\Phi(w)^{m-j}\) as the formal residue \(\operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{(e^w - 1)^{m-j}}{w^{m+1}}\,dw\), apply the change of variables \(z = e^w - 1 \iff w = \ln(1 + z)\), and resolve the simple pole at \(z = 0\) (\(w = 0\)) when summing the geometric series over \(m\)!

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

Step 1: Bijection Between Good Sequences and Permutations \(\pi \in S_n\)

Let \(a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)\) be a sequence of positive integers. Construct a permutation \(\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_n) \in S_n\) by writing down the 1-based positions \(\{i : a_i = 1\}\) in decreasing order, followed by \(\{i : a_i = 2\}\) in decreasing order, and so on.

  • Within each block of equal values \(a_i = c\), the indices decrease (\(\pi_r > \pi_{r+1}\), a descent).
  • Between the end of block \(c - 1\) (which is the first occurrence of \(c - 1\) in \(a\)) and the start of block \(c\) (which is the last occurrence of \(c\) in \(a\)), the “good sequence” condition says that the first occurrence of \(c - 1\) precedes the last occurrence of \(c\), which means \(\pi_r < \pi_{r+1}\) (an ascent) at every boundary between blocks!
  • Conversely, given any permutation \(\pi \in S_n\), we must start a new value \(c \leftarrow c + 1\) at every ascent \(\pi_r < \pi_{r+1}\) (and cannot split at a descent because elements within a block must decrease). Thus, the map \(a \mapsto \pi\) is a bijection between good sequences of length \(n\) and permutations \(\pi \in S_n\), where the value \(a_{\pi_m}\) assigned to the \(m\)-th element of \(\pi\) equals \(1 + (\text{number of ascents in the prefix } \pi_1, \dots, \pi_m)\)!

For a fixed prefix length \(m \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), there are \(\binom{n}{m}\) ways to choose the set of values in \(\{\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m\}\), \((n - m)!\) ways to arrange the suffix \((\pi_{m+1}, \dots, \pi_n)\), and \(\left\langle \begin{matrix} m \\ k - 1 \end{matrix} \right\rangle\) ways to arrange \((\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m)\) with \(k - 1\) ascents. Since \(\binom{n}{m}(n - m)! = \frac{n!}{m!}\), summing over \(m = 1, \dots, n\) yields: \[ \text{Ans}_k = n! \sum_{m=1}^n \frac{1}{m!} \left\langle \begin{matrix} m \\ k - 1 \end{matrix} \right\rangle \quad (1 \le k \le n). \]

Step 2: Bivariate Eulerian EGF and Reduction to Powers of \(\Phi(w) = \frac{e^w - 1}{w}\)

Recall the exponential generating function of the Eulerian polynomials \(A_m(u) = \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \left\langle \begin{matrix} m \\ j \end{matrix} \right\rangle u^j\): \[ 1 + \sum_{m=1}^\infty A_m(u) \frac{x^m}{m!} = \frac{u - 1}{u - e^{(u - 1)x}} = \frac{1}{1 - x \cdot \frac{e^{(u-1)x} - 1}{(u - 1)x}}. \] Set \(v := u - 1\), and define the unit formal power series \[ \Phi(w) := \frac{e^w - 1}{w} = 1 + \frac{w}{2!} + \frac{w^2}{3!} + \dots \in \mathbb{F}_p[[w]], \quad \Phi(0) = 1. \] Then the bivariate EGF in \((x, v)\) is simply \[ \sum_{m=0}^\infty A_m(1 + v) \frac{x^m}{m!} = \frac{1}{1 - x \Phi(vx)} = \sum_{r=0}^\infty x^r \Phi(vx)^r. \] Extract \([x^m v^j]\) from both sides (for \(m \ge 1\) and \(0 \le j \le m - 1\)): on the right-hand side, \(x^r \Phi(vx)^r\) contributes to \(x^m v^j\) only when \(r + j = m \iff r = m - j\), giving \[ [v^j] \frac{A_m(1 + v)}{m!} = [w^j] \Phi(w)^{m - j}. \] Therefore, if we define the polynomial \(P(v) := \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} c_j v^j = \sum_{m=1}^n \frac{A_m(1 + v)}{m!}\), then its coefficients \(c_j\) are given by \[ c_j = \sum_{m = j + 1}^n [w^j] \Phi(w)^{m - j} = \sum_{r = 1}^{n - j} [w^j] \Phi(w)^r \quad (0 \le j \le n - 1), \] and once we know \(c_0, c_1, \dots, c_{n-1}\), expanding \(P(u - 1) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} c_j (u - 1)^j\) via a single polynomial Taylor shift gives \(\frac{\text{Ans}_{k}}{n!} = [u^{k-1}] P(u - 1)\) in \(O(n \log n)\) time.

Step 3: Formal Residue Change of Variables & Explicit L’Hôpital Double-Pole Cancellation

To evaluate \(c_j = \sum_{m = j + 1}^n [w^j] \Phi(w)^{m - j}\) for all \(j = 0, \dots, n - 1\) simultaneously, rewrite the \(m\)-th term as a formal residue by factoring \(\Phi(w)^{m - j} = w^{-(m - j)} \bigl(w \Phi(w)\bigr)^{m - j}\): \[ [w^j] \Phi(w)^{m - j} = \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{\Phi(w)^{m - j}}{w^{j + 1}}\,dw = \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{\bigl(w \Phi(w)\bigr)^{m - j}}{w^{m + 1}}\,dw. \] Notice that the base \(z := w \Phi(w) = e^w - 1 \in \mathcal{G}_1(\mathbb{F}_p)\) has the closed-form compositional inverse \(w = g(z) = \ln(1 + z)\) (requiring no Newton iteration to invert).

  1. Change of Variables \(z = e^w - 1 \iff w = \ln(1 + z)\): Applying Theorem 11.1 with \(w = \ln(1 + z)\) and \(dw = \frac{dz}{1 + z}\) gives \[ \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{(e^w - 1)^{m - j}}{w^{m + 1}}\,dw = \operatorname{Res}_{z=0} \frac{z^{m - j}}{\bigl(\ln(1 + z)\bigr)^{m + 1} (1 + z)}\,dz = [z^j] \left( \frac{1}{1 + z} \left(\frac{z}{\ln(1 + z)}\right)^{m + 1} \right). \] The extraction index \([z^j]\) is now completely decoupled from \(m\), while \(m\) appears only as the exponent \(m + 1\) of the unit series \(Q(z) := \frac{z}{\ln(1 + z)}\).

  2. Summing the Geometric Series over \(m = j + 1, \dots, n\): Summing \(Q(z)^{m + 1}\) over \(m = j + 1, \dots, n\) via the finite geometric series formula yields \[ \sum_{m = j + 1}^n Q(z)^{m + 1} = \frac{Q(z)^{n + 2} - Q(z)^{j + 2}}{Q(z) - 1}. \] Therefore, for every \(0 \le j \le n - 1\): \[ c_j = [z^j] \left( \frac{Q(z)^{n + 2}}{(1 + z)\bigl(Q(z) - 1\bigr)} \right) - [z^j] \left( \frac{Q(z)^{j + 2}}{(1 + z)\bigl(Q(z) - 1\bigr)} \right), \quad \text{where } Q(z) = \frac{z}{\ln(1 + z)}. \]

  3. Resolving the Pole at \(z = 0\) (\(Q(0) = 1\)) via L’Hôpital Cancellation & Inverse Substitution:

    • First Term \(\text{Term}_1(j)\): Since \(\ln(1 + z) = z - \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{3} - \dots\), we have \[ Q(z) = \frac{z}{\ln(1 + z)} = 1 + \frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{12}z^2 + \dots, \quad \text{so } Q(z) - 1 = \frac{1}{2}z - \frac{1}{12}z^2 + \dots \text{ has valuation } \operatorname{val}(Q - 1) = 1. \] Define the unit series \(R(z) := \frac{Q(z) - 1}{z} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{12}z + \dots \in \mathbb{F}_p[[z]]^\times\). Dividing by \(Q(z) - 1 = z R(z)\) shifts the coefficient extraction from \([z^j]\) to \([z^{j + 1}]\): \[ \text{Term}_1(j) := [z^{j + 1}] \left( \frac{Q(z)^{n + 2}}{(1 + z) R(z)} \right), \] which computes \(\text{Term}_1(j)\) for all \(j = 0, \dots, n - 1\) simultaneously in one pow and inv.
    • Second Term \(\text{Term}_2(j)\): Write \(\text{Term}_2(j) = [z^j]\left(\frac{Q(z)^{j+2}}{(1+z)(Q(z)-1)}\right)\) as a formal residue in \(z\) and use \(\frac{Q(z)}{z} = \frac{1}{\ln(1 + z)}\) to transform back to \(w = \ln(1 + z)\) (\(z = e^w - 1\), \(\frac{dz}{1 + z} = dw\), \(Q(z) = \frac{e^w - 1}{w}\)): \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Term}_2(j) &= \operatorname{Res}_{z=0} \frac{Q(z)}{Q(z) - 1} \cdot \left(\frac{Q(z)}{z}\right)^{j + 1} \frac{dz}{1 + z} \\ &= \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{\frac{e^w - 1}{w}}{\frac{e^w - 1}{w} - 1} \cdot \frac{dw}{w^{j + 1}} \\ &= \operatorname{Res}_{w=0} \frac{e^w - 1}{e^w - 1 - w} \cdot \frac{dw}{w^{j + 1}} = [w^j] \left(\frac{e^w - 1}{e^w - 1 - w}\right). \end{aligned} \] Because \(e^w - 1 - w = \frac{w^2}{2} + \frac{w^3}{6} + \dots\) has valuation \(2\) while \(e^w - 1 = w + \frac{w^2}{2} + \dots\) has valuation \(1\), their ratio has a simple pole at \(w = 0\). Factoring out \(w\) and \(w^2\) shifts the extraction to \([w^{j + 1}]\) of a unit series: \[ \text{Term}_2(j) = [w^{j + 1}] \left( \frac{(e^w - 1)/w}{\bigl(e^w - 1 - w\bigr)/w^2} \right) \quad (0 \le j \le n - 1). \] (Sanity check at \(n = 1, j = 0\): \(\frac{Q(z)^3}{(1+z)R(z)} = 2 + \frac{4}{3}z + O(z^2)\) gives \(\text{Term}_1(0) = \frac{4}{3}\), and \(\frac{(e^w-1)/w}{(e^w-1-w)/w^2} = 2 + \frac{1}{3}w + O(w^2)\) gives \(\text{Term}_2(0) = \frac{1}{3}\), so \(c_0 = \frac{4}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = 1\), matching \(\text{Ans}_1 = 1! \cdot c_0 = 1\).)
    • Combined Formula: Both \(\text{Term}_1(j)\) and \(\text{Term}_2(j)\) are single-coefficient lookups from two fixed power series of degree \(n + 1\): \[ c_j = [z^{j + 1}] \left( \frac{Q(z)^{n + 2}}{(1 + z) R(z)} \right) - [w^{j + 1}] \left( \frac{(e^w - 1)/w}{(e^w - 1 - w)/w^2} \right). \]

  4. Final Complexity Contract:

    • Compute \(Q(z) = \frac{z}{\ln(1 + z)} \bmod z^{n+2}\), \(R(z) = \frac{Q(z) - 1}{z} \bmod z^{n+1}\), and \(\frac{Q(z)^{n+2}}{(1+z)R(z)} \bmod z^{n+1}\) via polynomial ln, inv, and pow in \(O(n \log n)\) time.
    • Compute \(\frac{(e^w - 1)/w}{(e^w - 1 - w)/w^2} \bmod w^{n+1}\) via one polynomial inv in \(O(n \log n)\) time.
    • Subtract to obtain \(c_0, \dots, c_{n-1}\), perform one Taylor shift \(P(u - 1) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} c_j (u - 1)^j\) in \(O(n \log n)\) time, and multiply by \(n!\) to output \(\text{Ans}_1, \dots, \text{Ans}_n\). Total time: \(O(n \log n)\), space: \(O(n)\).

Mastery 2 (#203) — 2021 CoE-II E: 数树 (Counting Trees — 4-Subtask Grand Synthesis Across Characteristic \(0\) and Huge-\(N\) Characteristic \(p\))

  • Source: Luogu P7592 — 2021 CoE-II E: 数树 (Curriculum Pool #203, 3B#22)
  • Prerequisites: Chapter 3 (inv, ln, exp, pow), Chapter 10 (Newton’s method), §11.2 (LIF Form 1 & Form 2), §11.4 (Characteristic-\(p\) LIF & Base-\(p\) Frobenius Decimation)

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

We consider a family of plane rooted tree counting problems controlled by a branching weight polynomial \(\Phi(u) = \sum_{i=0}^d \phi_i u^i\) with \(\phi_0 \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\) (where an internal node with \(i\) children has weight \(\phi_i\)) and a root weight polynomial \(H(u) = \sum_{j=1}^{d_H} h_j u^j\), so that the generating function \(F(x)\) of trees satisfies \(F(x) = x \Phi(F(x))\) and the target answer for \(N\) vertices is \([x^N] H(F(x)) \bmod p\).

Solve all four progressive regimes (Subtasks 1–4) of the problem:

  • Subtask 1 (Small \(N\), Standard Prime): \(N \le 5000\), \(d \le 5000\), \(p = 998244353\).
  • Subtask 2 (LargeSingle-\(N\), Standard NTT Prime): \(N \le 2 \times 10^5\), \(d \le 2 \times 10^5\), \(p = 998244353\), querying a single \(N\).
  • Subtask 3 (All-\(n\) Simultaneous Query, Standard NTT Prime): Output \([x^n] H(F(x)) \bmod 998244353\) for all \(n = 1, 2, \dots, N\) up to \(N \le 10^5\) when \(\Phi(u)\) is a sparse low-degree polynomial (\(d \le 5\)).
  • Subtask 4 (Huge \(N \le 10^{18}\) Modulo a Small Prime \(p\) Where \(p \mid N\) Is Possible!): \(N \le 10^{18}\), \(d, d_H \le 20\), and \(p \le 10^4\) is a small prime (so \(N \ge p\) and \(p \mid N\) can occur!).

Required Complexity:

  • Subtasks 1–2: \(O(N \log N)\) time.
  • Subtask 3: \(O(N \log N)\) time.
  • Subtask 4: \(O(d p \log(dp) \log_p N)\) time and \(O(dp)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\)): For \(\Phi(u) = 1 + u + u^2\) (Motzkin plane trees where each vertex has \(0, 1,\) or \(2\) children) and \(H(u) = u\), iterating \(F(x) = x(1 + F(x) + F(x)^2)\) gives \([x^1]F(x) = 1\), \([x^2]F(x) = 1\), \([x^3]F(x) = 2\), and \([x^4]F(x) = 4\). Modulo \(p = 3\) at \(N = 3\) (\(p \mid N\)), Form 2 gives \([u^3]\bigl(u(1 - u^2)(1 + u + u^2)^2\bigr) = [u^2]\bigl((1 - u^2)(1 + 2u + 3u^2 + 2u^3 + u^4)\bigr) = 3 - 1 = 2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3\).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Each subtask probes a different algorithmic regime of the implicit equation \(F(x) = x \Phi(F(x))\):

  • Subtasks 1 & 2 ask for a single coefficient \([x^N] H(F(x))\) when \(N < p\).
  • Subtask 3 asks for all coefficients \(n = 1, \dots, N\) when \(\Phi(u)\) has small degree \(d \le 5\).
  • Subtask 4 asks for a single coefficient at huge \(N \le 10^{18}\) modulo a small prime \(p \le 10^4\) where \(p \mid N\) may hold!
Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad
  • For Subtasks 1 & 2, use Form 1 (\([x^N]H(F(x)) = \frac{1}{N}[u^{N-1}]H'(u)\Phi(u)^N\)) or Form 2 (\([u^N]H(u)(\Phi(u) - u\Phi'(u))\Phi(u)^{N-1}\)) via one polynomial pow in \(O(N \log N)\) time.
  • For Subtask 3, since \(d \le 5\) is small and we need all \(n \le N\), apply Newton’s method (Chapter 10) to solve \(G(F(x)) = F(x) - x\Phi(F(x)) = 0 \bmod x^{N+1}\), then substitute \(F(x)\) into \(H(u)\) in \(O(d N \log N)\) time!
  • For Subtask 4, classical Form 1 fails whenever \(p \mid N\) (Failure Mode #5). Use Form 2 Derivative-Free LIF paired with Base-\(p\) Frobenius Digit Decimation (§11.4)!
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

In Subtask 4, define \(A_0(u) := H(u)\bigl(\Phi(u) - u\Phi'(u)\bigr) \bmod p\), which has degree \(\le d_H + d\). Then \([x^N] H(F(x)) = [u^N] \bigl(A_0(u) \Phi(u)^{N-1}\bigr) \bmod p\). At each base-\(p\) step, extract \((k_0, m_0) = (k \bmod p, m \bmod p)\), multiply \(A(u)\) by \(\Phi(u)^{m_0}\) via binary exponentiation in \(\mathbb{F}_p[u]\), and decimate every \(p\)-th coefficient starting at \(k_0\).

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Subtasks 1 & 2 (\(N \le 2 \times 10^5\), \(p = 998244353\), Single-\(N\) Extraction)

Since \(1 \le N < p = 998244353\) and \(\phi_0 = \Phi(0) \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\), both Form 1 and Form 2 of Lagrange–Bürmann Inversion apply directly: \[ [x^N] H(F(x)) = \frac{1}{N} [u^{N-1}] \Bigl( H'(u) \Phi(u)^N \Bigr) = [u^N] \Bigl( H(u)\bigl(\Phi(u) - u\Phi'(u)\bigr) \Phi(u)^{N-1} \Bigr). \] Using Form 1:

  1. Truncate \(\Phi(u)\) and \(H'(u)\) modulo \(u^N\).
  2. Compute \(\Phi(u)^N \bmod u^N\) via \(\phi_0^N \exp\bigl(N \ln(\phi_0^{-1} \Phi(u))\bigr) \bmod u^N\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time.
  3. Compute the single dot product \([u^{N-1}] \bigl(H'(u) \Phi(u)^N\bigr)\) in \(O(N)\) time and multiply by \(N^{-1} \bmod p\).

2. Subtask 3 (All \(n = 1, \dots, N\) up to \(N \le 10^5\), Small Degree \(d \le 5\))

When we need \([x^n] H(F(x))\) for all \(n = 1, \dots, N\), running LIF separately for each \(n\) would take \(O(N^2 \log N)\) time. Instead, because \(d = \deg \Phi \le 5\) and \(d_H = \deg H \le 5\) are small, we solve for the power series \(F(x) \bmod x^{N+1}\) directly using Newton’s Method (Chapter 10):

  1. Define \(\mathcal{P}(F) := F(x) - x \Phi(F(x)) = 0\). Given \(F_k(x) \equiv F(x) \bmod x^{2^k}\), the Newton iteration is \[ F_{k+1}(x) \equiv F_k(x) - \frac{F_k(x) - x \Phi(F_k(x))}{1 - x \Phi'(F_k(x))} \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}. \] Since \(\deg \Phi = d \le 5\), evaluating \(\Phi(F_k(x))\) and \(\Phi'(F_k(x))\) via Horner’s rule takes \(d\) polynomial multiplications, so finding \(F(x) \bmod x^{N+1}\) takes \(T(N) = T(N/2) + O(d N \log N) = O(d N \log N)\) time.
  2. Finally, evaluate \(H(F(x)) = \sum_{j=1}^{d_H} h_j F(x)^j \bmod x^{N+1}\) via Horner’s rule in \(O(d_H N \log N)\) time and read off all coefficients \([x^1], \dots, [x^N]\).

3. Subtask 4 (Huge \(N \le 10^{18}\), Small Prime \(p \le 10^4\), Supporting \(p \mid N\))

When \(N \le 10^{18}\) and \(p \le 10^4\):

  • We cannot use Form 1 because \(p \mid N\) is possible (\(N \equiv 0 \pmod p\) has no inverse in \(\mathbb{F}_p\); see Failure Mode #5).
  • We cannot use \(\ln / \exp\) or ODE recurrences for \(\Phi(u)^{N-1}\) because \(p \le N\) causes division by zero at every multiple of \(p\).

Instead, we apply Form 2 (另类拉格朗日反演), which holds over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) for every \(N \ge 1\) without dividing by \(N\): \[ [x^N] H(F(x)) = [u^N] \Bigl( A_0(u) \Phi(u)^{N - 1} \Bigr) \pmod p, \] where \[ A_0(u) := H(u) \bigl(\Phi(u) - u \Phi'(u)\bigr) \in \mathbb{F}_p[u] \] is a polynomial of degree at most \(d_H + d \le 40\). Now we apply Base-\(p\) Frobenius Decimation (§11.4) to extract \([u^k] \bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^m\bigr) \bmod p\) starting from \(A(u) = A_0(u)\), \(k = N\), and \(m = N - 1\):

  1. While \(k > 0\) or \(m > 0\):
    • Let \(k_0 = k \bmod p\) and \(m_0 = m \bmod p\).
    • Since \(0 \le m_0 < p\), compute \(\Phi(u)^{m_0} \in \mathbb{F}_p[u]\) (of degree \(\le d m_0 < dp\)) by binary exponentiation of polynomials in \(\mathbb{F}_p[u]\), and multiply by \(A(u)\) to get \(B(u) = A(u) \Phi(u)^{m_0}\).
    • By the Frobenius identity \(\Phi(u)^p \equiv \Phi(u^p) \pmod p\), we have \[ [u^k] \bigl(A(u) \Phi(u)^m\bigr) \equiv [u^{p \lfloor k/p \rfloor + k_0}] \Bigl( B(u) \cdot \Phi(u^p)^{\lfloor m/p \rfloor} \Bigr) = [v^{\lfloor k/p \rfloor}] \Bigl( A_{\text{next}}(v) \Phi(v)^{\lfloor m/p \rfloor} \Bigr) \pmod p, \] where \(A_{\text{next}}(v) = \sum_{j \ge 0} \bigl([u^{p j + k_0}] B(u)\bigr) v^j\).
    • Replace \(A \leftarrow A_{\text{next}}\), \(k \leftarrow \lfloor k / p \rfloor\), and \(m \leftarrow \lfloor m / p \rfloor\).
  2. When the loop terminates at \(k = m = 0\), output \([v^0] A(v) \bmod p\).

By Proposition 11.2, the degree of \(A(v)\) never exceeds \(\max(d_H + d, d) = d_H + d \le 40\) at the start of any iteration. Thus each iteration multiplies polynomials of degree at most \(d p \le 2 \times 10^5\), running in \(O\bigl(d p \log(dp) \log_p N\bigr)\) time and \(O(dp)\) space!
