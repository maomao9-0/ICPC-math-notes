In Chapter 11, univariate Lagrange Inversion allowed us to count rooted trees of a single vertex type governed by \(u(x) = x \Phi(u(x))\). What happens when our combinatorial world becomes genuinely multidimensional?

  • Suppose a rooted tree has \(d\) distinct vertex colors, where the children of a color-\(i\) vertex are governed by a multivariate branching law \(\Phi_i(u_1, \dots, u_d)\). If we naively apply univariate Lagrange Inversion one variable at a time, we create spurious directed cycles of dependencies among the \(d\) colors. How do we algebraically purge every directed cycle at once?
  • Suppose a 2D lattice walk is confined to the first quadrant \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2 = \{(i, j) : i \ge 0, j \ge 0\}\). Now its bivariate functional equation contains two unknown boundary series: \(F(x; t)\) along the horizontal axis \(j = 0\) and \(G(y; t)\) along the vertical axis \(i = 0\). Substituting a single root \(Y_0(x; t)\) of the kernel \(K(x, y; t) = 0\) leaves \(F(x; t)\) coupled with \(G(Y_0(x; t); t)\)—one equation in two unknowns! How do we decouple them without solving an infinite linear system?

This chapter develops the four canonical multivariate engines that resolve these exact obstructions: Multivariate Good–Lagrange Inversion (§17.1), MacMahon’s Master Theorem (§17.2), Newton–Girard Symmetric Function Duality (§17.3), and the Obstinate Kernel Method & Bivariate PDE Companion Matrices (§17.4).

17.1 Multivariate Good–Lagrange Inversion (Forms 1 & 2 and Bipartite Collapse)

Concrete Combinatorial Model: Multi-Type Rooted Arborescences

Before writing any determinants or formal residues, let us name the concrete combinatorial objects being counted. Fix an integer \(d \ge 1\) representing \(d\) distinct vertex colors (or types) \(\{1, 2, \dots, d\}\). We want to count multi-type rooted labeled (or ordered) trees containing exactly \(n_1\) vertices of color \(1\), \(n_2\) vertices of color \(2\), \(\dots\), and \(n_d\) vertices of color \(d\).

In such a tree, each vertex of color \(i\) chooses its out-neighborhood (its multiset or tuple of children across all \(d\) colors) according to a local weight series

\[\Phi_i(u_1, \dots, u_d) \in K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]], \qquad \text{with } [u_1^0 \cdots u_d^0]\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) \in K^\times.\]

Let \(u_i = u_i(x_1, \dots, x_d) \in K[[x_1, \dots, x_d]]\) be the generating series for multi-type trees rooted at a vertex of color \(i\), where \(x_j\) marks the number of vertices of color \(j\). Because a tree rooted at color \(i\) consists of one root vertex of color \(i\) (contributing \(x_i\)) together with an allowed configuration of subtrees rooted at its children (contributing \(\Phi_i(u_1, \dots, u_d)\)), the vector of series \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, \dots, u_d)\) satisfies the coupled implicit system:

\[u_i = x_i \Phi_i(u_1, \dots, u_d), \qquad i = 1, 2, \dots, d.\]

Given an arbitrary probe series \(H(u_1, \dots, u_d) \in K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\) representing a forest of roots (for instance, \(H(\mathbf{u}) = u_1^{a_1} \cdots u_d^{a_d}\) for an ordered forest with \(a_i\) roots of color \(i\)), our goal is to answer:

The Central Question: What is the exact coefficient \([x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] H(u_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, u_d(\mathbf{x}))\) in terms of the known branching series \(\Phi_1(\mathbf{u}), \dots, \Phi_d(\mathbf{u})\)?

Why Naive Product Extraction Fails: The Cycle Obstruction

If you look at the univariate Lagrange formula \([x^n]H(u) = \frac{1}{n}[u^{n-1}]H'(u)\Phi(u)^n\) (or its division-free residue form \([x^n]H(u) = [u^n]H(u)\big(1 - \frac{u \Phi'(u)}{\Phi(u)}\big)\Phi(u)^n\)), it is tempting to guess that in \(d\) variables the answer is simply \([u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}] H(\mathbf{u}) \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i}\), or perhaps multiplied by the product of univariate factors \(\prod_{i=1}^d \big(1 - \frac{u_i \partial_i \Phi_i}{\Phi_i}\big)\).

Both guesses are false. Let us see why combinatorially:

  • The naive product \([u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}] \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i}\) lets each of the \(n_i\) vertices of color \(i\) independently choose its children across all colors so that the total in-degree of color \(j\) equals \(n_j\).
  • In a directed graph where every vertex has out-degree governed by \(\Phi_i\) and the total number of arcs entering color \(j\) matches the number of vertices of color \(j\), the resulting functional digraph is a disjoint union of rooted trees (arborescences rooted at the forest specified by \(H(\mathbf{u})\)) plus arbitrary directed cycles!
  • Multiplying by \(\prod_{i=1}^d \big(1 - \frac{u_i \partial_i \Phi_i}{\Phi_i}\big)\) only cancels monochromatic directed cycles (cycles that stay entirely within a single color \(i\)). It fails to cancel heterochromatic directed cycles such as a vertex of color \(1\) pointing to a vertex of color \(2\) which points back to color \(1\)!

To cancel all directed cycles—both monochromatic and multi-color—we need a determinant.

NoteDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Good–Lagrange Inversion
  • Diagnostic Signal: You have \(d \ge 2\) coupled generating functions \(u_1, \dots, u_d\) satisfying \(u_i = x_i \Phi_i(u_1, \dots, u_d)\) where each variable \(u_i\) carries its own distinct formal marker \(x_i\) (valuation \(\operatorname{val}_{\mathbf{x}}(u_i) = 1\) with \([x_i^1]\mathbf{u}\) supported on \(u_i\)), and you want to extract a specific multi-index \([x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] H(u_1, \dots, u_d)\).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    1. Write the target coefficient as an iterated \(d\)-dimensional formal Cauchy residue in \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_d)\): \[[x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] H(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})) = \operatorname{Res}_{\mathbf{x}} \left[ H(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})) \prod_{i=1}^d x_i^{-n_i - 1} \right].\]
    2. Since \(u_i = x_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\) with \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) \in K^\times\), the inverse map \(\mathbf{u} \mapsto \mathbf{x}(\mathbf{u})\) is given explicitly by \(x_i(\mathbf{u}) = u_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\). Notice that \(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}\) and the linear part of \(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{u})\) is diagonal and invertible: \(\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j}(\mathbf{0}) = \delta_{i,j} \Phi_i(\mathbf{0})^{-1} \in K^\times\).
    3. Apply the \(d\)-dimensional formal residue change-of-variables theorem (\(\operatorname{Res}_{\mathbf{x}} F(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} = \operatorname{Res}_{\mathbf{u}} F(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{u})) \det\!\left(\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j}\right) d\mathbf{u}\)). Differentiate \(x_i = u_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\) with respect to \(u_j\) using the quotient rule: \[\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j} = \frac{\delta_{i,j}}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})} - \frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^2}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j} = \frac{1}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\left(\delta_{i,j} - \frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j}\right).\]
    4. Factor \(\prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\) out of the rows of the \(d \times d\) Jacobian determinant, and note that conjugating a matrix \(I - M\) by \(\operatorname{diag}(u_1, \dots, u_d)\) preserves its determinant (\(\det(I - \tilde{J}) = \det(I - J)\) where \(J_{i,j} = \frac{u_j}{\Phi_i}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j}\)). Combining \(\prod_{i=1}^d x_i^{-n_i-1} = \prod_{i=1}^d u_i^{-n_i-1} \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i+1}\) with \(\prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\) cancels the extra \(+1\) in the exponent of \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\)!

Theorem 17.1: Multivariate Good–Lagrange Inversion (I. J. Good, 1960)

Let \(K\) be any commutative ring with \(1\). Let \(\Phi_1(\mathbf{u}), \dots, \Phi_d(\mathbf{u}) \in K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\) be formal power series whose constant terms are units in \(K\):

\[[u_1^0 \cdots u_d^0]\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) \in K^\times \qquad \text{for all } i \in \{1, \dots, d\}.\]

Then the coupled functional system

\[u_i = x_i \Phi_i(u_1, \dots, u_d), \qquad i = 1, \dots, d,\]

possesses a unique solution \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}) = (u_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, u_d(\mathbf{x})) \in K[[x_1, \dots, x_d]]^d\) with \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}\). Define the \(d \times d\) Good–Lagrange logarithmic Jacobian matrix \(J(\mathbf{u})\) with entries in \(K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\) by

\[J_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u_j}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})} \frac{\partial \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}{\partial u_j}, \qquad 1 \le i, j \le d.\]

For any formal power series \(H(\mathbf{u}) \in K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\) (or formal Laurent series when \(n_i\) are large enough) and any tuple of integers \(\mathbf{n} = (n_1, \dots, n_d) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^d\):

  1. Form 1 (Direct / Arborescence Extraction Form — Division-Free in \(n_i\)): \[[x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] H(u_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, u_d(\mathbf{x})) = [u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}] \left( H(\mathbf{u}) \det\!\big(I_d - J(\mathbf{u})\big) \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i} \right).\]
  2. Form 2 (Inverse-Jacobian / Cycle-Sum Form): \[[x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] \frac{H(u_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, u_d(\mathbf{x}))}{\det\!\big(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\big)} = [u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}] \left( H(\mathbf{u}) \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i} \right).\]

Single-Step Verbalized Proof:

  1. Step 1 (Existence, uniqueness, and valuation): Because \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) \in K^\times\), each equation \(u_i = x_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\) expresses \(u_i\) as \(x_i\) times a series of total degree \(\ge 0\) in \(\mathbf{u}\). Therefore, for every multi-index \(\mathbf{n}\), the coefficient \([x_1^{n_1}\cdots x_d^{n_d}]u_i\) depends only on coefficients of \(\mathbf{u}\) of strictly smaller total \(\mathbf{x}\)-degree \(\sum_j n_j - 1\), proving existence and uniqueness over any commutative ring \(K\) without dividing by integers.
  2. Step 2 (Rewriting coefficient extraction as a multivariate formal residue): By definition of the iterated formal residue operator \(\operatorname{Res}_{\mathbf{x}} = \operatorname{Res}_{x_1} \cdots \operatorname{Res}_{x_d}\) on \(K((x_1))\cdots((x_d))\), extracting \([x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}]\) from \(H(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\) is identical to extracting \([x_1^{-1} \cdots x_d^{-1}]\) from \(H(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})) \prod_{i=1}^d x_i^{-n_i-1}\): \[[x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] H(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})) = \operatorname{Res}_{\mathbf{x}} \left( H(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})) \prod_{i=1}^d x_i^{-n_i - 1} \right).\]
  3. Step 3 (Jacobian of the substitution \(x_i = u_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\)): Since \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) \in K^\times\), \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\) is a multiplicative unit in \(K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\). Solving the defining relation for \(x_i\) gives \(x_i(\mathbf{u}) = u_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\). Computing the \((i, j)\) partial derivative gives \[\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j} = \frac{1}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})} \left( \delta_{i,j} - \frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})} \frac{\partial \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}{\partial u_j} \right).\]
  4. Step 4 (Evaluating the Jacobian determinant and conjugating by \(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{u})\)): Let \(\tilde{J}_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j}\). Factoring \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\) from the \(i\)-th row of the matrix \(\big(\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j}\big)_{i,j=1}^d\) gives \[\det\!\left(\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j}\right) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\right) \det\!\big(I_d - \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u})\big).\] Notice that for every permutation \(\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_d\) in the Leibniz expansion of \(\det(I_d - \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}))\), each non-fixed index \(i \in \operatorname{supp}(\sigma)\) appears once as a row index (contributing a factor \(u_i\) in the numerator of \(\tilde{J}_{i, \sigma(i)}\)) and once as a column index \(\sigma(k) = i\). Replacing the row factor \(u_i\) by the column factor \(u_j\) replaces \(\tilde{J}_{i,j}\) by \(J_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u_j}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j}\) without changing the product along any cycle of \(\sigma\). Thus \(\det(I_d - \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u})) = \det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}))\).
  5. Step 5 (Applying the multivariate formal residue change-of-variables): Substituting \(x_i^{-n_i-1} = u_i^{-n_i-1} \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i+1}\) and \(d\mathbf{x} = \det\!\big(\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial u_j}\big) d\mathbf{u}\) into the formal residue yields \[\operatorname{Res}_{\mathbf{u}} \left( H(\mathbf{u}) \left(\prod_{i=1}^d u_i^{-n_i-1} \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i+1}\right) \left(\prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\right) \det\!\big(I_d - J(\mathbf{u})\big) \right) = [u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}] \left( H(\mathbf{u}) \det\!\big(I_d - J(\mathbf{u})\big) \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i} \right),\] which establishes Form 1. Finally, since \(\det(I_d - J(\mathbf{0})) = \det(I_d) = 1 \in K^\times\), replacing \(H(\mathbf{u})\) by \(H(\mathbf{u}) / \det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}))\) in Form 1 cancels the determinant factor on the right-hand side and yields Form 2. \(\blacksquare\)
ImportantAlgebraic Hypothesis & Characteristic-\(p\) Safety of Form 1

Look closely at Form 1 of Theorem 17.1:

\[[x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_d^{n_d}] H(\mathbf{u}) = [u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}] H(\mathbf{u}) \det\!\big(I_d - J(\mathbf{u})\big) \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i}.\]

  1. Zero Division by \(n_i\): Unlike the univariate derivative formula \(\frac{1}{n}[u^{n-1}]H'(u)\Phi(u)^n\), Form 1 never divides by \(n_1, \dots, n_d\)! It only divides by \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\), which is invertible in \(K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\) whenever \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) \in K^\times\).
  2. Valid in Arbitrary Characteristic \(p > 0\): Consequently, Form 1 remains 100% valid over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) even when \(p \mid n_i\), and even when one or more \(n_i = 0\)!

Combinatorial Meaning (Gessel 1987): Why \(\det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}))\) Cancels Directed Cycles

Why does the Leibniz expansion of \(\det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}))\) turn arbitrary functional digraphs into rooted trees? Following Ira Gessel’s 1987 combinatorial proof:

  1. Expand the product \(H(\mathbf{u}) \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i}\). Extracting \([u_1^{n_1} \cdots u_d^{n_d}]\) counts all weighted multi-type functional digraphs on vertex sets \(V_1, \dots, V_d\) with \(|V_i| = n_i\), where each vertex \(v \in V_i\) chooses a bundle of outgoing directed edges to children according to \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\), plus a set of “root stubs” chosen according to \(H(\mathbf{u})\), such that the total number of incoming edges to color \(j\) equals \(n_j\).
  2. Because every non-root vertex has in-degree \(1\) (after pairing incoming stubs with vertices of color \(j\)), such a digraph decomposes into a rooted forest (arborescences rooted at the \(H(\mathbf{u})\) stubs) together with a collection of vertex-disjoint directed cycles.
  3. Now look at the operator \(J_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u_j}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\frac{\partial \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}{\partial u_j}\). In the factor \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i}\), replacing one copy of \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\) by \(u_j \frac{\partial \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}{\partial u_j}\) distinguishes (marks) one outgoing edge from a vertex of color \(i\) to a child of color \(j\).
  4. In the determinant expansion \(\det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u})) = \sum_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_d} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i: \sigma(i)=i} 1 \cdot \prod_{i: \sigma(i)\neq i} (-J_{i, \sigma(i)}(\mathbf{u}))\) (together with diagonal terms from \(I_d - J\)), a term of weight \((-1)^c\) picks a collection of \(c\) vertex-disjoint marked directed cycles across the colors!
  5. For any functional digraph containing \(C \ge 1\) directed cycles, any subset of \(c \subseteq \{1, \dots, C\}\) cycles can be marked, contributing \(\sum_{c=0}^C (-1)^c \binom{C}{c} = (1 - 1)^C = 0\). Only functional digraphs with \(C = 0\) directed cycles—that is, pure rooted arborescences—survive with weight \((1-1)^0 = 1\)!
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Omitting \(\det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}))\) or Confusing Form 1 with Form 2 in Multivariate Good–Lagrange
  1.   2. If a branching series has \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) = 0\), you must first shift variables or factor out the minimal monomial power before applying Theorem 17.1.

Corollary 17.1: The Bipartite Rank-1 Collapse (\(d = 2\))

In competitive programming, the single most frequent two-type tree system is a bipartite (alternating) tree where vertices of color \(1\) only have children of color \(2\), and vertices of color \(2\) only have children of color \(1\):

\[u_1 = x_1 \Phi_1(u_2), \qquad u_2 = x_2 \Phi_2(u_1), \qquad \Phi_1(0), \Phi_2(0) \in K^\times.\]

Look at what happens to the \(2 \times 2\) Good–Lagrange matrix \(J(u_1, u_2)\) in this bipartite regime: because \(\frac{\partial \Phi_1}{\partial u_1} = 0\) and \(\frac{\partial \Phi_2}{\partial u_2} = 0\), the diagonal of \(J\) vanishes identically:

\[J(u_1, u_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \dfrac{u_2 \Phi_1'(u_2)}{\Phi_1(u_2)} \\[8pt] \dfrac{u_1 \Phi_2'(u_1)}{\Phi_2(u_1)} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies \det\!\big(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2)\big) = 1 - \frac{u_1 \Phi_2'(u_1)}{\Phi_2(u_1)} \cdot \frac{u_2 \Phi_1'(u_2)}{\Phi_1(u_2)}.\]

Notice the miracle: every factor in \(\det(I_2 - J) \Phi_1(u_2)^{n_1} \Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2}\) is a sum of products of a pure function of \(u_1\) and a pure function of \(u_2\)! The 2D coefficient extraction completely decouples into two 1D extractions:

TipCorollary 17.1 (Bipartite Good–Lagrange Decoupling Formula)

Let \(u_1 = x_1 \Phi_1(u_2)\) and \(u_2 = x_2 \Phi_2(u_1)\) with \(\Phi_1(0), \Phi_2(0) \in K^\times\). For any integers \(n_1, n_2 \ge 1\) and \(a, b \ge 0\):

  1. Division-Free Form (Valid in any characteristic \(p\)): \[[x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] (u_1^a u_2^b) = \Big([u_1^{n_1-a}]\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2}\Big)\Big([u_2^{n_2-b}]\Phi_1(u_2)^{n_1}\Big) - \Big([u_1^{n_1-a-1}]\Phi_2'(u_1)\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2-1}\Big)\Big([u_2^{n_2-b-1}]\Phi_1'(u_2)\Phi_1(u_2)^{n_1-1}\Big).\]
  2. Factored Closed Form (When \(n_1, n_2 \in K^\times\)): Using the identity \([u_1^{n_1-a-1}] \Phi_2'(u_1)\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2-1} = \frac{1}{n_2}[u_1^{n_1-a-1}]\frac{d}{du_1}\big(\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2}\big) = \frac{n_1 - a}{n_2}[u_1^{n_1-a}]\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2}\) and the symmetric identity for \(u_2\), the two terms combine into a single product: \[[x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] (u_1^a u_2^b) = \left(1 - \frac{(n_1 - a)(n_2 - b)}{n_1 n_2}\right) \Big([u_1^{n_1-a}]\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2}\Big)\Big([u_2^{n_2-b}]\Phi_1(u_2)^{n_1}\Big) = \frac{a n_2 + b n_1 - a b}{n_1 n_2} \Big([u_1^{n_1-a}]\Phi_2(u_1)^{n_2}\Big)\Big([u_2^{n_2-b}]\Phi_1(u_2)^{n_1}\Big).\]

Concrete \(N \le 4\) Trace: Scoins’s Theorem on Bipartite Colored Labeled Trees

Let us test Corollary 17.1 on a hand-checkable concrete instance: Scoins’s Theorem (1962) counting labeled bipartite trees on \(n_1\) vertices of color \(1\) (say, white vertices \(\{W_1, \dots, W_{n_1}\}\)) and \(n_2\) vertices of color \(2\) (black vertices \(\{B_1, \dots, B_{n_2}\}\)), rooted at a white vertex.

In the exponential generating function (EGF) world where white vertices have white labels and black vertices have black labels, an unordered set of black children has EGF \(\Phi_1(u_2) = e^{u_2}\), and an unordered set of white children has EGF \(\Phi_2(u_1) = e^{u_1}\). Thus:

\[u_1 = x_1 e^{u_2}, \qquad u_2 = x_2 e^{u_1}.\]

We want to count bipartite trees rooted at a white vertex (\(H(u_1, u_2) = u_1\), so \(a = 1, b = 0\)). Applying Corollary 17.1 with \(a = 1, b = 0\):

\[[x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] u_1 = \frac{1 \cdot n_2 + 0 \cdot n_1 - 0}{n_1 n_2} \Big([u_1^{n_1 - 1}] e^{n_2 u_1}\Big) \Big([u_2^{n_2}] e^{n_1 u_2}\Big) = \frac{1}{n_1} \cdot \frac{n_2^{n_1 - 1}}{(n_1 - 1)!} \cdot \frac{n_1^{n_2}}{n_2!}.\]

Multiplying by \(n_1! n_2!\) to pass from EGF coefficients to labeled graphs gives the exact number of white-rooted bipartite trees:

\[T^{\text{rooted}}_{n_1, n_2} = n_1! n_2! [x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] u_1 = n_1^{n_2} n_2^{n_1 - 1} \implies T^{\text{unrooted}}_{n_1, n_2} = \frac{T^{\text{rooted}}_{n_1, n_2}}{n_1} = n_1^{n_2 - 1} n_2^{n_1 - 1}.\]

Hand Check for \((n_1, n_2) = (2, 2)\) (\(N = n_1 + n_2 = 4\)):

  • Let the white vertices be \(\{W_1, W_2\}\) and black vertices be \(\{B_1, B_2\}\).
  • Every spanning tree of the complete bipartite graph \(K_{2,2}\) is a path of length \(3\) obtained by removing \(1\) of the \(4\) edges of the 4-cycle \(W_1 - B_1 - W_2 - B_2 - W_1\). Thus there are exactly \(4\) unrooted labeled bipartite trees, and since each has \(n_1 = 2\) choices of white root, there are \(4 \times 2 = 8\) white-rooted trees.
  • Let us trace both terms of the division-free Form 1 (\([u_1^2 u_2^2] u_1 \big(1 - u_1 u_2\big) e^{2 u_2} e^{2 u_1}\)) directly:
    1. Naive functional digraph term (\(1\) in \(1 - u_1 u_2\)): \[n_1! n_2! [u_1^1] e^{2 u_1} \cdot [u_2^2] e^{2 u_2} = 2! 2! \left(\frac{2^1}{1!}\right) \left(\frac{2^2}{2!}\right) = 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 16.\] Why \(16\) instead of \(8\)? Because besides the \(8\) valid trees, there are \(8\) disconnected functional digraphs consisting of a rooted singleton \((W_{\text{root}})\) (where the root chooses \(0\) children) plus a directed 2-cycle \(W_{\text{other}} \leftrightarrows B_{\text{cycle}}\) and the remaining black vertex pointing into \(W_{\text{root}}\) or \(W_{\text{other}}\)!
    2. Cycle-cancellation term (\(-u_1 u_2\) in \(1 - u_1 u_2\)): \[- n_1! n_2! [u_1^0] e^{2 u_1} \cdot [u_2^1] e^{2 u_2} = - 2! 2! \left(\frac{2^0}{0!}\right) \left(\frac{2^1}{1!}\right) = - 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = -8.\]
    3. Net count: \(16 - 8 = 8 = 2^2 \cdot 2^{2-1}\), matching Scoins’s formula and the 4-cycle edge-deletion count!

17.2 MacMahon’s Master Theorem & The BEST Theorem

Concrete Combinatorial Model: Homogeneous Linear Powers & Weighted Cycle Covers

Suppose we are given a \(d \times d\) matrix \(A = (a_{i,j})_{1 \le i, j \le d}\) over a commutative ring \(K\) and non-negative integers \(m_1, \dots, m_d \ge 0\). Consider the product of \(d\) homogeneous linear forms raised to the powers \(m_1, \dots, m_d\):

\[P(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^d \left( \sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} x_j \right)^{m_i}.\]

What is the diagonal-like coefficient \([x_1^{m_1} x_2^{m_2} \cdots x_d^{m_d}] P(\mathbf{x})\)?

Combinatorially, imagine \(d\) boxes labeled \(1, \dots, d\), where box \(i\) initially contains \(m_i\) distinguishable balls of color \(i\). Each ball in box \(i\) is independently thrown into some box \(j \in \{1, \dots, d\}\) with transition weight \(a_{i,j} x_j\). Asking for the coefficient of \(x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_d^{m_d}\) asks for the total weight of all ball-redistribution assignments in which every box \(j\) ends up with the exact same number of balls \(m_j\) that it started with!

In 1915, Major Percy Alexander MacMahon proved a remarkable identity that turns this coefficient extraction into the coefficient of the reciprocal of a single \(d \times d\) determinant. With Good–Lagrange Form 2 in our toolkit, MacMahon’s Master Theorem is a three-line corollary!

  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:

    3. Set \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u}) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} u_j\)! Theorem 17.2: MacMahon’s Master Theorem (1915)

Let \(K\) be any commutative ring, let \(A = (a_{i,j})_{1 \le i, j \le d} \in K^{d \times d}\) be a \(d \times d\) matrix, and let \(X = \operatorname{diag}(x_1, \dots, x_d)\). For all non-negative integers \(m_1, \dots, m_d \ge 0\):

\[[x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_d^{m_d}] \prod_{i=1}^d \left( \sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} x_j \right)^{m_i} = [x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_d^{m_d}] \frac{1}{\det(I_d - X A)}.\]

Furthermore, expanding \(\det(I_d - X A)\) by principal minors over all subsets \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}\) gives the explicit polynomial denominator

\[\det(I_d - X A) = 1 + \sum_{\emptyset \neq S \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}} (-1)^{|S|} \det(A_S) \prod_{i \in S} x_i,\]

where \(A_S = (a_{i,j})_{i, j \in S}\) is the principal submatrix of \(A\) indexed by \(S\).

Single-Step Verbalized Proof:

  1. Step 1 (Homogenization via \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u}) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} u_j\)): Define \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u}) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} u_j \in K[[u_1, \dots, u_d]]\). Since \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{0}) = 1 \in K^\times\), Theorem 17.1 applies. Moreover, because \(\prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{m_i}\) has total degree at most \(M = \sum_{i=1}^d m_i\) in \(u_1, \dots, u_d\), and the monomial \(u_1^{m_1} \cdots u_d^{m_d}\) has total degree exactly \(M\), only the top-degree homogeneous part \(\sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} u_j\) of each factor \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\) contributes to \([u_1^{m_1} \cdots u_d^{m_d}]\): \[[u_1^{m_1} \cdots u_d^{m_d}] \prod_{i=1}^d \left( \sum_{j=1}^d a_{i,j} u_j \right)^{m_i} = [u_1^{m_1} \cdots u_d^{m_d}] \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{m_i}.\]
  2. Step 2 (Applying Good–Lagrange Form 2): By Form 2 of Theorem 17.1 with \(H(\mathbf{u}) = 1\), \[[u_1^{m_1} \cdots u_d^{m_d}] \prod_{i=1}^d \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{m_i} = [x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_d^{m_d}] \frac{1}{\det\!\big(I_d - J(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\big)},\] where \(u_i = x_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\) and \(J_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u_j}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j} = \frac{u_j a_{i,j}}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\).
  3. Step 3 (Eliminating \(\mathbf{u}\) in favor of \(\mathbf{x}\) via similarity): Recall from Step 4 of Theorem 17.1 that \(\det(I_d - J(\mathbf{u})) = \det(I_d - \tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}))\), where \(\tilde{J}_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j} = \frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})} a_{i,j}\).
  4. Step 4 (Substituting the defining relation \(u_i / \Phi_i(\mathbf{u}) = x_i\)): By definition of the implicit system \(u_i = x_i \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\), the ratio \(\frac{u_i}{\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})}\) is literally \(x_i\)! Therefore \(\tilde{J}_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})) = x_i a_{i,j}\), so the matrix \(\tilde{J}(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}))\) is equal to \(X A\) where \(X = \operatorname{diag}(x_1, \dots, x_d)\). Substituting \(\det(I_d - \tilde{J}) = \det(I_d - X A)\) completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

Worked Application: The Full 3-Parameter Dixon’s Identity

Consider the famous 3-parameter alternating binomial sum discovered by A. C. Dixon (1891, 1903): for any non-negative integers \(a, b, c \ge 0\), evaluate

\[S(a, b, c) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} (-1)^k \binom{a+b}{a+k} \binom{b+c}{b+k} \binom{c+a}{c+k}.\]

In univariate generating functions, this sum looks formidable because \(k\) appears in all three factors simultaneously. Let us see how MacMahon’s Master Theorem evaluates \(S(a, b, c)\) in seconds!

  1. Rewrite the sum as a 3D diagonal coefficient: Observe that by the binomial theorem, \[\binom{a+b}{a+k} = \binom{a+b}{b-k} = [x_1^{a+k} x_2^{b-k}] (x_1 - x_2)^{a+b} (-1)^{b-k}.\] Applying this symmetrically to the three binomial coefficients around the 3-cycle \((x_1, x_2, x_3)\): \[\binom{a+b}{b-k}\binom{b+c}{c-k}\binom{c+a}{a-k} (-1)^k = (-1)^{a+b+c} \big[x_1^{a+k} x_2^{b-k}\big](x_2 - x_1)^{a+b} \cdot \big[x_2^{b+k} x_3^{c-k}\big](x_3 - x_2)^{b+c} \cdot \big[x_3^{c+k} x_1^{a-k}\big](x_1 - x_3)^{c+a}.\] Better yet, divide by \(x_1^a x_2^b x_3^c\): in the product \((x_2 - x_3)^{b+c} (x_3 - x_1)^{c+a} (x_1 - x_2)^{a+b}\), a monomial contributes to \([x_1^{b+c} x_2^{c+a} x_3^{a+b}]\) if and only if:

    • Factor 1 \((x_2 - x_3)^{b+c}\) picks \(x_2^{b+k} (-x_3)^{c-k}\) for some integer \(k\),
    • Then to reach total degree \(c+a\) in \(x_2\) and \(a+b\) in \(x_3\), Factor 3 \((x_1 - x_2)^{a+b}\) must pick \(x_1^{a+k}(-x_2)^{b-k}\), and Factor 2 \((x_3 - x_1)^{c+a}\) must pick \(x_3^{c+k}(-x_1)^{a-k}\)! Collecting the signs \((-1)^{(c-k) + (a-k) + (b-k)} = (-1)^{a+b+c-3k} = (-1)^{a+b+c} (-1)^k\), we obtain the exact MacMahon form with \(m_1 = b+c, m_2 = c+a, m_3 = a+b\): \[S(a, b, c) = (-1)^{a+b+c} [x_1^{b+c} x_2^{c+a} x_3^{a+b}] (x_2 - x_3)^{b+c} (x_3 - x_1)^{c+a} (x_1 - x_2)^{a+b}.\]

  2. Apply MacMahon’s Master Theorem (\(d = 3\)): Here the linear forms have coefficient matrix \[A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.\] Notice that \(A\) is skew-symmetric (\(A^T = -A\))! Therefore:

    • Every \(1 \times 1\) principal minor is \(0\) (\(a_{i,i} = 0\)).
    • Every \(2 \times 2\) principal minor is \(\det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \pm 1 \\ \mp 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = +1\).
    • The \(3 \times 3\) determinant \(\det(A) = 0\) because a skew-symmetric matrix of odd dimension is singular (\(A \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}\))!

  3. Evaluate \(\det(I_3 - X A)^{-1}\): By the principal minor expansion in Theorem 17.2, \[\det(I_3 - X A) = 1 - (0)(x_1 + x_2 + x_3) + (+1)(x_1 x_2 + x_2 x_3 + x_3 x_1) - (0) x_1 x_2 x_3 = 1 + x_1 x_2 + x_2 x_3 + x_3 x_1.\] Therefore, by MacMahon’s Master Theorem and the multinomial theorem: \[S(a, b, c) = (-1)^{a+b+c} [x_1^{b+c} x_2^{c+a} x_3^{a+b}] \frac{1}{1 + x_1 x_2 + x_2 x_3 + x_3 x_1} = (-1)^{a+b+c} [x_1^{b+c} x_2^{c+a} x_3^{a+b}] (-1)^{a+b+c} \binom{a+b+c}{a, b, c} (x_2 x_3)^a (x_3 x_1)^b (x_1 x_2)^c = \frac{(a+b+c)!}{a!\,b!\,c!}.\]

Concrete Hand Check for \(a = b = c = 1\):

  • Direct sum: \(k = -1\) gives \((-1)^{-1}\binom{2}{0}\binom{2}{0}\binom{2}{0} = -1\); \(k = 0\) gives \((-1)^0 \binom{2}{1}^3 = +8\); \(k = +1\) gives \((-1)^1 \binom{2}{2}^3 = -1\). Total: \(-1 + 8 - 1 = 6\).
  • Dixon’s formula: \(\frac{(1+1+1)!}{1! 1! 1!} = \frac{3!}{1} = 6\). Exact match!

Connection to the BEST Theorem: Cycle Covers vs. Single Eulerian Trails

Look closely at what MacMahon’s Master Theorem counts vs. what we need when counting Eulerian trails in a directed multigraph (such as De Bruijn sequence reconstruction in SS-2 #044 ABC 336 G):

  • In a directed graph with vertex out-degrees \(m_i = d^+(i)\) and adjacency matrix \(A\), the coefficient \([x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_d^{m_d}] \prod_{i=1}^d \big(\sum_j a_{i,j} x_j\big)^{m_i} = [x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_d^{m_d}] \det(I_d - X A)^{-1}\) counts all local pairings of incoming and outgoing edges at each vertex. Globally, such a pairing decomposes the edges into a single Eulerian circuit PLUS arbitrary disconnected Eulerian sub-circuits!
  • How do we forbid disconnected Eulerian components and count single connected Eulerian circuits/trails? By the BEST Theorem (de Bruijn, van Aardenne-Ehrenfest, Smith, and Tutte, 1951):
    1. In any single Eulerian circuit (or trail ending at \(t\)), look at the last-exit edge leaving each vertex \(v \neq t\). Because you can never return to \(v\) after taking its last exit, the set of last-exit edges cannot contain any directed cycle—it forms a spanning directed arborescence \(T\) converging into the root \(t\)!
    2. Conversely, given any spanning arborescence \(T\) rooted at \(t\) and any local cyclic ordering of the remaining \(d^+(v) - 1\) outgoing edges at each vertex \(v\), following the next unused edge at each visit automatically traverses every edge in a single Eulerian tour without ever getting stranded early!
    3. By Tutte’s Directed Matrix-Tree Theorem (which is itself the linear case of Good–Lagrange Form 1!), the number of spanning arborescences converging to \(t\) equals the \((t, t)\)-cofactor \(\det L^{(t)}\) of the directed Laplacian \(L = \operatorname{diag}(d^+) - A\). Multiplying by \(\prod_v (d^+(v) - 1)!\) (and dividing by \(\prod_e X_e!\) if parallel edges of the same type \(e\) are indistinguishable) gives the exact Eulerian trail count!

17.3 The Ring of Symmetric Functions \(\Lambda\), Newton–Girard Duality & Failure Mode #7

Concrete Combinatorial Model: Subsets vs. Multisets vs. Power Moments

Suppose we have a set of variables (or eigenvalues, or item weights) \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m\). In combinatorial enumeration and polynomial root algebra, three generating series in an auxiliary formal variable \(t\) package all symmetric information about \(\{x_1, \dots, x_m\}\):

  1. Elementary Symmetric Generating Function \(E(t)\) (Subsets without replacement): \[E(t) = \sum_{k \ge 0} e_k t^k = \prod_{i=1}^m (1 + x_i t), \qquad e_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le m} x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k}.\] Here \([t^k] E(t) = e_k\) is the total weight of all size-\(k\) subsets of items (each item used at most once).
  2. Complete Homogeneous Symmetric Generating Function \(H(t)\) (Multisets with replacement): \[H(t) = \sum_{k \ge 0} h_k t^k = \prod_{i=1}^m \frac{1}{1 - x_i t}, \qquad h_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 \le \dots \le i_k \le m} x_{i_1} \cdots x_{i_k}.\] Here \([t^k] H(t) = h_k\) is the total weight of all size-\(k\) multisets of items (each item may be repeated arbitrarily many times).
  3. Power Sum Generating Function \(P(t)\) (Monochromatic \(k\)-th power moments): \[P(t) = \sum_{k \ge 1} p_k t^k = \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{x_i t}{1 - x_i t}, \qquad p_k = \sum_{i=1}^m x_i^k.\] Here \([t^k] P(t) = p_k\) is the \(k\)-th power sum (a single item chosen \(k\) times simultaneously).
  4. Schur Symmetric Functions (\(s_\lambda\), indexed by integer partitions \(\lambda = (\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_\ell > 0)\)): While \(e_k\), \(h_k\), and \(p_k\) are 1-parameter families generated by \(E(t), H(t), P(t)\), the linear basis of \(\Lambda\) as a module is formed by the Schur polynomials \(s_\lambda(x_1, \dots, x_m)\), defined via the Jacobi–Trudi Determinant in terms of \(h_k\) (or dual conjugate \(\lambda'\) in terms of \(e_k\), a special case of Lindström–Gessel–Viennot non-intersecting lattice paths in Chapter 21): \[s_\lambda = \det_{1 \le i, j \le \ell}\!\bigl(h_{\lambda_i - i + j}\bigr) = \det_{1 \le i, j \le \lambda_1}\!\bigl(e_{\lambda'_i - i + j}\bigr), \qquad (\text{with } h_0 = e_0 = 1 \text{ and } h_r = e_r = 0 \text{ for } r < 0).\] Notice that for a single-row partition \(\lambda = (k)\), \(s_{(k)} = h_k\), and for a single-column partition \(\lambda = (1^k)\), \(s_{(1^k)} = e_k\).

17.4 2D Quarter-Plane Lattice Walks (\(i, j \ge 0\)), The Obstinate Kernel Method & Bivariate PDE Companion Matrices

Concrete Combinatorial Model: Lattice Walks in the First Quadrant \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\)

Let \(\mathcal{S} \subseteq \{-1, 0, +1\}^2 \setminus \{(0, 0)\}\) be a finite set of small steps in the plane, optionally weighted by \(w_{a,b} \in K\). We start at the origin \((0, 0)\) (or a general cell \((i_0, j_0) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\)) and take \(n\) steps from \(\mathcal{S}\) subject to the quarter-plane constraint: the walk must stay inside the first quadrant \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2 = \{(i, j) : i \ge 0 \text{ and } j \ge 0\}\) at every single intermediate step \(0 \le s \le n\).

Let \(q(i, j; n)\) be the number of valid quarter-plane walks of length \(n\) ending at \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\), and define the trivariate generating function:

\[Q(x, y; t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} t^n \sum_{i \ge 0, \, j \ge 0} q(i, j; n) x^i y^j \in K[x, y][[t]].\]

Notice that the boundary values are specialized evaluations of \(Q(x, y; t)\):

  • \(Q(0, 0; t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} q(0, 0; n) t^n\) counts excursions returning to the origin \((0, 0)\).
  • \(Q(1, 1; t) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \big(\sum_{i, j \ge 0} q(i, j; n)\big) t^n\) counts all quarter-plane walks of length \(n\) regardless of endpoint.
  • \(Q(x, 0; t)\) and \(Q(0, y; t)\) are the boundary generating functions along the horizontal axis \(j = 0\) and the vertical axis \(i = 0\).

Step 1: Deriving the Fundamental Kernel Equation of the Quarter Plane

Let \(S(x, y) = \sum_{(a, b) \in \mathcal{S}} w_{a,b} x^a y^b \in K[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]\) be the Laurent step polynomial of the walk. Since every step \((a, b) \in \{-1, 0, +1\}^2\), we can group \(S(x, y)\) by powers of \(y\) or by powers of \(x\):

\[S(x, y) = A_{-1}(x) y^{-1} + A_0(x) + A_1(x) y = \tilde{A}_{-1}(y) x^{-1} + \tilde{A}_0(y) + \tilde{A}_1(y) x.\]

Let us trace what happens when we append one step \(t S(x, y)\) to an existing quarter-plane walk \(Q(x, y; t)\):

  • Every walk of length \(n \ge 1\) is obtained by taking a valid walk of length \(n - 1\) and appending a step \((a, b) \in \mathcal{S}\), EXCEPT that we must subtract any step that steps across the bottom boundary into \(j = -1\) or across the left boundary into \(i = -1\)!
  • When does a step cross into \(j = -1\)? Precisely when the walk is on the horizontal axis \(j = 0\) (series \(Q(x, 0; t)\)) and takes a downward step \(b = -1\) (Laurent factor \(t y^{-1} A_{-1}(x)\)).
  • When does a step cross into \(i = -1\)? Precisely when the walk is on the vertical axis \(i = 0\) (series \(Q(0, y; t)\)) and takes a leftward step \(a = -1\) (Laurent factor \(t x^{-1} \tilde{A}_{-1}(y)\)).
  • Finally, if \((-1, -1) \in \mathcal{S}\) with weight \(\epsilon = w_{-1,-1} = [x^{-1}]A_{-1}(x) = [y^{-1}]\tilde{A}_{-1}(y)\), a walk at the corner \((0, 0)\) (series \(Q(0, 0; t)\)) stepping by \((-1, -1)\) was subtracted twice (once in \(Q(x, 0; t)\) and once in \(Q(0, y; t)\)), so by Inclusion–Exclusion we must add back \(\epsilon t x^{-1} y^{-1} Q(0, 0; t)\)!

This step-by-step accounting yields the fundamental recurrence:

\[Q(x, y; t) = 1 + t S(x, y) Q(x, y; t) - t y^{-1} A_{-1}(x) Q(x, 0; t) - t x^{-1} \tilde{A}_{-1}(y) Q(0, y; t) + \epsilon t x^{-1} y^{-1} Q(0, 0; t).\]

Multiplying through by \(x y\) to clear all negative powers \(x^{-1}, y^{-1}\) and moving \(t S(x, y) Q(x, y; t)\) to the left-hand side gives the Quarter-Plane Kernel Equation:

\[K(x, y; t) Q(x, y; t) = x y - F(x; t) - G(y; t) + \epsilon t Q(0, 0; t),\]

where:

  • \(K(x, y; t) = x y \big(1 - t S(x, y)\big) \in K[x, y, t]\) is the bivariate kernel polynomial,
  • \(F(x; t) = t x A_{-1}(x) Q(x, 0; t) \in t K[x][[t]]\) depends only on \(x\) and \(t\), and
  • \(G(y; t) = t y \tilde{A}_{-1}(y) Q(0, y; t) \in t K[y][[t]]\) depends only on \(y\) and \(t\).
NoteDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: The Obstinate Kernel Method (Bousquet-Mélou & Mishna, 2010)
  • Diagnostic Signal: You have a 2D recurrence on the quarter plane \(i, j \ge 0\) (or a 3D ballot walk \(x_1 \ge x_2 \ge x_3\) after changing coordinates to \(i = x_1 - x_2 \ge 0\) and \(j = x_2 - x_3 \ge 0\)) whose kernel equation has the decoupled-boundary shape \[K(x, y; t) Q(x, y; t) = x y - F(x; t) - G(y; t) + C(t),\] where \(F(x; t)\) is independent of \(y\) and \(G(y; t)\) is independent of \(x\).
  • Why the 1D Kernel Method Stalls: If we substitute a root \(y = Y_0(x; t)\) of \(K(x, y; t) = 0\), the left-hand side vanishes, giving \(0 = x Y_0(x; t) - F(x; t) - G(Y_0(x; t); t) + C(t)\). Because \(G(Y_0(x; t); t)\) is an unknown function of \(Y_0(x; t)\), one equation cannot determine two unknown functions \(F\) and \(G\)!
  • Reverse-Engineering the Obstinate Kernel Method:
    1. Instead of stopping at one root substitution, look at the symmetries of the step polynomial \(S(x, y)\)! Because \(S(x, y) = A_{-1}(x) y^{-1} + A_0(x) + A_1(x) y\) has degree \(1\) in \(y\) and \(y^{-1}\), the quadratic equation \(S(x, y) = S(x, y')\) has two roots for \(y'\): the trivial root \(y' = y\), and the Vieta second root \(y' = \frac{A_{-1}(x)}{A_1(x) y}\)!
    2. Define the vertical Vieta involution \(\Psi(x, y) = \left(x, \frac{A_{-1}(x)}{A_1(x) y}\right)\) and the horizontal Vieta involution \(\Phi(x, y) = \left(\frac{\tilde{A}_{-1}(y)}{\tilde{A}_1(y) x}, y\right)\).
    3. Notice two extraordinary properties of \(\Phi\) and \(\Psi\):
      • Kernel Invariance: Both \(\Phi\) and \(\Psi\) leave \(S(x, y)\) (and hence \(1 - t S(x, y)\)) completely invariant: \(S(\Phi(x, y)) = S(\Psi(x, y)) = S(x, y)\)!
      • Coordinate Fixing: \(\Psi\) fixes the first coordinate \(x\), so \(F(\Psi(x, y)) = F(x; t)\)! Therefore, subtracting the \(\Psi\)-transformed equation from the original equation cancels \(F(x; t)\) identically! Similarly, \(\Phi\) fixes the second coordinate \(y\), so subtracting the \(\Phi\)-transformed equation cancels \(G(y; t)\) identically!
    4. Apply \(\Psi, \Phi, \Psi, \Phi, \dots\) alternately—this is why Mireille Bousquet-Mélou called it the “Obstinate” Kernel Method: we obstinately keep generating new pairs \((x', y')\) that share the exact same value of \(S(x', y') = S(x, y)\)! If the group \(\mathcal{G} = \langle \Phi, \Psi \rangle\) has finite order \(2m\), the alternating sum around the \(2m\)-element orbit cancels every single \(F\) and \(G\) term in pairs!

Theorem 17.4: The Group of the Walk & The Obstinate Kernel Formula

Assume \(A_{\pm 1}(x), \tilde{A}_{\pm 1}(y) \neq 0\), and define the two birational Vieta involutions:

\[\Phi(x, y) = \left( \frac{\tilde{A}_{-1}(y)}{\tilde{A}_1(y) x}, \, y \right), \qquad \Psi(x, y) = \left( x, \, \frac{A_{-1}(x)}{A_1(x) y} \right).\]

Since \(\Phi^2 = \Psi^2 = \operatorname{id}\), the group \(\mathcal{G} = \langle \Phi, \Psi \rangle\) generated by \(\Phi\) and \(\Psi\) is a dihedral group. Suppose \(\mathcal{G}\) is finite of order \(|\mathcal{G}| = 2m\), where each element \(g \in \mathcal{G}\) has a well-defined sign \(\operatorname{sgn}(g) = (-1)^{\ell(g)} \in \{+1, -1\}\) (with \(\operatorname{sgn}(\Phi) = \operatorname{sgn}(\Psi) = -1\)).

If the corner weight \(\epsilon = w_{-1,-1} = 0\) (or when the alternating sum of the constant term vanishes, as happens whenever \(m\) is odd or by homogeneous extraction), start from the Fundamental Quarter-Plane Kernel Equation:

\[\big(1 - t S(x, y)\big) x y Q(x, y; t) = x y - F(x; t) - G(y; t).\]

Moving \(F(x; t) + G(y; t)\) to the left-hand side and dividing by \(1 - t S(x, y)\) (which is a unit in \(\mathbb{Q}[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}][[t]]\)) yields the exact algebraic decomposition:

\[x y Q(x, y; t) + \frac{F(x; t) + G(y; t)}{1 - t S(x, y)} = \frac{x y}{1 - t S(x, y)}.\]

Taking the alternating orbit sum over \(g \in \mathcal{G}\) cancels all boundary functions \(\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) F(g(x); t) = 0\) and \(\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) G(g(y); t) = 0\) (while leaving \(1 - t S(x, y)\) invariant), yielding the Algebraic Orbit Sum Identity:

\[\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) g\big(x y Q(x, y; t)\big) = \frac{\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) g(x y)}{1 - t S(x, y)}.\]

When every non-identity element \(g \in \mathcal{G} \setminus \{\operatorname{id}\}\) sends \(g\big(x y Q(x, y; t)\big)\) to a Laurent series supported strictly outside the positive quadrant \(x^{>0} y^{>0}\) (i.e., each term in \(g\big(x y Q(x, y; t)\big)\) for \(g \neq \operatorname{id}\) has degree \(\le 0\) in \(x\) or degree \(\le 0\) in \(y\)), applying the positive-part extraction operator \([x^{>0} y^{>0}]\) isolates the identity term \(g = \operatorname{id}\) on the left-hand side:

\[x y Q(x, y; t) = [x^{>0} y^{>0}] \left( \frac{\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) g(x y)}{1 - t S(x, y)} \right).\]

Equivalently, the number of \(n\)-step walks from \((0, 0)\) to \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\) is given directly by the boundary-free Laurent coefficient:

\[q(i, j; n) = [x^{i+1} y^{j+1} t^n] x y Q(x, y; t) = [x^{i+1} y^{j+1}] \left( S(x, y)^n \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) g(x y) \right).\]

Concrete Orbit Traces: Order 4 (\(D_2\)) and Order 6 (\(D_3\))

Let us trace \(\mathcal{G} = \langle \Phi, \Psi \rangle\) on the two most important quarter-plane walk families in competitive programming:

1. Order \(|\mathcal{G}| = 4\) (Klein 4-Group \(D_2\), Orthant Reflection Principle)

Suppose the step polynomial is x-y decoupled (separable) or symmetric under \(x \leftrightarrow x^{-1}\) and \(y \leftrightarrow y^{-1}\), such as the Diagonal Walk \(\mathcal{S} = \{(\pm 1, \pm 1)\}\) with \(S(x, y) = (x + x^{-1})(y + y^{-1})\) or the Simple Square Lattice Walk \(\mathcal{S} = \{(\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\}\) with \(S(x, y) = x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\).

  • Here \(\Phi(x, y) = (x^{-1}, y)\) and \(\Psi(x, y) = (x, y^{-1})\), which commute and generate the 4-element group \(\mathcal{G} = \{(x, y), (x^{-1}, y), (x, y^{-1}), (x^{-1}, y^{-1})\}\).

  • The alternating orbit numerator is: \[\sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \operatorname{sgn}(g) g(x y) = x y - x^{-1} y - x y^{-1} + x^{-1} y^{-1} = \left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right)\left(y - \frac{1}{y}\right).\]

  • Notice that for \(g \neq \operatorname{id}\), \(g(x y Q(x, y; t))\) has only negative powers of \(x\) or negative powers of \(y\)! Thus Theorem 17.4 gives immediately: \[x y Q(x, y; t) = [x^{>0} y^{>0}] \frac{(x - x^{-1})(y - y^{-1})}{1 - t S(x, y)}.\]

  • Hand Check for \(N = 2\) steps of the Simple Walk \(\mathcal{S} = \{(\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\}\) returning to \((0, 0)\): We want \(q(0, 0; 2) = [x^1 y^1] (x - x^{-1})(y - y^{-1})(x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1})^2\). Expanding \((x - x^{-1})(y - y^{-1}) = xy - x y^{-1} - x^{-1} y + x^{-1} y^{-1}\), we extract the constant/quadrant shifts of \((x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1})^2\):

    • From \(x y\): \([x^0 y^0] S(x, y)^2 = 4\) (the 4 unrestricted 2-step returns: \(R L, L R, U D, D U\)).
    • From \(-x y^{-1}\): \(-[x^0 y^2] S(x, y)^2 = -1\) (cancels the illegal walk \(D U\) that stepped to \(y = -1\)).
    • From \(-x^{-1} y\): \(-[x^2 y^0] S(x, y)^2 = -1\) (cancels the illegal walk \(L R\) that stepped to \(x = -1\)).
    • From \(+x^{-1} y^{-1}\): \(+[x^2 y^2] S(x, y)^2 = 0\). Total: \(q(0, 0; 2) = 4 - 1 - 1 + 0 = 2\) (the two valid quarter-plane walks: \(R L\) and \(U D\)).

2. Order \(|\mathcal{G}| = 6\) (Dihedral Group \(D_3 \cong \mathfrak{S}_3\): Tandem & Kreweras Walks)

Consider the Tandem Walk \(\mathcal{S} = \{(+1, 0), (-1, +1), (0, -1)\}\) with step polynomial

\[S(x, y) = x + \frac{y}{x} + \frac{1}{y} = y x^{-1} + y^{-1} + x.\]

Why is this walk so important? Because if you track 3 candidates in a 3D Ballot Walk (\(x_1 \ge x_2 \ge x_3\)) where candidate 1 gets a vote \((+1, 0, 0)\), candidate 2 gets a vote \((0, +1, 0)\), or candidate 3 gets a vote \((0, 0, +1)\), the gap coordinates \(i = x_1 - x_2 \ge 0\) and \(j = x_2 - x_3 \ge 0\) evolve by \((+1, 0)\), \((-1, +1)\), and \((0, -1)\)—which is literally the Tandem Walk!

Let us compute the Vieta involutions \(\Phi\) and \(\Psi\) of \(S(x, y) = x + y x^{-1} + y^{-1}\):

  • Grouping in \(x\): \(S(x, y) = y x^{-1} + y^{-1} + 1 \cdot x\), so \(\tilde{A}_{-1}(y) = y\) and \(\tilde{A}_1(y) = 1\). Thus \(\Phi(x, y) = \left(\frac{y}{x}, \, y\right)\).
  • Grouping in \(y\): \(S(x, y) = 1 \cdot y^{-1} + x + x^{-1} y\), so \(A_{-1}(x) = 1\) and \(A_1(x) = x^{-1}\). Thus \(\Psi(x, y) = \left(x, \, \frac{x}{y}\right)\).

Let us trace the orbit of \((x, y)\) under alternating applications of \(\Phi\) and \(\Psi\):

\[(x, y) \xrightarrow{\;\Phi\;} \left(\frac{y}{x}, \, y\right) \xrightarrow{\;\Psi\;} \left(\frac{y}{x}, \, \frac{1}{x}\right) \xrightarrow{\;\Phi\;} \left(\frac{1}{y}, \, \frac{1}{x}\right) \xrightarrow{\;\Psi\;} \left(\frac{1}{y}, \, \frac{x}{y}\right) \xrightarrow{\;\Phi\;} \left(x, \, \frac{x}{y}\right) \xrightarrow{\;\Psi\;} (x, y).\]

The orbit closes after 6 steps! Moreover, look at the 5 non-identity elements \(g(x y)\):

\[x y Q(x, y; t) = [x^{>0} y^{>0}] \frac{x y - \dfrac{y^2}{x} + \dfrac{y}{x^2} - \dfrac{1}{x y} + \dfrac{x}{y^2} - \dfrac{x^2}{y}}{1 - t\left(x + \dfrac{y}{x} + \dfrac{1}{y}\right)}.\]

Stepping Stone 1 (#154) — Alternating Sequences ([CQOI2018] 交错序列)

  • Source: Luogu P4456 — CQOI 2018
  • Prerequisite Tags: Symmetric Functions & Moments, Failure Mode #7 (Small Prime p <= a+b), Polynomial Companion Matrix Exponentiation

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

A binary string of length \(n\) (\(01\)-string) is called valid (an alternating sequence) if it contains no two adjacent 1s. If a valid binary string of length \(n\) contains \(x\) zeros and \(y\) ones (so \(x + y = n\)), its weight is defined as \(x^a y^b\). Given \(n\), \(a\), \(b\), and a prime modulus \(p\), compute the sum of \(x^a y^b\) over all valid binary strings of length \(n\) modulo \(p\):

\[\operatorname{Ans} = \left( \sum_{\substack{s \in \{0,1\}^n \\ \text{no } 11 \text{ in } s}} (\#0(s))^a (\#1(s))^b \right) \bmod p.\]

  • Constraints: \(1 \le n \le 10^7\), \(0 \le a, b \le 45\) (\(a + b \le 90\)), and \(p \le 10^9\) is an arbitrary prime (in particular, \(p\) may be strictly smaller than \(a + b\), e.g., \(p = 2, 3, 5\)).
  • Required Complexity: \(O\big((a+b)^2 \log n\big)\) time and \(O(a + b)\) space, using zero divisions by \(1, 2, \dots, a+b\).

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n = 3, a = 1, b = 1\))

For \(n = 3\), there are \(F_{3+2} = 5\) valid binary strings without 11:

  • 000: \((x, y) = (3, 0) \implies x^1 y^1 = 3 \cdot 0 = 0\).
  • 001, 010, 100: \((x, y) = (2, 1) \implies x^1 y^1 = 2 \cdot 1 = 2\) each (total \(3 \times 2 = 6\)).
  • 101: \((x, y) = (1, 2) \implies x^1 y^1 = 1 \cdot 2 = 2\). Total sum: \(0 + 6 + 2 = 8\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Notice two critical features of the problem:

  1. For every string of length \(n\), the number of zeros is \(x = n - y\). Substituting \(x = n - y\) into \(x^a y^b\) via the binomial theorem eliminates \(x\) completely and reduces the problem to computing the unnormalized power moments \(S_m(n) = \sum_{\text{valid } s} y^m\) for \(0 \le m \le a + b \le 90\).
  2. Watch out for Failure Mode #7: the prime \(p\) is arbitrary and can be \(\le a + b\)! If you use an Exponential Generating Function \(u = e^z\) where \([z^m] e^{y z} = \frac{y^m}{m!}\), you will have to multiply by \(m!\) at the end, which is fine, only if you never divided by \(1, \dots, m\) when building \(e^z\)! How can we compute all moments \(y^m\) using purely division-free polynomial arithmetic?
Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Instead of the EGF substitution \(u = e^z\) (which requires dividing by \(k!\) in its Taylor series), use the Binomial Moment OGF substitution \(u = 1 + v\)!

  • In the bivariate OGF \(F(t, u) = \sum_{n \ge 0} t^n \sum_{\text{valid } s \in \{0,1\}^n} u^{\#1(s)}\), substituting \(u = 1 + v\) turns \(u^y\) into \((1 + v)^y = \sum_{m \ge 0} \binom{y}{m} v^m\), whose coefficients \(\binom{y}{m} \in \mathbb{Z}\) are 100% division-free integers!
  • Once we have the binomial moments \(B_m(n) = \sum_{\text{valid } s} \binom{y}{m} = [t^n v^m] F(t, 1 + v)\) for \(0 \le m \le a + b\), how do we convert \(\binom{y}{m}\) into \(y^k\) without dividing? Use Stirling numbers of the second kind \(\left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\}\): \[y^k = \sum_{m=0}^k \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\} m! \binom{y}{m} \implies S_k(n) = \sum_{m=0}^k \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\} m! \, B_m(n).\]
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Let \(D = a + b \le 90\). A valid binary string is built step-by-step by a 2-state automaton (State 0: ends in 0 or empty; State 1: ends in 1):

  • Appending 0 transitions from either state to State 0 with weight \(1\).
  • Appending 1 transitions from State 0 to State 1 with weight \(u = 1 + v\). Thus the state vector of polynomials \(\begin{pmatrix} P_0(v) \\ P_1(v) \end{pmatrix} \in \big(\mathbb{F}_p[v]/(v^{D+1})\big)^2\) transitions via multiplication by the \(2 \times 2\) polynomial matrix \(M(v) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 + v & 0 \end{pmatrix} \bmod v^{D+1}\). Raise \(M(v)\) to the \(n\)-th power modulo \(v^{D+1}\) using binary exponentiation in \(O(D^2 \log n)\) time!

Eliminating \(x = n - y\): Since every valid string of length \(n\) satisfies \(x + y = n\), we expand \(x^a y^b = (n - y)^a y^b\) by the binomial theorem: \[\sum_{s} x^a y^b = \sum_{j=0}^a \binom{a}{j} n^{a-j} (-1)^j \left( \sum_{s} y^{b+j} \right) = \sum_{j=0}^a (-1)^j \binom{a}{j} n^{a-j} S_{b+j}(n),\] where \(S_k(n) = \sum_{s} y(s)^k\) for \(0 \le k \le D = a + b \le 90\).

  2. Bypassing Failure Mode #7 via the Binomial Moment Basis \(u = 1 + v\): Because the modulus \(p\) can be smaller than \(D\) (e.g., \(p = 2, 3, \dots, 89\)), \(m!\) is not invertible modulo \(p\) for \(m \ge p\). We therefore avoid the exponential generating function \(u = e^z\) and instead work in the truncated polynomial ring \(R = \mathbb{F}_p[v]/(v^{D+1})\) by setting \(u = 1 + v\). For a string \(s\) with \(y\) ones, its weight in \(R\) is \[u^y = (1 + v)^y = \sum_{m=0}^D \binom{y}{m} v^m \pmod{v^{D+1}}.\] Summing over all valid strings \(s\) of length \(n\) yields the polynomial \(B(n; v) = \sum_{m=0}^D B_m(n) v^m \in R\), where \(B_m(n) = \sum_s \binom{y(s)}{m}\).

  3. 2-State Polynomial Automaton: Partition valid strings of length \(i\) into two sets:

    • Those ending in 0 (or the empty string at \(i = 0\)), with generating polynomial \(P_0^{(i)}(v) \in R\).

    • Those ending in 1, with generating polynomial \(P_1^{(i)}(v) \in R\). At \(i = 0\), the empty string has \(y = 0\) ones and can be extended by either 0 or 1, so \(P_0^{(0)}(v) = 1\) and \(P_1^{(0)}(v) = 0\). For each step \(i \to i + 1\):

    • Appending 0 is valid after both 0 and 1 and adds zero 1s (weight \(1\)): \(P_0^{(i+1)}(v) = P_0^{(i)}(v) + P_1^{(i)}(v)\).

    • Appending 1 is valid only after 0 and adds one 1 (weight \(1 + v\)): \(P_1^{(i+1)}(v) = (1 + v) P_0^{(i)}(v)\). In matrix form over \(R = \mathbb{F}_p[v]/(v^{D+1})\): \[\begin{pmatrix} P_0^{(n)}(v) \\ P_1^{(n)}(v) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 + v & 0 \end{pmatrix}^n \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \pmod{v^{D+1}}, \qquad B(n; v) = P_0^{(n)}(v) + P_1^{(n)}(v).\]

  4. Division-Free Conversion from Binomial Moments \(B_m(n)\) to Power Moments \(S_k(n)\): Using the polynomial identity \(y^k = \sum_{m=0}^k \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\} m! \binom{y}{m}\) (which holds in \(\mathbb{Z}[y]\) and hence modulo any \(p\)), we precompute \(\binom{i}{j} \bmod p\) via Pascal’s triangle and \(\left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\} \bmod p\) via \(\left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\} = \left\{\begin{matrix} k-1 \\ m-1 \end{matrix}\right\} + m \left\{\begin{matrix} k-1 \\ m \end{matrix}\right\}\), then evaluate: \[S_k(n) = \sum_{m=0}^k \left\{\begin{matrix} k \\ m \end{matrix}\right\} m! \, [v^m] B(n; v) \pmod p.\] Finally, we plug \(S_{b+j}(n)\) into the binomial sum from Step 1. Every single operation is an addition, subtraction, or multiplication!

Verification on Hand Trace (\(n = 3, a = 1, b = 1, D = 2\))

  • Source: AtCoder Beginner Contest 336 — Problem G: 16 Integers
  • Prerequisite Tags: MacMahon Master Theorem vs. BEST Theorem, De Bruijn Digraph, Directed Matrix-Tree Laplacian Cofactor

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given \(16\) non-negative integers \(X_{0,0,0,0}, X_{0,0,0,1}, \dots, X_{1,1,1,1}\). Count the number of binary sequences \((A_1, A_2, \dots, A_N) \in \{0, 1\}^N\) of length \(N = 3 + \sum_{i,j,k,l \in \{0,1\}} X_{i,j,k,l}\) such that for every 4-tuple \((i, j, k, l) \in \{0, 1\}^4\), there are exactly \(X_{i,j,k,l}\) indices \(m \in \{1, \dots, N-3\}\) satisfying \((A_m, A_{m+1}, A_{m+2}, A_{m+3}) = (i, j, k, l)\).   • Constraints: \(0 \le X_{i,j,k,l} \le 10^6\), \(\sum_{i,j,k,l} X_{i,j,k,l} \ge 1\) and \(\le 10^6\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O\big(8^5 + \sum X_{i,j,k,l}\big)\) (or \(O\big(8^3 + \sum X_{i,j,k,l}\big)\)) time and \(O\big(\sum X_{i,j,k,l}\big)\) space.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(X_{0,1,0,1} = 1, X_{1,0,1,0} = 1\), all other \(X = 0\))

Here \(N = 3 + 2 = 5\), and our sequence must contain 0101 once and 1010 once:

  • In the De Bruijn graph on \(8\) vertices \(\{0, 1\}^3\), 0101 is the directed edge \(010 \to 101\), and 1010 is the directed edge \(101 \to 010\).
  • The two edges form a 2-cycle \(010 \leftrightarrows 101\). An Eulerian trail of length \(2\) can start at \(010\) (01010: edges \(010 \to 101 \to 010\)) or start at \(101\) (10101: edges \(101 \to 010 \to 101\)).
  • Thus there are 2 valid binary sequences (01010 and 10101).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Every length-\(N\) binary string corresponds bijectively to a walk of \(M = N - 3 = \sum X_{i,j,k,l}\) directed edges in the De Bruijn digraph \(G = (V, E)\) with \(8\) vertices \(V = \{0, 1\}^3\), where each 4-tuple \(e = (i, j, k, l)\) is a directed edge from \(u = (i, j, k)\) to \(v = (j, k, l)\) with multiplicity \(X_e\). Therefore, the problem asks for the number of Eulerian trails in \(G\) (where parallel copies of the same 4-bit pattern \(e\) are indistinguishable, because a binary string is determined purely by the sequence of 4-bit substrings!).

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Recall the distinction from Section 17.2 between MacMahon’s Master Theorem and the BEST Theorem:

  • MacMahon’s \(\det(I - X A)^{-1}\) counts arbitrary unions of Eulerian circuits (including disconnected cycles).
  • To count single connected Eulerian trails from a start vertex \(s \in \{0, 1\}^3\) to an end vertex \(t \in \{0, 1\}^3\), we add one virtual return edge \(t \to s\) to turn the trail into a rooted Eulerian circuit at \(t\) (choosing the virtual edge \(t \to s\) as the fixed last-exit edge of \(t\)).
  • By the BEST Theorem, for a fixed pair \((s, t)\) where the augmented graph is degree-balanced (\(d^+_{\text{aug}}(v) = d^-_{\text{aug}}(v)\) for all \(v\)) and all active vertices (those with \(d^+_{\text{aug}}(v) > 0\)) are connected, the number of distinguishable-edge Eulerian trails from \(s\) to \(t\) is \[\det L^{(t)} \cdot \prod_{v \in V_{\text{active}}} \big(d^+_{\text{aug}}(v) - 1\big)!,\] where \(L^{(t)}\) is the \((|V_{\text{active}}| - 1) \times (|V_{\text{active}}| - 1)\) minor of the directed Laplacian \(L_{u,v} = d^+_{\text{aug}}(u) \delta_{u,v} - A_{u,v}\) obtained by deleting row and column \(t\) (restricting to the active vertex set \(V_{\text{active}}\)!).
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Don’t forget two crucial details:

  1. Does the virtual edge \(t \to s\) appear inside the Laplacian \(L\)? No! In our rooted Eulerian circuit, the virtual edge \(t \to s\) is fixed as the last-exit edge of \(t\), while for every active vertex \(v \neq t\), its last-exit edge must be one of the original edges of \(G\) forming a spanning arborescence converging into \(t\). Since the \((t, t)\)-cofactor \(\det L^{(t)}\) does not depend on row \(t\) of \(L\) anyway, you can compute \(\det L^{(t)}\) directly from the unaugmented Laplacian \(L_{u,v} = \big(\sum_{w} A_{u,w}\big) \delta_{u,v} - A_{u,v}\) restricted to \(V_{\text{active}} \setminus \{t\}\)!
  2. Because the \(X_e\) parallel edges of each 4-bit type \(e \in \{0, 1\}^4\) are indistinguishable in the output binary string, divide the final answer by \(\prod_{e \in \{0,1\}^4} X_e!\).

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. De Bruijn Multigraph Construction: Construct the directed multigraph \(G\) on the \(8\) vertices \(V = \{0, 1, \dots, 7\}\) (representing 3-bit strings \((i, j, k)\) in binary). For each of the \(16\) tuples \(e = (i, j, k, l) \in \{0, 1\}^4\), add \(X_e\) parallel directed edges from \(u = 4i + 2j + k\) to \(v = 2j + 2k + l\). Let \(d^+(u) = \sum_v A_{u,v}\) and \(d^-(u) = \sum_v A_{v,u}\) be the out-degree and in-degree of vertex \(u\) in \(G\). Let \(V_{\text{active}} = \{u \in V : d^+(u) + d^-(u) > 0\}\).

  2. Enumerating Valid Start/End Pairs \((s, t)\): Every Eulerian trail in \(G\) starts at some vertex \(s \in V_{\text{active}}\) and ends at some vertex \(t \in V_{\text{active}}\). Adding one virtual edge \(e^* : t \to s\) yields an augmented multigraph \(G_{\text{aug}}(s, t)\) with degrees \[d^+_{\text{aug}}(u) = d^+(u) + [u = t], \qquad d^-_{\text{aug}}(u) = d^-(u) + [u = s].\] A pair \((s, t) \in V_{\text{active}}^2\) can support an Eulerian trail if and only if \(d^+_{\text{aug}}(u) = d^-_{\text{aug}}(u)\) for all \(u \in V\). Specifically, if every \(u \in V\) initially satisfies \(d^+(u) = d^-(u)\), then \(s = t\) may be chosen as any vertex in \(V_{\text{active}}\); if exactly one vertex has \(d^+(u) - d^-(u) = +1\) and one has \(d^+(v) - d^-(v) = -1\), then \(s = u\) and \(t = v\) are uniquely forced; otherwise, no Eulerian trail exists and the answer is \(0\).

  3. Applying the BEST Theorem via Last-Exit Arborescences: Fix a valid pair \((s, t) \in V_{\text{active}}^2\). Temporarily label all \(X_e\) parallel copies of each edge type \(e\) so that all \(M = \sum_e X_e\) edges of \(G\) are distinguishable. By adding the virtual edge \(e^* : t \to s\), every Eulerian trail from \(s\) to \(t\) in \(G\) corresponds bijectively to an Eulerian circuit in \(G_{\text{aug}}(s, t)\) starting and ending with \(e^*\).

    • For each vertex \(u \in V_{\text{active}} \setminus \{t\}\), record the last edge leaving \(u\) along the trail; for \(t\), the last edge leaving \(t\) in the augmented circuit is \(e^*\).
    • The \(|V_{\text{active}}| - 1\) last-exit edges from \(u \in V_{\text{active}} \setminus \{t\}\) form a directed spanning arborescence of \(G[V_{\text{active}}]\) rooted at and converging into \(t\).
    • By Tutte’s Directed Matrix-Tree Theorem (Good–Lagrange Form 1 for linear forms), the number of such spanning arborescences is \(\det L^{(t)}\), where \(L\) is the unaugmented \(|V_{\text{active}}| \times |V_{\text{active}}|\) out-degree Laplacian of \(G[V_{\text{active}}]\): \[L_{u, v} = d^+(u)\delta_{u,v} - A_{u, v} \qquad (u, v \in V_{\text{active}}),\] and \(L^{(t)}\) is obtained by deleting the row and column of \(t\). (If \(G[V_{\text{active}}]\) is disconnected, \(\det L^{(t)} = 0\) automatically!)
    • At each vertex \(u \in V_{\text{active}}\), the remaining \(d^+_{\text{aug}}(u) - 1\) outgoing edges can be ordered in \(\big(d^+_{\text{aug}}(u) - 1\big)!\) ways.

  4. Dividing by Indistinguishable Edge Multiplicities: Summing over all valid pairs \((s, t) \in V_{\text{active}}^2\) and dividing by \(\prod_{e \in \{0,1\}^4} X_e!\) because parallel edges of the same 4-bit pattern \(e\) produce identical binary strings gives the exact count: \[\operatorname{Ans} = \frac{1}{\prod_{e \in \{0,1\}^4} X_e!} \sum_{\substack{(s, t) \in V_{\text{active}}^2 \\ \forall u,\, d^+(u) + [u=t] = d^-(u) + [u=s]}} \det L^{(t)} \prod_{u \in V_{\text{active}}} \big(d^+(u) + [u = t] - 1\big)! \pmod{998244353}.\]

Verification on Hand Trace (\(X_{0101} = 1, X_{1010} = 1\))

Here \(V_{\text{active}} = \{2, 5\}\) (representing 010 and 101), with \(A_{2,5} = A_{5,2} = 1\) and \(d^+(2) = d^-(2) = d^+(5) = d^-(5) = 1\).

  • Valid pairs \((s, t)\) are \((2, 2)\) and \((5, 5)\).
  • The \(2 \times 2\) Laplacian on \(\{2, 5\}\) is \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), so deleting row/col \(t\) gives \(\det L^{(t)} = 1\).
  • For \((s, t) = (2, 2)\): \(\det L^{(2)} (1 + 1 - 1)! (1 + 0 - 1)! = 1 \cdot 1! \cdot 0! = 1\).

Intermediate 1 (#228) — Unlabeled Phylogenetic & Multiset Trees (LibreOJ 6671)

  • Source: LibreOJ 6671 — Multiset / Unlabeled Tree Functional Equation
  • Prerequisite Tags: Symmetric Function Duality (H(t) & E(t)), Pólya Exp/Ln Harmonics, Newton Iteration

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Count the number of unlabeled rooted trees with \(n\) leaves (\(1 \le n \le 10^5\)) modulo \(998244353\), where each internal vertex is unweighted and selects an unordered non-empty multiset of at least 2 subtrees. Specifically, compute \([x^1] F(x), \dots, [x^N] F(x) \bmod 998244353\) for the canonical unlabeled rooted phylogenetic/series-parallel tree equation:

\[F(x) = x + \operatorname{MSET}_{\ge 2}(F(x)) = x + \prod_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{\big(1 - x^k\big)^{f_k}} - 1 - F(x), \qquad \text{where } F(x) = \sum_{k \ge 1} f_k x^k.\]

  • Constraints: \(N \le 10^5\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space via Newton Iteration + Pólya Harmonic Separation.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

Let \(f_n = [x^n] F(x)\) be the number of unlabeled rooted phylogenetic trees with \(n\) leaves where every internal node has degree \(\ge 2\):

  • \(n = 1\): \(1\) leaf (o) \(\implies f_1 = 1\).
  • \(n = 2\): \(1\) tree ((o, o)) \(\implies f_2 = 1\).
  • \(n = 3\): \(2\) trees ((o, o, o) and (o, (o, o))) \(\implies f_3 = 2\).
  • \(n = 4\): \(5\) trees ((o,o,o,o), (o,o,(o,o)), ((o,o),(o,o)), (o,(o,o,o)), (o,(o,(o,o)))) \(\implies f_4 = 5\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Whenever an unlabeled combinatorial class takes an unordered multiset of components from a class \(\mathcal{F}\) with OGF \(F(x) = \sum_{k \ge 1} f_k x^k\), the infinite product \(\prod_{k \ge 1} (1 - x^k)^{-f_k}\) is literally the Complete Homogeneous Symmetric Generating Function \(H(t)\) specialized so that there are \(f_k\) formal variables equal to \(x^k\)! Similarly, if components in the set must be pairwise distinct (\(\operatorname{PSET}\)), the product \(\prod_{k \ge 1} (1 + x^k)^{f_k}\) is the Elementary Symmetric Generating Function \(E(t)\)!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Apply Theorem 17.3 (the Pólya / Newton–Girard exponential identity \(H(t) = \exp\!\big(\sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{p_j}{j} t^j\big)\)):

  • Since there are \(f_k\) formal variables of weight \(x^k\), the \(j\)-th power sum of these variables is

    \[p_j = \sum_{k \ge 1} f_k \big(x^k\big)^j = F(x^j).\]

  • Therefore, the multiset operator becomes

    \[\operatorname{MSET}(F)(x) = \prod_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{(1 - x^k)^{f_k}} = \exp\!\left( \sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{F(x^j)}{j} \right), \qquad \operatorname{PSET}(F)(x) = \exp\!\left( \sum_{j \ge 1} (-1)^{j-1} \frac{F(x^j)}{j} \right).\]

  • Notice how the \(j = 1\) term in the exponent is \(F(x)\) itself, while all higher-harmonic terms \(S_{\ge 2}(x) = \sum_{j \ge 2} \frac{F(x^j)}{j}\) depend only on \([x^1]F, \dots, [x^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}]F\)!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

During the doubling step \(m \to 2m\) of Newton’s Method (assuming \(F(x) \bmod x^m\) is already known):

  1. For \(j \ge 2\), \(F(x^j) \bmod x^{2m}\) only requires coefficients of \(F\) up to degree \(\lfloor (2m-1)/2 \rfloor < m\), which are already 100% known! Compute \(S_{\ge 2}(x) = \sum_{j=2}^{2m-1} \frac{F(x^j)}{j} \bmod x^{2m}\) in \(O(m \log m)\) harmonic time, and set \(E_{\ge 2}(x) = \exp(S_{\ge 2}(x)) \bmod x^{2m}\).

  2. Now the functional equation \(2 F(x) - x + 1 - \exp\!\big(F(x) + S_{\ge 2}(x)\big) = 0 \pmod{x^{2m}}\) is a standard univariate equation in \(F(x)\) alone! Apply one Newton step:

    \[F_{\text{new}}(x) = F(x) - \frac{2 F(x) - x + 1 - E_{\ge 2}(x) e^{F(x)}}{2 - E_{\ge 2}(x) e^{F(x)}} \pmod{x^{2m}}.\]

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Symmetric Function Translation of Unlabeled Multisets: Let \(F(x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} f_n x^n\) be the OGF of our unlabeled rooted trees. A tree is either a single leaf (\(x\)) or an internal root attached to an unordered multiset of \(\ge 2\) subtrees. Since the OGF of all multisets of subtrees is \(\operatorname{MSET}(F)(x) = \prod_{k \ge 1} (1 - x^k)^{-f_k}\), subtracting the empty multiset (\(1\)) and single-subtree multisets (\(F(x)\)) gives

    \[F(x) = x + \prod_{k \ge 1} (1 - x^k)^{-f_k} - 1 - F(x) \iff 2 F(x) - x + 1 = \prod_{k \ge 1} (1 - x^k)^{-f_k}.\]

    Taking logarithms on the right-hand side and expanding \(-\ln(1 - x^k) = \sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{x^{j k}}{j}\):

    \[\ln \prod_{k \ge 1} (1 - x^k)^{-f_k} = \sum_{k \ge 1} f_k \sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{x^{j k}}{j} = \sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{1}{j} \sum_{k \ge 1} f_k \big(x^j\big)^k = \sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{F(x^j)}{j}.\]

    Exponentiating gives the Pólya functional equation:

    \[\Phi(F) := 2 F(x) - x + 1 - \exp\!\left( F(x) + \sum_{j \ge 2} \frac{F(x^j)}{j} \right) = 0.\]

  2. Harmonic Separation for Newton Iteration (\(m \to 2m\)): Suppose we have already computed \(F_m(x) = F(x) \bmod x^m\). When lifting from precision \(x^m\) to \(x^{2m}\), look at any harmonic \(j \ge 2\): since \(\operatorname{val}_x(F) = 1\), we have

    \[F(x^j) \equiv F_m(x^j) \pmod{x^{j m}}, \qquad \text{and } j m \ge 2m \text{ for all } j \ge 2.\]

    Therefore, the entire higher-harmonic sum

    \[S_{\ge 2}(x) = \sum_{j=2}^{2m-1} \frac{F_m(x^j)}{j} \pmod{x^{2m}}\]

    is completely fixed during the step \(m \to 2m\)! It contains only \(\sum_{j=2}^{2m-1} \frac{2m}{j} = O(m \log m)\) nonzero terms and can be accumulated in \(O(m)\) time by iterating over \((k, j)\) with \(j \ge 2\) and \(j k < 2m\).

  3. Univariate Newton Step: Treating \(S_{\ge 2}(x) \bmod x^{2m}\) as a known polynomial, let \(W(x) = \exp\!\big(F_m(x) + S_{\ge 2}(x)\big) \bmod x^{2m}\). Differentiating \(\Phi(F)\) with respect to \(F(x)\) gives \(\Phi'(F_m) = 2 - W(x)\). Since \([x^0]W(x) = e^0 = 1\), the constant term of \(\Phi'(F_m)\) is \(2 - 1 = 1 \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\). Thus Newton’s iteration gives

    \[F_{2m}(x) = F_m(x) - \frac{2 F_m(x) - x + 1 - W(x)}{2 - W(x)} \pmod{x^{2m}}.\]

Verification on Hand Trace (\(N = 4\))

  • Start with \(F_1(x) = 0 \bmod x^1\).

  • \(m = 1 \to 2\): \(S_{\ge 2}(x) = 0 \bmod x^2\), \(W(x) = 1 \bmod x^2 \implies F_2(x) = 0 - \frac{-x}{1} = x \bmod x^2\) (\(f_1 = 1\)).

  • \(m = 2 \to 4\): With \(F_2(x) = x\), \(S_{\ge 2}(x) = \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} \bmod x^4\). Then \(F_2(x) + S_{\ge 2}(x) = x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3\), so

    \[W(x) = \exp\!\left(x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3\right) \equiv 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\]

    Then \(2 F_2(x) - x + 1 - W(x) = 2x - x + 1 - (1 + x + x^2 + x^3) = -x^2 - x^3\), and \(2 - W(x) = 1 - x - x^2 - x^3\). Dividing gives

    \[F_4(x) = x - \frac{-x^2 - x^3}{1 - x - x^2 - x^3} \equiv x + \frac{x^2 + x^3}{1 - x} \equiv x + x^2 + 2 x^3 \pmod{x^4},\]

    giving \(f_1 = 1, f_2 = 1, f_3 = 2\). One more step yields \(f_4 = 5\). Exact match!

Intermediate 2 (#219) — Strengthened Multi-Type Trees (LibreOJ 3398: 带加强和多项木)

  • Source: LibreOJ 3398 — Strengthened Multi-Type Rooted Trees
  • Prerequisite Tags: 2x2 Multivariate Good–Lagrange Inversion (Form 1), Logarithmic Jacobian Determinant, Multi-Type Arborescences

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Consider rooted ordered multi-type trees with two vertex colors (\(1\) and \(2\)). A vertex of color \(1\) chooses its children according to the bivariate weight series \(\Phi_1(u_1, u_2) = (1 - u_1)^{-a_1} (1 - u_2)^{-b_1}\), and a vertex of color \(2\) chooses its children according to \(\Phi_2(u_1, u_2) = (1 - u_1)^{-a_2} (1 - u_2)^{-b_2}\), so the generating series \(u_1(x_1, x_2)\) and \(u_2(x_1, x_2)\) satisfy

\[u_1 = x_1 (1 - u_1)^{-a_1} (1 - u_2)^{-b_1}, \qquad u_2 = x_2 (1 - u_1)^{-a_2} (1 - u_2)^{-b_2}.\]

Given \(n_1, n_2 \ge 1\), exponent parameters \(a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2 \ge 1\), and root forest multiplicities \((r_1, r_2)\), compute \([x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] \big(u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2}\big) \bmod 998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le n_1, n_2 \le 10^5\), \(0 \le r_1 \le n_1, 0 \le r_2 \le n_2\), \(r_1 + r_2 \ge 1\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(n_1 + n_2)\) (for factored \((1-u_1)^{-a_i}(1-u_2)^{-b_i}\)) or \(O((n_1 + n_2)\log(n_1 + n_2))\) time and \(O(n_1 + n_2)\) space.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n_1 = 1, n_2 = 1, a_1 = b_1 = a_2 = b_2 = 1, r_1 = 1, r_2 = 0\))

Here \(\Phi_1(u_1, u_2) = \Phi_2(u_1, u_2) = \frac{1}{(1-u_1)(1-u_2)}\). We want the number of ordered trees rooted at color \(1\) (\(r_1 = 1, r_2 = 0\)) with \(n_1 = 1\) vertex of color \(1\) and \(n_2 = 1\) vertex of color \(2\):

  • Since the root has color \(1\) and there is only \(1\) vertex of color \(1\), the single color-\(2\) vertex must be a child of the root.
  • In \(\Phi_1(u_1, u_2) = (1 + u_1 + \dots)(1 + u_2 + \dots)\), choosing \(0\) children of color \(1\) and \(1\) child of color \(2\) has weight \(1 \cdot 1 = 1\), and that color-\(2\) leaf chooses \(0\) children of both colors with weight \(1\).
  • Thus the exact coefficient \([x_1^1 x_2^1] u_1\) is \(1\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Here both \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) appear inside both \(\Phi_1(u_1, u_2)\) and \(\Phi_2(u_1, u_2)\), so the diagonal entries of the \(2 \times 2\) Good–Lagrange matrix \(J(u_1, u_2)\) are nonzero! This is the canonical diagnostic signal for the full \(2 \times 2\) Multivariate Good–Lagrange Inversion (Theorem 17.1, Form 1).

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Write down the \(2 \times 2\) Good–Lagrange logarithmic Jacobian \(J(u_1, u_2)\) for \(\Phi_i(u_1, u_2) = (1 - u_1)^{-a_i} (1 - u_2)^{-b_i}\): \[\frac{u_1}{\Phi_i}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_1} = \frac{a_i u_1}{1 - u_1}, \qquad \frac{u_2}{\Phi_i}\frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_2} = \frac{b_i u_2}{1 - u_2}.\] Therefore, \[I_2 - J(u_1, u_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \dfrac{a_1 u_1}{1 - u_1} & -\dfrac{b_1 u_2}{1 - u_2} \\[10pt] -\dfrac{a_2 u_1}{1 - u_1} & 1 - \dfrac{b_2 u_2}{1 - u_2} \end{pmatrix}.\] Compute its determinant \(\det(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2))\) and multiply by \(u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2} \Phi_1(u_1, u_2)^{n_1} \Phi_2(u_1, u_2)^{n_2}\)!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Notice the algebraic simplification when you expand \(\det(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2))\): \[\det(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2)) = \left(1 - \frac{a_1 u_1}{1 - u_1}\right)\left(1 - \frac{b_2 u_2}{1 - u_2}\right) - \frac{a_2 b_1 u_1 u_2}{(1 - u_1)(1 - u_2)} = \frac{(1 - (a_1+1)u_1)(1 - (b_2+1)u_2) - a_2 b_1 u_1 u_2}{(1 - u_1)(1 - u_2)}.\] Because \(\Phi_1^{n_1} \Phi_2^{n_2} = (1 - u_1)^{-(a_1 n_1 + a_2 n_2)} (1 - u_2)^{-(b_1 n_1 + b_2 n_2)}\) is a product of a 1D function of \(u_1\) and a 1D function of \(u_2\), and the numerator of \(\det(I_2 - J)\) has only \(4\) monomials in \((u_1, u_2)\), the coefficient \([u_1^{n_1} u_2^{n_2}]\) evaluates in \(O(1)\) binomial evaluations! (And for general separable \(\Phi_i(u_1, u_2) = A_i(u_1) B_i(u_2)\), it takes \(O((n_1 + n_2)\log(n_1 + n_2))\) via 1D polynomial powers!)

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Applying \(2 \times 2\) Good–Lagrange Form 1 (Theorem 17.1): Since \(\Phi_1(0, 0) = \Phi_2(0, 0) = 1 \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\), Form 1 of Theorem 17.1 with \(H(u_1, u_2) = u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2}\) gives \[[x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}] \big(u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2}\big) = [u_1^{n_1 - r_1} u_2^{n_2 - r_2}] \left( \det\!\big(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2)\big) \Phi_1(u_1, u_2)^{n_1} \Phi_2(u_1, u_2)^{n_2} \right).\]

  2. Evaluating \(\det(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2))\): For any separable branching laws \(\Phi_i(u_1, u_2) = A_i(u_1) B_i(u_2)\) (\(i = 1, 2\)), the logarithmic derivatives separate completely: \[J_{i, 1}(u_1, u_2) = \frac{u_1 A_i'(u_1)}{A_i(u_1)}, \qquad J_{i, 2}(u_1, u_2) = \frac{u_2 B_i'(u_2)}{B_i(u_2)}.\] Therefore, expanding the \(2 \times 2\) determinant \(\det(I_2 - J(u_1, u_2))\) yields: \[\det(I_2 - J) = \left(1 - \frac{u_1 A_1'(u_1)}{A_1(u_1)}\right)\left(1 - \frac{u_2 B_2'(u_2)}{B_2(u_2)}\right) - \left(\frac{u_1 A_2'(u_1)}{A_2(u_1)}\right)\left(\frac{u_2 B_1'(u_2)}{B_1(u_2)}\right).\] Notice the combinatorial meaning of the two pieces:

    • The diagonal product \(\big(1 - \frac{u_1 A_1'}{A_1}\big)\big(1 - \frac{u_2 B_2'}{B_2}\big)\) cancels monochromatic \(1 \to 1\) cycles and monochromatic \(2 \to 2\) cycles.
    • The off-diagonal term \(-\big(\frac{u_1 A_2'}{A_2}\big)\big(\frac{u_2 B_1'}{B_1}\big)\) cancels alternating 2-color cycles \(1 \to 2 \to 1\)!

  3. Specializing to \(A_i(u_1) = (1 - u_1)^{-a_i}\) and \(B_i(u_2) = (1 - u_2)^{-b_i}\): Let \(A = a_1 n_1 + a_2 n_2\) and \(B = b_1 n_1 + b_2 n_2\). Then \[\Phi_1(u_1, u_2)^{n_1} \Phi_2(u_1, u_2)^{n_2} = (1 - u_1)^{-A} (1 - u_2)^{-B},\] and \[\det(I_2 - J) = \frac{\big(1 - (a_1 + 1) u_1\big)\big(1 - (b_2 + 1) u_2\big) - a_2 b_1 u_1 u_2}{(1 - u_1)(1 - u_2)} = \frac{1 - (a_1+1)u_1 - (b_2+1)u_2 + \big((a_1+1)(b_2+1) - a_2 b_1\big) u_1 u_2}{(1 - u_1)(1 - u_2)}.\] Combining the denominator \((1 - u_1)(1 - u_2)\) with \((1 - u_1)^{-A} (1 - u_2)^{-B}\) gives \((1 - u_1)^{-(A+1)} (1 - u_2)^{-(B+1)}\). Setting \(k_1 = n_1 - r_1\) and \(k_2 = n_2 - r_2\), and using \([u^k](1 - u)^{-(M+1)} = \binom{M + k}{k}\) (with \(\binom{M - 1}{-1} = 0\)), we obtain the closed-form formula: \[\begin{aligned} [x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2}]\big(u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2}\big) &= \binom{A + k_1}{k_1}\binom{B + k_2}{k_2} - (a_1 + 1)\binom{A + k_1 - 1}{k_1 - 1}\binom{B + k_2}{k_2} \\ &\quad - (b_2 + 1)\binom{A + k_1}{k_1}\binom{B + k_2 - 1}{k_2 - 1} + \big((a_1 + 1)(b_2 + 1) - a_2 b_1\big)\binom{A + k_1 - 1}{k_1 - 1}\binom{B + k_2 - 1}{k_2 - 1}. \end{aligned}\]

Verification on Hand Trace (\(n_1 = 1, n_2 = 1, a_i = b_i = 1, r_1 = 1, r_2 = 0\))

Here \(A = 2, B = 2\), \(k_1 = 1 - 1 = 0\), \(k_2 = 1 - 0 = 1\). Since \(k_1 - 1 = -1\), all terms with \(\binom{A + k_1 - 1}{k_1 - 1}\) vanish, leaving: \[\binom{2 + 0}{0}\binom{2 + 1}{1} - (1 + 1)\binom{2 + 0}{0}\binom{2 + 0}{0} = 1 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1.\] Exact match!

Advanced 1 (#015) — 12 Directions (AtCoder fps_24_v)

  • Source: AtCoder FPS 24 Problems — Problem V: 12 Directions
  • Prerequisite Tags: 2D Lattice Walk Laurent Factorization, Coordinate Change Decoupling, Single-Pass Convolution / D-Finite Extraction

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Consider a 2D lattice walk on \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) starting at \((0, 0)\). In each step, the walker takes one cardinal step \((dx_1, dy_1) \in \{(+1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, +1), (0, -1)\}\) followed by one diagonal-or-stay step \((dx_2, dy_2) \in \{(+1, +1), (0, 0), (-1, -1)\}\), yielding \(4 \times 3 = 12\) weighted transitions \((dx, dy) = (dx_1 + dx_2, \, dy_1 + dy_2)\):

\[\mathcal{S}_{12} = \Big\{ (+2, +1)^{\times 1},\; (+1, +2)^{\times 1},\; (-2, -1)^{\times 1},\; (-1, -2)^{\times 1},\; (+1, 0)^{\times 2},\; (-1, 0)^{\times 2},\; (0, +1)^{\times 2},\; (0, -1)^{\times 2} \Big\},\]

governed by the 12-term Laurent step polynomial

\[S(x, y) = \left(x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\right)\left(x y + 1 + x^{-1} y^{-1}\right).\]

Given \(N\) and target coordinates \((X, Y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\), compute \([x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^N \bmod 998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 2.5 \times 10^5\), \(|X|, |Y| \le 2 N\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time (via 1D polynomial convolution) or \(O(N)\) time (via D-finite recurrence) and \(O(N)\) space.

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 1, (X, Y) = (2, 1)\) and \((X, Y) = (1, 0)\))

For \(N = 1\), \(S(x, y) = (x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1})(x y + 1 + x^{-1} y^{-1})\):

  • For \((X, Y) = (2, 1)\): Only the product \(x \cdot (x y) = x^2 y^1\) reaches \((2, 1)\), so \([x^2 y^1] S(x, y)^1 = 1\).
  • For \((X, Y) = (1, 0)\): Both \(x \cdot 1 = x\) and \(y^{-1} \cdot (x y) = x\) reach \((1, 0)\), so \([x^1 y^0] S(x, y)^1 = 2\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Whenever a 2D unrestricted lattice walk has a step set of size \(8\) or \(12\) that looks asymmetric (containing steps like \((\pm 2, \pm 1)\) and \((\pm 1, \pm 2)\) alongside \((\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\)), check whether the bivariate Laurent polynomial \(S(x, y)\) factors into simpler factors after rotating coordinates to \(u = x y\) and \(v = x / y\)!

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Recall the classic 4-step trick from Chapter 15: the simple 2D walk polynomial \(x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\) factors into diagonal coordinates:

\[x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} = x^{-1} y^{-1} (1 + x y)(x + y) = (x y)^{-1/2} (x / y)^{-1/2} (1 + x y)\left(1 + \frac{x}{y}\right).\]

Now look at the second factor of \(S(x, y)\): it is \(x y + 1 + x^{-1} y^{-1}\), which depends only on \(u = x y\) and is completely independent of \(v = x / y\)! Substitute \(u = x y\) and \(v = x / y\) (so \(x = u^{1/2} v^{1/2}\) and \(y = u^{1/2} v^{-1/2}\), meaning \(x^X y^Y = u^{(X+Y)/2} v^{(X-Y)/2}\)):

\[S(x, y)^N = \underbrace{v^{-N/2} (1 + v)^N}_{\text{depends ONLY on } v} \cdot \underbrace{u^{-N/2} (1 + u)^N \big(u + 1 + u^{-1}\big)^N}_{\text{depends ONLY on } u}.\]

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Because \(S(x, y)^N\) decouples completely into a function of \(v = x/y\) times a function of \(u = x y\):

  1. Check parity: \(N + X - Y\) and \(N + X + Y\) must both be even integers (since \(x + y \equiv 1 \pmod 2\) for each step in \(x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\), while \(x y + 1 + x^{-1} y^{-1}\) changes \(x + y\) by \(\pm 2\) or \(0\)). Let \(K_v = \frac{N + X - Y}{2}\) and \(K_u = \frac{N + X + Y}{2}\). If \(N + X - Y\) is odd, or \(K_v \notin [0, N]\), or \(K_u \notin [-N, 2N]\), return \(0\).
  2. The \(v\)-factor is a single binomial coefficient: \([v^{(X-Y)/2}] v^{-N/2}(1+v)^N = [v^{K_v}](1+v)^N = \binom{N}{K_v}\)!
  3. The \(u\)-factor is \([u^{K_u}] (1 + u)^N (u^{-1} + 1 + u)^N = [u^{K_u + N}] (1 + u)^N (1 + u + u^2)^N\). Since \((1+u)^N (1+u+u^2)^N = (1 + u)^{N} \sum_{j=0}^N \binom{N}{j} u^j (1+u)^j = \sum_{j=0}^N \binom{N}{j} u^j (1 + u)^{N + j}\), we can evaluate \([u^{K_u + N}]\) as a single \(O(N)\) sum without even needing an NTT!
NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Diagonal Coordinate Transformation (\(u = x y, v = x y^{-1}\)): Write the first factor of \(S(x, y)\) in terms of \(u = x y\) and \(v = x y^{-1}\):

    \[x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} = \frac{x^2 y + y + x y^2 + x}{x y} = \frac{(1 + x y)(x + y)}{x y} = u^{-1/2} v^{-1/2} (1 + u)(1 + v).\]

    Since the second factor is \(x y + 1 + x^{-1} y^{-1} = u + 1 + u^{-1} = u^{-1}(1 + u + u^2)\), raising \(S(x, y)\) to the \(N\)-th power separates the variables \(u\) and \(v\) completely:

    \[S(x, y)^N = v^{-N/2} (1 + v)^N \cdot u^{-3N/2} (1 + u)^N (1 + u + u^2)^N.\]

  2. Extracting \([x^X y^Y]\) via \((u, v)\): Any monomial \(u^{a - 3N/2} v^{b - N/2}\) in the expansion of \(S(x, y)^N\) equals

    \[(x y)^{a - 3N/2} (x y^{-1})^{b - N/2} = x^{a + b - 2N} y^{a - b - N}.\]

    Setting the exponents equal to \((X, Y)\) yields the unique integer indices \((a, b)\):

    \[\begin{cases} a + b - 2N = X \\ a - b - N = Y \end{cases} \iff \begin{cases} a = \dfrac{3N + X + Y}{2}, \\[6pt] b = \dfrac{N + X - Y}{2}. \end{cases}\]

    If \(N + X - Y\) is odd, or \(b \notin [0, N]\), or \(a \notin [0, 3N]\), the coefficient is \(0\). Otherwise:

    \[[x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^N = \Big([v^b] (1 + v)^N\Big) \cdot \Big([u^a] (1 + u)^N (1 + u + u^2)^N\Big).\]

  3. \(O(N)\) Closed-Form Sum for the \(u\)-Factor: The first factor is immediately \([v^b](1+v)^N = \binom{N}{b}\). For the second factor, group \(1 + u + u^2 = (1 + u)^2 - u\) (or \(1 + u(1 + u)\)) and expand by the binomial theorem:

    \[(1 + u)^N \big(1 + u(1 + u)\big)^N = (1 + u)^N \sum_{j=0}^N \binom{N}{j} u^j (1 + u)^j = \sum_{j=0}^N \binom{N}{j} u^j (1 + u)^{N + j}.\]

    Extracting \([u^a]\) from each summand gives \([u^{a-j}](1 + u)^{N+j} = \binom{N + j}{a - j}\). Therefore:

    \[[x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^N = \binom{N}{b} \sum_{j = \max(0, \lceil (a - N)/2 \rceil)}^{\min(N, a)} \binom{N}{j} \binom{N + j}{a - j} \pmod{998244353}.\]

Verification on Hand Trace (\(N = 1\))

  • For \((X, Y) = (2, 1)\): \(b = \frac{1 + 2 - 1}{2} = 1\), \(a = \frac{3 + 2 + 1}{2} = 3\). Then \(\binom{1}{1} \sum_{j=1}^1 \binom{1}{1}\binom{2}{2} = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1\).
  • For \((X, Y) = (1, 0)\): \(b = \frac{1 + 1 - 0}{2} = 1\), \(a = \frac{3 + 1 + 0}{2} = 2\). Then \(\binom{1}{1} \big[ \binom{1}{0}\binom{1}{2} + \binom{1}{1}\binom{2}{1} \big] = 1 \cdot (0 + 2) = 2\). Exact match!

Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Preconditions: Step count \(N \le 2.5 \times 10^5\), target coordinates \((X, Y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\), and prime modulus \(p = 998244353 > 2N\).
  • Postconditions (Output): The number of \(N\)-step 12-direction walks \([x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^N \bmod 998244353\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space (using precomputed factorials up to \(2N\)).
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: Under the diagonal coordinate change \(u = x y\) and \(v = x y^{-1}\), \(S(x, y)^N\) separates into \(v^{-N/2}(1+v)^N \cdot u^{-3N/2}(1+u)^N(1+u+u^2)^N\), and the \(j\)-th summand \(\binom{N}{j}\binom{N+j}{a-j}\) extracts \([u^a]\) from \(\binom{N}{j} u^j (1+u)^{N+j}\).

Advanced 2 (#204) — Coupled Bipartite Tree Enumeration (Luogu P7599: 数树)

  • Source: Luogu P7599 (2021 CoE-II E) — Bipartite Coupled Tree Jump & Enumeration
  • Prerequisite Tags: Bipartite Rank-1 Good–Lagrange Collapse (Corollary 17.1), Decoupled 1D Polynomial Powers, Cartesian / Alternating Tree Decomposition

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Consider rooted alternating (bipartite) trees with \(n_1\) left-type (color \(1\)) vertices and \(n_2\) right-type (color \(2\)) vertices resulting from the Cartesian jump / two-type branching decomposition:

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

A particle starts at \((0, 0)\) in the 2D quarter plane \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2 = \{(i, j) : i \ge 0, j \ge 0\}\). In each step, it moves by one of the three Tandem steps:

\[\mathcal{S} = \big\{ (+1, 0), \, (-1, +1), \, (0, -1) \big\},\]

subject to the condition that its coordinates \((i, j)\) remain non-negative (\(i \ge 0\) and \(j \ge 0\)) after every step. Given \(N \le 10^6\) and target endpoint \((i, j) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\), compute the number of valid \(N\)-step quarter-plane walks ending at \((i, j)\) modulo \(998244353\) (and state the closed form for excursions returning to \((i, j) = (0, 0)\) when \(N = 3m\)).

Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 3, (i, j) = (0, 0)\) and \(N = 3, (i, j) = (1, 1)\))

For \(N = 3\):

  • Returning to \((0, 0)\) (\(m = 1\)): Any walk returning to \((0, 0)\) must use each of \(A = (+1, 0)\), \(B = (-1, +1)\), and \(C = (0, -1)\) once.   • Ending at \((i, j) = (1, 1)\) in \(N = 3\) steps: Net displacement \((+1, +1)\) requires two \(A = (+1, 0)\) steps and one \(B = (-1, +1)\) step. Since \(A\) must precede \(B\) so \(i\) stays \(\ge 0\), the valid sequences are \(A B A\) and \(A A B\), giving \(q(1, 1; 3) = 2\)!

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Unrestricted Trinomial Coefficient Formula: By the multinomial theorem,

    \[S(x, y)^N = \left(x + \frac{y}{x} + \frac{1}{y}\right)^N = \sum_{\substack{a, b, c \ge 0 \\ a + b + c = N}} \frac{N!}{a!\,b!\,c!} x^{a - b} y^{b - c}.\]

    \[W(u, v; N) := [x^u y^v] S(x, y)^N = \binom{N}{\frac{N + 2u + v}{3}, \, \frac{N - u + v}{3}, \, \frac{N - u - 2v}{3}}.\]

    \[q(i, j; N) = [x^{i+1} y^{j+1}] \left( x y - \frac{y^2}{x} + \frac{y}{x^2} - \frac{1}{x y} + \frac{x}{y^2} - \frac{x^2}{y} \right) S(x, y)^N.\]

Then \(\det\!\big(I_3 - J(\mathbf{u})\big) = \det M(\mathbf{u}) \cdot \prod_{i=1}^3 \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{-1}\), so Good–Lagrange Form 1 simplifies to the division-free polynomial-determinant form:

\[[x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2} x_3^{n_3}] \big(u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2} u_3^{r_3}\big) = [u_1^{n_1 - r_1} u_2^{n_2 - r_2} u_3^{n_3 - r_3}] \left( \det M(\mathbf{u}) \prod_{i=1}^3 \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i - 1} \right).\]

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Let \(k_i = n_i - r_i\). We work in the truncated 3D polynomial ring \(R = \mathbb{F}_p[u_1, u_2, u_3]/(u_1^{k_1+1}, u_2^{k_2+1}, u_3^{k_3+1})\) using the \(\chi\)-twisted Kronecker FFT from Chapter 16 (§16.3, Theorem 16.1), where a mixed-radix index \(I = e_1 + (k_1+1)e_2 + (k_1+1)(k_2+1)e_3\) has the floor-quotient grading \(\chi(I) = \big(\lfloor I/(k_1+1) \rfloor + \lfloor I/((k_1+1)(k_2+1)) \rfloor\big) \bmod 3 = \big(e_2 + (k_2 + 2)e_3\big) \bmod 3\):

  4. Extract \([u_1^{k_1} u_2^{k_2} u_3^{k_3}] \big(\det M(\mathbf{u}) \cdot P(\mathbf{u})\big)\) in \(O(V)\) time by reading a single inner product of complementary coefficients without even needing a final convolution!

  1. Clearing Denominators in the \(3 \times 3\) Good–Lagrange Determinant: By Theorem 17.1 (Form 1) with \(d = 3\) and \(H(\mathbf{u}) = u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2} u_3^{r_3}\), setting \(k_i = n_i - r_i \ge 0\) gives

    \[[x_1^{n_1} x_2^{n_2} x_3^{n_3}] \big(u_1^{r_1} u_2^{r_2} u_3^{r_3}\big) = [u_1^{k_1} u_2^{k_2} u_3^{k_3}] \left( \det M(\mathbf{u}) \prod_{i=1}^3 \Phi_i(\mathbf{u})^{n_i - 1} \right),\]

    where \(M_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \delta_{i,j} \Phi_i(\mathbf{u}) - u_j \frac{\partial \Phi_i}{\partial u_j}\). Notice that \(M_{i,j}(\mathbf{u})\) requires zero polynomial divisions—for each monomial \(c \, u_1^{e_1} u_2^{e_2} u_3^{e_3}\) in \(\Phi_i(\mathbf{u})\), the operator \(u_j \frac{\partial}{\partial u_j}\) simply multiplies its coefficient by \(e_j\)!     \[[u_1^{e_1} u_2^{e_2} u_3^{e_3}] M_{i,j}(\mathbf{u}) = \big(\delta_{i,j} - e_j\big) [u_1^{e_1} u_2^{e_2} u_3^{e_3}] \Phi_i(\mathbf{u}).\]

    \[\begin{aligned} \det M(\mathbf{u}) &= M_{1,1}\big(M_{2,2} M_{3,3} - M_{2,3} M_{3,2}\big) - M_{1,2}\big(M_{2,1} M_{3,3} - M_{2,3} M_{3,1}\big) \\ &\quad + M_{1,3}\big(M_{2,1} M_{3,2} - M_{2,2} M_{3,1}\big) \pmod{(u_1^{k_1+1}, u_2^{k_2+1}, u_3^{k_3+1})}. \end{aligned}\]

    \[L(\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{i=1}^3 (n_i - 1) \ln \tilde{\Phi}_i(\mathbf{u}) \in R,\]

    \[\operatorname{Ans} = \sum_{e_1=0}^{k_1} \sum_{e_2=0}^{k_2} \sum_{e_3=0}^{k_3} \Big([u_1^{e_1} u_2^{e_2} u_3^{e_3}] \det M(\mathbf{u})\Big) \Big([u_1^{k_1 - e_1} u_2^{k_2 - e_2} u_3^{k_3 - e_3}] P(\mathbf{u})\Big) \pmod{998244353}.\]
