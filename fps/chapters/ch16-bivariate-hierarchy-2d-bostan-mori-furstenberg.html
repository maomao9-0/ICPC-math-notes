When a combinatorial problem tracks two or more independent statistics—such as length and weight, red vertices and blue vertices, or horizontal and vertical displacement on a grid—our generating functions acquire multiple formal variables. In one variable, a single hierarchy \(K[x] \subset K[[x]] \subset K((x))\) suffices for nearly every operation. In two or more variables, however, the order in which you allow infinite sums and negative exponents changes the algebraic meaning of a rational function.

In this chapter, we first build a precise map of the five bivariate rings where multi-parameter series live (§16.1). We then develop the four algorithmic regimes of Bivariate Bostan–Mori for extracting \([x^N y^k] P(x, y)/Q(x, y)\) (§16.2), introduce Twisted Kronecker Substitution and the Radial Euler Derivation to compute \(d\)-dimensional FPS operations in \(O(d N \log N)\) without \(2^d\) zero-padding blowup (§16.3), and prove Furstenberg’s Diagonal Theorem connecting 2D rational diagonals to 1D algebraic series alongside Failure Mode #15 and Lipshitz’s Theorem for 3D+ diagonals (§16.4).

16.1 The 5-Ring Bivariate Hierarchy & Where Each Series Lives

Suppose you are counting lattice walks or multi-parameter structures with two formal variables \(x\) and \(y\), and your derivation produces the simple rational expression

\[F(x, y) = \frac{1}{x - y}.\]

What is the coefficient \([x^{-1} y^0] F(x, y)\)? As written, the question is ill-posed until you specify which ring you are expanding \(F(x, y)\) in:

  • If you treat \(x\) as the primary Laurent variable and expand in powers of \(y/x\), you write \[\frac{1}{x - y} = \frac{1}{x(1 - y/x)} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^{-k-1} y^k \in K((x))[[y]],\] where the coefficient of \(x^{-1} y^0\) is \(1\), and every term has a negative power of \(x\) and a non-negative power of \(y\).
  • If instead you treat \(y\) as the primary Laurent variable and expand in powers of \(x/y\), you write \[\frac{1}{x - y} = -\frac{1}{y(1 - x/y)} = -\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n y^{-n-1} \in K((y))[[x]],\] where the coefficient of \(x^{-1} y^0\) is \(0\), and every term has a non-negative power of \(x\) and a negative power of \(y\).

If you subtract these two expansions inside the module \(K[[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]]\) of doubly-infinite formal series, they do not cancel to zero; their difference is the formal bilateral delta series \(\sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} x^{-k-1} y^k \neq 0\). Thus, before dividing, substituting, or extracting a residue in two variables, we must know exactly which bivariate ring we are working in.

The Five Canonical Bivariate Rings

Let \(K\) be a commutative ring with identity (in competitive programming, typically \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) for an NTT prime \(p = 998244353\)). We organize bivariate series into five distinct rings according to which exponents are bounded below and which coefficients are finite polynomials:

  1. The Symmetric Bivariate Formal Power Series Ring \(K[[x, y]]\): An element \(F(x, y) = \sum_{n \ge 0, k \ge 0} f_{n, k} x^n y^k \in K[[x, y]]\) is an arbitrary infinite array of coefficients indexed by pairs of non-negative integers \((n, k) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2\). Addition is componentwise, and multiplication is the finite Cauchy product \[[x^n y^k](F \cdot G) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \sum_{j=0}^{k} f_{i, j} g_{n-i, k-j}.\]

    • Unit Condition: \(F(x, y) \in K[[x, y]]^\times\) is invertible in \(K[[x, y]]\) if and only if its constant term is a unit in \(K\): \([x^0 y^0] F(x, y) = f_{0, 0} \in K^\times\).
    • Admissible Infinite Sums: A family \(\{H_m(x, y)\}_{m \ge 0}\) in \(K[[x, y]]\) is summable if and only if its total valuation \(\operatorname{val}_{(x, y)}(H_m) := \min\{n + k : [x^n y^k] H_m \neq 0\}\) tends to \(+\infty\) as \(m \to \infty\).

  2. The Polynomial-Coefficient Power Series Ring \(K[y][[x]]\): An element \(F(x, y) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} P_n(y) x^n \in K[y][[x]]\) is a formal power series in \(x\) whose \(n\)-th coefficient \(P_n(y) = [x^n] F(x, y) \in K[y]\) is a finite-degree polynomial in \(y\) for every \(n \ge 0\).

    • Combinatorial Meaning: \(x\) marks the “size” or “step count” of an object, and an object of size \(n\) can have at most finitely many (say \(\le c n\)) secondary features marked by \(y\).
    • Strict Inclusion \(K[x, y] \subsetneq K[y][[x]] \subsetneq K[[x, y]]\): Consider the geometric series \(A(x, y) = \frac{1}{1 - x y} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} y^n x^n\). For each fixed \(n\), the coefficient of \(x^n\) is the monomial \(y^n \in K[y]\) of degree \(n\), and for each fixed \(k\), the coefficient of \(y^k\) is \(x^k \in K[x]\). Thus \[\frac{1}{1 - x y} \in \big(K[y][[x]] \cap K[x][[y]]\big) \setminus K[x, y].\] By contrast, consider the binomial generating function \(B(x, y) = \frac{1}{1 - x - y} = \sum_{n, k \ge 0} \binom{n+k}{k} x^n y^k\). If we group by powers of \(x\), we obtain \[\frac{1}{1 - x - y} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{n+k}{k} y^k\right) x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{(1 - y)^{n+1}}.\] Here \([x^n] B(x, y) = (1 - y)^{-(n+1)} \in K[[y]] \setminus K[y]\) has infinitely many nonzero powers of \(y\), whereas \([x^n] \left(\frac{1}{1 - x - y}\right)\) viewed in \(K[x][[y]]\) has coefficient \([y^k] B(x, y) = (1 - x)^{-(k+1)} \notin K[x]\). However, if we rewrite \(\frac{1}{1 - x(1 + y)} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (1+y)^n x^n\), then \([x^n] = (1+y)^n \in K[y]\) has degree \(n\), so \(\frac{1}{1 - x(1+y)} \in K[y][[x]] \setminus K[x][[y]]\).
    • Unit Condition: Because the units of the polynomial ring \(K[y]\) over a domain (or field) \(K\) are purely the nonzero constants \(K^\times\), a series \(F(x, y) = \sum_{n \ge 0} P_n(y) x^n \in K[y][[x]]\) is invertible in \(K[y][[x]]\) if and only if \(P_0(y) \in K^\times\) is a nonzero constant independent of \(y\). For example, \(1 - y - x\) is invertible in \(K[[x, y]]\) (since \(f_{0,0} = 1\)), but its inverse \(\sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{x^n}{(1-y)^{n+1}}\) does not belong to \(K[y][[x]]\) because \(P_0(y) = 1 - y \notin K^\times\).

  3. The Laurent-Polynomial-Coefficient Ring \((K[y, y^{-1}])[[x]]\): An element \(F(x, y) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} L_n(y) x^n \in (K[y, y^{-1}])[[x]]\) has a finite Laurent polynomial \(L_n(y) = \sum_{k = -M_n}^{M_n} f_{n, k} y^k \in K[y, y^{-1}]\) at each step \(n \ge 0\).

    • Combinatorial Meaning: \(x\) marks time steps \(n \ge 0\) and \(y\) marks a signed 1D displacement \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\) (such as a random walk with step generator \(S(y) = y + y^{-1}\), where after \(n\) steps the displacement lies in \([-n, n]\)).
    • Unit Condition: Since the units of \(K[y, y^{-1}]\) over a field \(K\) are the single-term Laurent monomials \(c y^m\) (\(c \in K^\times, m \in \mathbb{Z}\)), \(F(x, y) \in (K[y, y^{-1}])[[x]]^\times\) if and only if \([x^0] F(x, y) = c y^m\) for some \(c \in K^\times\) and \(m \in \mathbb{Z}\).

  4. The Iterated Formal Laurent Rings \(K((x))[[y]]\) and \(K((y))[[x]]\): When \(K\) is a field, \(K((x))\) is the field of formal Laurent series in \(x\), and \(K((x))[[y]]\) consists of formal power series \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} A_k(x) y^k\) whose coefficients \(A_k(x) \in K((x))\) are Laurent series in \(x\) with exponents bounded below (for each fixed \(k\), \(A_k(x) = \sum_{n \ge v_k} a_{n, k} x^n\) for some \(v_k \in \mathbb{Z}\) that is allowed to drift to \(-\infty\) as \(k \to \infty\)).

    • Unit Condition: Because \(K((x))\) is a field, \(F(x, y) = \sum_{k \ge 0} A_k(x) y^k \in K((x))[[y]]\) is a unit if and only if the leading \(y^0\)-slice \(A_0(x)\) is not the zero Laurent series (\(A_0(x) \neq 0\)). For instance, \(x - y = x(1 - x^{-1} y)\) has \(A_0(x) = x \neq 0\), so it is invertible in \(K((x))[[y]]\), even though its constant term at \((0, 0)\) is zero.

  5. The Diagonal-Extraction Ring \(K((z))[[t]]\) (and \(K[[t]]((z))\)): To extract the main diagonal \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} F(x, y) := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_{n, n} t^n\) of a bivariate series \(F(x, y) = \sum_{n, k \ge 0} f_{n, k} x^n y^k \in K[[x, y]]\), we perform the change of variables \(x = z\) and \(y = t / z\). Why is this substitution valid without creating infinite divergent sums at \(z^0\)? Group the substituted series by powers of \(t\): \[F(z, t / z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \left( \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_{n, k} z^{n - k} \right) t^k.\] For each fixed power \(t^k\), the inner sum over \(n \ge 0\) starts at \(n = 0\), so the exponent of \(z\) is \(n - k \ge -k\). Thus, the coefficient of \(t^k\) is a well-defined formal Laurent series in \(K((z))\) with valuation \(\ge -k\). Consequently, \(F(z, t/z)\) lives rigorously inside \(K((z))[[t]]\), and its constant term in \(z\) is simply \[\operatorname{CT}_z F(z, t / z) := [z^0] F(z, t / z) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} f_{k, k} t^k = \operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} F(x, y) \in K[[t]].\]

The Primary-Variable Selection Rule (主元法)

In competitive programming, a bivariate coefficient extraction \([x^n y^m] F(x, y)\) is almost never solved by expanding \(x\) and \(y\) simultaneously. Instead, because \([x^n y^m] F(x, y) = [x^n]\big([y^m] F(x, y)\big) = [y^m]\big([x^n] F(x, y)\big)\) inside \(K[[x, y]]\), we are free to choose which variable to extract first. This principle—known in the Chinese competitive programming literature as the Primary-Variable Method (主元法)—often reduces a seemingly \(O(n m)\) or \(O(n \log n)\) bivariate problem to a single \(O(\min(n, m))\) summation.

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Primary-Variable Selection (主元法)
  • Diagnostic Signal: You need a single coefficient \([x^n y^m] F(x, y)\) (or a single row \([x^n] F(x, y)\)) of a closed-form bivariate generating function \(F(x, y) = \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\) or \(\exp(C(x, y))\), and the two variables appear asymmetrically in the denominator or exponent.
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    1. Inspect \(\deg_x Q(x, y)\) versus \(\deg_y Q(x, y)\) after grouping \(Q(x, y)\) as a polynomial in \(x\) with coefficients in \(K[y]\), and vice versa: \[Q(x, y) = Q_0(x) - y Q_1(x) - y^2 Q_2(x) - \dots \quad \text{vs.} \quad Q(x, y) = \widetilde{Q}_0(y) - x \widetilde{Q}_1(y) - \dots\]
    2. Rule 1 (Linear Variable First): If \(Q(x, y) = A(x) - y B(x)\) is linear (degree \(1\)) in \(y\)—even if \(A(x)\) and \(B(x)\) have high degree \(k\) in \(x\)—extract \([y^m]\) first! By the geometric series in \(K((x))[[y]]\), \[[y^m] \frac{1}{A(x) - y B(x)} = \frac{B(x)^m}{A(x)^{m+1}},\] which collapses the bivariate extraction into a 1D extraction \([x^n] \big(B(x)^m A(x)^{-(m+1)}\big)\).
    3. Rule 2 (Small Target Exponent First): If \(n \le 10^{18}\) is huge while \(m \le 10^5\) is moderate (or vice versa), expand in the variable with the small target exponent first so that the remaining extraction is a low-degree convolution or binomial coefficient.

Worked Concrete Trace (\(N \le 4\)): Asymmetric Extraction on \(F(x, y) = \frac{1}{1 - x - y x^2 (2 - x)}\)

Suppose we want to compute \([x^4 y^2] F(x, y)\) where

\[F(x, y) = \frac{1}{1 - x - y x^2(2 - x)} \in \mathbb{Q}[[x, y]].\]

  • Attempt 1 (Extracting \([x^4]\) first — Painful): Viewed as a polynomial in \(x\), the denominator is \(Q(x, y) = 1 - x - 2y x^2 + y x^3\), which is a cubic in \(x\). Extracting \([x^n]\) directly requires solving a 3-term recurrence with polynomial coefficients in \(y\).
  • Attempt 2 (Extracting \([y^2]\) first via 主元法 — Immediate): Group the denominator by powers of \(y\): \(Q(x, y) = (1 - x) - y \cdot x^2(2 - x)\), which is linear in \(y\)! Expanding as a geometric series in \(y\) inside \(K[[x]][[y]]\) gives single-step equalities: \[\begin{aligned} F(x, y) &= \frac{1}{(1 - x) - y x^2(2 - x)} && \text{(group } 1-x \text{ as the } y^0 \text{ base)} \\ &= \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} y^m \frac{x^{2m}(2 - x)^m}{(1 - x)^{m+1}} && \text{(geometric series in } y \text{)}. \end{aligned}\] Therefore, extracting \([y^2]\) first yields the exact univariate rational function \[[y^2] F(x, y) = \frac{x^4 (2 - x)^2}{(1 - x)^3}.\] Now extracting \([x^4]\) from \(\frac{x^4(2 - x)^2}{(1 - x)^3}\) is equivalent to evaluating \([x^0] \frac{(2 - x)^2}{(1 - x)^3} = \frac{2^2}{1^3} = 4\). For general \((n, m)\), expanding \((2 - x)^m = \sum_{j=0}^m (-1)^j \binom{m}{j} 2^{m-j} x^j\) and \((1 - x)^{-(m+1)} = \sum_{r \ge 0} \binom{m+r}{m} x^r\) gives the closed-form \(O(m)\) sum \[[x^n y^m] F(x, y) = \sum_{j=0}^{m} (-1)^j \binom{m}{j} 2^{m-j} \binom{n - m - j}{m},\] which for \(n = 4, m = 2\) gives only the \(j = 0\) term \((-1)^0 \binom{2}{0} 2^2 \binom{4 - 2}{2} = 4\). This exact reduction powers our Stepping Stone 2 problem (CF 1821F — Timber, #129).

Mixed OGF/EGF Calculus & Differential Operators

When a combinatorial class has labeled vertices of size \(n\) (requiring an Exponential Generating Function factor \(\frac{x^n}{n!}\)) alongside unlabeled secondary parameters of count \(k\) such as edges, components, or ascents (requiring an Ordinary Generating Function factor \(y^k\)), we represent the family by a Mixed EGF–OGF in \(K[y][[x]]\) or \(K[[x, y]]\):

\[F(x, y) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} f_{n, k} y^k \frac{x^n}{n!}.\]

The standard differential operators act independently on each variable:

  1. Partial Differentiation (\(\partial_x\) and \(\partial_y\)): \[\partial_x \left(\frac{x^n}{n!} y^k\right) = \frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} y^k \quad (\text{shifts labeled size } n \to n-1), \qquad \partial_y \left(\frac{x^n}{n!} y^k\right) = k \frac{x^n}{n!} y^{k-1}.\]
  2. Pointing Operators (\(\Theta_x = x \partial_x\) and \(\Theta_y = y \partial_y\)): \[\Theta_x F(x, y) = \sum_{n, k \ge 0} n \, f_{n, k} y^k \frac{x^n}{n!} \quad (\text{distinguishes one labeled vertex}), \qquad \Theta_y F(x, y) = \sum_{n, k \ge 0} k \, f_{n, k} y^k \frac{x^n}{n!} \quad (\text{marks one secondary feature}).\] For instance, if \(F(x, y)\) is a probability generating function in \(y\) at fixed size \(n\), then the expected value and variance of \(k\) at size \(n\) are extracted directly from \(\left.[x^n/n!] \Theta_y F(x, y)\right|_{y=1}\) and \(\left.[x^n/n!] \Theta_y^2 F(x, y)\right|_{y=1}\).
  3. The Multivariate Exponential Formula: If \(C(x_1, \dots, x_d, y)\) is the mixed EGF/OGF of connected structures (with \([x_1^0 \cdots x_d^0] C = 0\), where \(x_1, \dots, x_d\) are EGF variables marking \(d\) distinct colors of labeled vertices and \(y\) is an OGF variable marking edges or components), then the generating function \(G = \exp(C)\) counts arbitrary sets of connected components, where the vertex sets of each color are partitioned via multinomial coefficients and the OGF weights \(y^k\) add across components: \[G(x_1, \dots, x_d, y) = \exp\!\big(C(x_1, \dots, x_d, y)\big) \iff C(x_1, \dots, x_d, y) = \ln G(x_1, \dots, x_d, y).\]

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 2\)): 2-Colored Connected Bipartite Graphs via Bivariate \(\ln\)

Let \(x\) mark labeled Red vertices and \(y\) mark labeled Blue vertices. A 2-colored bipartite graph with \(a\) Red and \(b\) Blue vertices has \(ab\) potential edges between Red and Blue, so there are \(2^{ab}\) such graphs in total. Thus the disconnected 2-colored EGF is

\[G(x, y) = \sum_{a \ge 0, b \ge 0} 2^{ab} \frac{x^a}{a!} \frac{y^b}{b!} = 1 + x + y + \frac{x^2}{2} + 2xy + \frac{y^2}{2} + x^2 y + x y^2 + 2 x^2 y^2 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + \dots\]

Let us verify the coefficients of \(C(x, y) = \ln G(x, y)\) up to \(a \le 2, b \le 2\):

  • \((a, b) = (1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\): \([x^1 y^0] C = 1\) (a single Red vertex is connected) and \([x^0 y^1] C = 1\) (a single Blue vertex is connected).

  • \((a, b) = (2, 0)\): \(2! [x^2 y^0] C = 2! \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}(1)^2\right) = 0\) (two Red vertices have \(0\) edges between them, so they cannot be connected!).

  • \((a, b) = (1, 1)\): \(1! 1! [x^1 y^1] C = 2^1 - 1 \cdot 1 = 1\) (the unique connected graph on \(1\) Red and \(1\) Blue vertex is the single edge \(K_{1, 1}\)).

  • \((a, b) = (2, 1)\) and \((2, 2)\) via Primary-Variable Expansion: Grouping \(G(x, y)\) by powers of \(y\) gives \(G(x, y) = \sum_{b \ge 0} \frac{y^b}{b!} \exp(2^b x) = e^x \big(1 + y e^x + \frac{y^2}{2} e^{3x} + \dots\big)\), so taking \(\ln G(x, y)\) in \(K[[x]][[y]]\) yields \[\ln G(x, y) = x + \ln\!\left(1 + y e^x + \frac{y^2}{2} e^{3x} + \dots\right) = x + y e^x + \frac{y^2}{2}\big(e^{3x} - e^{2x}\big) + \dots\] Reading off the coefficients of \(\frac{x^a}{a!} \frac{y^b}{b!}\) immediately gives:

    • For \(b = 1\), \(a = 2\): \(2! 1! [x^2 y^1] C = [x^2/2!] e^x = 1\) (both Red vertices must connect to the single Blue vertex, forming \(K_{2, 1}\)).
    • For \(b = 2\), \(a = 2\): \(2! 2! [x^2 y^2] C = [x^2/2!] (e^{3x} - e^{2x}) = 3^2 - 2^2 = 5\) (out of \(2^{2 \times 2} = 16\) bipartite graphs on \(K_{2, 2}\), the \(5\) connected ones are \(C_4\) plus the \(4\) spanning trees obtained by deleting one of the \(4\) edges of \(C_4\)).

Notice how expanding \(\ln G(x, y)\) in the secondary variable \(y\) first gave the entire \(b = 1\) and \(b = 2\) rows in closed form! When \(a, b, c \le 50\) are all moderate (as in UOJ 596 — 三维立体混元劲, #214), we instead compute the full 3D logarithm in \(O(A B C \log(A B C))\) time using Twisted Kronecker substitution (§16.3).

16.2 The 4 Regimes of Bivariate Bostan–Mori \([x^N]\frac{P(x,y)}{Q(x,y)}\)

In Chapter 5, we saw that if \(P(x), Q(x) \in K[x]\) with \(\deg P < \deg Q = d\) and \(Q(0) = 1\), then multiplying numerator and denominator by \(Q(-x)\) produces an even denominator \(Q(x) Q(-x) = V(x^2)\) and splits the numerator \(U(x) = P(x) Q(-x) = U_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x U_{\text{odd}}(x^2)\) by parity of \(N\):

\[[x^N] \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = \begin{cases} [x^{N/2}] \dfrac{U_{\text{even}}(x)}{V(x)}, & N \equiv 0 \pmod 2, \\[8pt] [x^{(N-1)/2}] \dfrac{U_{\text{odd}}(x)}{V(x)}, & N \equiv 1 \pmod 2. \end{cases}\]

Because this identity holds over any commutative ring \(R\), what happens when we replace the coefficient field \(K\) by a polynomial ring in a second variable \(y\)? Depending on the target exponents \((N, K)\) and the degrees \((d_x, d_y)\) of \(Q(x, y)\), four distinct algorithmic regimes emerge.

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Selecting the 2D Bostan–Mori Regime

Suppose you need to extract coefficients from a bivariate rational function \(\frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\) where \(\deg_x P < \deg_x Q = d_x\) and \(Q(0, 0) = 1\):

Regime Target Extraction Parameter Bounds Algebraic Engine Time Complexity
Regime 1: Truncated Ring \([x^N y^k]\) for \(0 \le k \le K\) \(N \le 10^{18}\), \(d_x K \le 2 \times 10^5\) BM over \(R = K[y]/(y^{K+1})\) \(O(d_x K \log(d_x K) \log N)\)
Regime 2: Root Evaluation \([x^N y^k]\) for \(0 \le k \le D_y\) \(N \le 10^{18}\), \(\deg_y([x^N] P/Q) \le D_y\) Evaluate \(y_r = c \omega_L^r\), \(L\) 1D BMs, IDFT \(O(L d_x \log d_x \log N + L \log L)\)

Regime 1: Truncated Auxiliary Ring \((K[y]/(y^{K+1}))[[x]]\)

Suppose \(N \le 10^{18}\) is a huge step count, \(d_x = \deg_x Q\) is small or moderate, and we want the coefficients \([x^N y^k] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\) for all \(0 \le k \le K\) where \(d_x K \le 2 \times 10^5\).

Set the coefficient ring to be the truncated polynomial ring \(R = K[y] / (y^{K+1})\). Since \([x^0 y^0] Q(x, y) \in K^\times\), the constant term in \(x\) is \([x^0] Q(x, y) = q_0(y) \in R^\times\) (a unit in \(K[y]/(y^{K+1})\) because its constant term \(q_0(0) \neq 0\)). We run the standard 1D Bostan–Mori loop on \(x\), treating every coefficient in \(x\) as a polynomial in \(y\) truncated modulo \(y^{K+1}\):

  1. At each of the \(\lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor + 1\) steps, \(P(x, y)\) has bidegree \((< d_x, \le K)\) and \(Q(x, y)\) has bidegree \((\le d_x, \le K)\).
  2. We compute the 2D polynomial products \(U(x, y) = P(x, y) Q(-x, y) \bmod y^{K+1}\) and \(V(x^2, y) = Q(x, y) Q(-x, y) \bmod y^{K+1}\) using Kronecker substitution \(x \mapsto z^{2K+1}, y \mapsto z\) (packing \((i, j) \mapsto i(2K+1) + j\)) and 1D NTT of length \(O(d_x K)\).
  3. After \(\lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor + 1\) steps, \(N\) reaches \(0\), and we return the single polynomial quotient \(P(0, y) Q(0, y)^{-1} \bmod y^{K+1}\) via 1D FPS inversion in \(y\).
  • Total Time Complexity: \(O(d_x K \log(d_x K) \log N)\).

Regime 2: Root-of-Unity Evaluation & Interpolation (\(y_r = c \omega_L^r\))

Suppose \([x^N] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\) is known to be a true polynomial in \(y\) of degree at most \(D_y\) (this occurs whenever \([x^0] Q(x, y) = 1\), so that \(\frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)} \in K[y][[x]]\), e.g., when \(Q(x, y) = \det(I - x A(y))\) for a weighted graph adjacency matrix \(A(y)\)), or suppose we first reduce the problem modulo \(y^{K+1}\) so that only \(y\)-degrees \(\le D_y\) occur during the multiplication.

Instead of multiplying 2D polynomials at every bit of \(N\) (which incurs a factor of \(\log(d_x L)\) inside the \(\log N\) loop), we decouple \(y\) before starting Bostan–Mori:

  1. Choose a power-of-two NTT length \(L = 2^{\lceil \log_2(D_y + 1) \rceil} > D_y\). Since \([x^N] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)} \in K[y]\) is a polynomial in \(y\) of degree at most \(D_y\), evaluating \(y\) at \(L > D_y\) distinct shifted roots of unity \(y_r = c \omega_L^r\) (\(r = 0, 1, \dots, L-1\)) determines the polynomial \([x^N] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\) uniquely.
  2. For each \(r \in \{0, 1, \dots, L-1\}\), substitute the scalar \(y = y_r \in \mathbb{F}_p\) into \(P(x, y)\) and \(Q(x, y)\) via 1D NTT in \(y\), obtaining univariate polynomials \(P_r(x) = P(x, y_r)\) and \(Q_r(x) = Q(x, y_r)\) of degree \(\le d_x\).
  3. Run \(L\) independent 1D Bostan–Mori calls to compute \(ans_r = [x^N] \frac{P_r(x)}{Q_r(x)}\) in \(O(d_x \log d_x \log N)\) time per point \(r\).
  4. Apply a single 1D Inverse NTT of length \(L\) (and divide \([y^k]\) by \(c^k\)) to recover the polynomial \([x^N] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\).
  • Total Time Complexity: \(O(L d_x \log d_x \log N + d_x L \log L)\), shaving a \(\frac{\log(d_x L)}{\log d_x}\) factor off Regime 1 whenever \(D_y\) is bounded (or when \(Q(x, y) = \det(I - x A(y))\) is evaluated at \(L\) roots of unity in QOJ 4815, #232).
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Vanishing Constant Term \(Q(0, \omega_L^r) = 0\) at a Root of Unity

What Goes Wrong: In Regime 2, 1D Bostan–Mori at point \(y_r = \omega_L^r\) requires the univariate denominator \(Q_r(x) = Q(x, y_r)\) to have a nonzero constant term \(Q_r(0) = Q(0, y_r) \neq 0 \pmod p\). If \([x^0] Q(x, y) = q_0(y)\) is a non-constant polynomial in \(y\) (for example, \(Q(x, y) = (1 - y) - x(1 + y)\)), then at \(r = 0\) we have \(y_0 = \omega_L^0 = 1\), causing \(Q(0, 1) = 0\) and division by zero on the very first step of Bostan–Mori!

Remedy (Shifted Root-of-Unity Evaluation \(y_r = c \omega_L^r\)): Before running the \(L\) evaluations, pick a random (or deterministic) nonzero shift \(c \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\) such that \(q_0(c \omega_L^r) \neq 0 \pmod p\) for all \(0 \le r < L\). Because \(q_0(y)\) has degree \(\le d_y \ll p\) and the cosets \(c \cdot \langle \omega_L \rangle\) partition \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\) into \((p - 1)/L\) disjoint sets, testing \(c = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) finds a valid shift \(c\) in at most \(d_y + 1\) tries. Evaluate \(y\) at \(y_r = c \omega_L^r\) by scaling \([y^k] \mapsto c^k [y^k]\) before the forward NTT, and unscale \([y^k] \mapsto c^{-k} [y^k]\) after the final INTT.

Regime 3: Kinoshita–Li \(x\)-Halving / \(y\)-Doubling (\(O(n \log^2 n)\))

What if the target \(x\)-exponent \(n \le 2 \times 10^5\) and the target \(y\)-degree \(n\) are equal, and the initial denominator has high degree in \(x\) (\(d_x = n\)) but degree \(1\) in \(y\) (\(d_y = 1\))? This exact shape governs the Power Projection Problem (and by Tellegen transposition, FPS Composition and Lagrange Inversion, which we develop fully in Chapter 21):

Given \(f(x), g(x) \in K[[x]] / (x^n)\) with \(f(0) = 0\), compute all \(n\) linear projections

\[w_k = [x^{n-1}] g(x) f(x)^k \qquad \text{for } k = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1.\]

Summing over \(k \ge 0\) with \(y^k\) encodes the entire output vector \((w_0, \dots, w_{n-1})\) as a single \(x\)-coefficient of a bivariate rational function:

\[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} w_k y^k = [x^{n-1}] \frac{g(x)}{1 - y f(x)} \pmod{y^n}.\]

At first glance, Regime 1 looks hopeless here: since \(\deg_x(1 - y f(x)) < n\) and we want \(y\) up to \(y^{n-1}\), a naive \(n \times n\) grid has size \(n^2\)! The breakthrough observation of Yasunori Kinoshita and Baitian Li (ISSAC 2024) is that in Bostan–Mori on \(\frac{g(x)}{1 - y f(x)}\), we do not start with \(y\)-degree \(n\), and we do not keep \(x\)-degree \(n\):

  1. Initial State (\(j = 0\)): Set \(N_0 = n - 1\), \(P_0(x, y) = g(x)\), and \(Q_0(x, y) = 1 - y f(x)\). Notice their degrees: \[\deg_x P_0, \deg_x Q_0 < N_0 + 1 = n, \qquad \deg_y P_0 = 0 < K_0 = 1, \quad \deg_y Q_0 \le K_0 = 1.\]
  2. State at Recursion Level \(j\): After \(j\) parity-splitting steps in \(x\), we need \([x^{N_j}] \frac{P_j(x, y)}{Q_j(x, y)} \bmod y^n\) where \(N_j = \lfloor (n - 1) / 2^j \rfloor\). Because we only ever extract \([x^{N_j}]\), we truncate \(P_j(x, y)\) and \(Q_j(x, y)\) modulo \(x^{N_j + 1}\), while the \(y\)-degree at most doubles at each multiplication \(Q_j(x, y) Q_j(-x, y)\): \[\deg_x P_j, \deg_x Q_j \le N_j < \left\lceil \frac{n}{2^j} \right\rceil, \qquad \deg_y P_j < K_j = 2^j, \quad \deg_y Q_j \le K_j = 2^j.\]
  3. The Volume Invariant (\(\deg_x \cdot \deg_y = \Theta(n)\)): At every single level \(j \in \{0, 1, \dots, \lceil \log_2 n \rceil\}\), the \(x\)-dimension halves while the \(y\)-dimension doubles, so the total number of coefficients in \(P_j(x, y)\) and \(Q_j(x, y)\) is invariant: \[\text{Volume}_j = (N_j + 1) \cdot K_j \approx \frac{n}{2^j} \cdot 2^j = n.\] Therefore, each level \(j\) requires a single 2D polynomial multiplication of total volume \(O(n)\), which runs in \(O(n \log n)\) time via Kronecker substitution! Summing over \(j = 0, 1, \dots, \lceil \log_2 n \rceil\) yields a total time complexity of \[\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil \log_2 n \rceil} O(n \log n) = O(n \log^2 n).\]
  4. Terminal Base Case (\(N_L = 0\)): Because \(f(0) = 0\), we have \(Q_0(0, y) = 1 - y f(0) = 1\), and by induction \(Q_j(0, y) = Q_{j-1}(0, y)^2 = 1\) at every level \(j\). When \(N_L = 0\) at \(L = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil\), the \(x\) variable has vanished completely and \(Q_L(0, y) = 1\), so the answer polynomial is simply \(P_L(0, y) \bmod y^n\) with zero inversions required!
Notemaspypy’s Monic \(y\)-Reversal & Pointer to Chapter 21

In \(Q_j(x, y) = 1 + q_{j, 1}(x) y + \dots + q_{j, K_j}(x) y^{K_j}\), the \(y\)-degree is \(K_j = 2^j\), which has \(2^j + 1\) coefficients in \(y\)—one slot too many for an exact power-of-two FFT block of size \(2^j\)! As popularized by maspypy, if we reverse \(y\) by storing \(\widetilde{Q}_j(x, y) := y^{K_j} Q_j(x, y^{-1}) = y^{K_j} + \sum_{r=0}^{K_j - 1} \tilde{q}_{j, r}(x) y^r\), then the leading term \([y^{K_j}] \widetilde{Q}_j(x, y) = Q_j(x, 0) = 1\) is a pure constant (independent of \(x\)). Omitting this known constant \(1\) leaves exactly \(2^j\) coefficients in \(y\), eliminating all padding waste. In Chapter 21, we will combine this exact memory layout with Tellegen’s Transposition Principle to implement \(O(n \log^2 n)\) FPS Composition \(g(f(x)) \bmod x^n\) and Compositional Inverse \(f^{\langle -1 \rangle}(x) \bmod x^n\).

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(n = 4\)): Kinoshita–Li \(x\)-Halving / \(y\)-Doubling

Let \(n = 4\), \(g(x) = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3\), and \(f(x) = x + 2x^2\) (note \(f(0) = 0\)). We want \(w_k = [x^3] g(x) f(x)^k\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, 3\):

  • Direct Check:
    • \(k = 0\): \([x^3](1 + x + x^2 + x^3) = 1\).
    • \(k = 1\): \([x^3](1 + x + x^2 + x^3)(x + 2x^2) = 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 = 3\).
    • \(k = 2\): \([x^3](1 + x + x^2 + x^3)(x^2 + 4x^3 + 4x^4) = 1 \cdot 4 + 1 \cdot 1 = 5\).
    • \(k = 3\): \([x^3](1 + x + x^2 + x^3)(x^3 + 6x^4 + \dots) = 1\).
    • Target polynomial: \(W(y) = 1 + 3y + 5y^2 + y^3\).
  • Level \(j = 0\) (\(N_0 = 3\) is odd, \(K_0 = 1\)):
    • \(P_0(x, y) = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3\), \(\quad Q_0(x, y) = 1 - y(x + 2x^2) = 1 - x y - 2 x^2 y\).
    • Flip sign of \(x\): \(Q_0(-x, y) = 1 + x y - 2 x^2 y\).
    • Multiply numerator modulo \(x^4\): \[U_0(x, y) = P_0(x, y) Q_0(-x, y) \bmod x^4 = 1 + x(1 + y) + x^2(1 - y) + x^3(1 - y).\]
    • Since \(N_0 = 3\) is odd, extract odd \(x\)-powers (\(x^1\) and \(x^3\)) and replace \(x^{2i+1} \mapsto x^i\): \[P_1(x, y) = (1 + y) + x(1 - y) \qquad (\deg_x P_1 = 1 \le N_1 = 1,\; \deg_y P_1 = 1 < K_1 = 2).\]
    • Multiply denominator modulo \(x^4\): \[V_0(x^2, y) = Q_0(x, y) Q_0(-x, y) \bmod x^4 = (1 - 2x^2 y)^2 - x^2 y^2 \equiv 1 - x^2(4y + y^2) \pmod{x^4}.\] Replacing \(x^2 \mapsto x\) gives \(Q_1(x, y) = 1 - x(4y + y^2)\) (\(\deg_x Q_1 = 1 \le N_1 = 1,\; \deg_y Q_1 = 2 \le K_1 = 2\)).
  • Level \(j = 1\) (\(N_1 = \lfloor 3/2 \rfloor = 1\) is odd, \(K_1 = 2\)):
    • Flip sign of \(x\): \(Q_1(-x, y) = 1 + x(4y + y^2)\).
    • Multiply numerator modulo \(x^2\): \[U_1(x, y) = P_1(x, y) Q_1(-x, y) \bmod x^2 = (1 + y) + x\big((1 - y) + (1 + y)(4y + y^2)\big).\]
    • Since \(N_1 = 1\) is odd, extract the coefficient of \(x^1\): \[P_2(0, y) = (1 - y) + (4y + 5y^2 + y^3) = 1 + 3y + 5y^2 + y^3.\]
  • Level \(j = 2\) (\(N_2 = 0\), Base Case):
    • Since \(Q_2(0, y) = 1\), we read off \(W(y) = P_2(0, y) = 1 + 3y + 5y^2 + y^3\), matching \((1, 3, 5, 1)\)!
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Nonzero Constant Term \(f(0) \neq 0\) and Premature \(x\)-Truncation in Regime 3
  1. Calling Kinoshita–Li with \(f(0) = c \neq 0\): If \(f(0) = c \neq 0\), then \(Q_0(0, y) = 1 - c y \neq 1\), so \(y f(x)\) has valuation \(0\) in \(x\), and \(\frac{g(x)}{1 - y f(x)}\) is not a polynomial in \(y\) at each power of \(x\)! Remedy: Write \(f(x) = c + \tilde{f}(x)\) with \(\tilde{f}(0) = 0\). Run Kinoshita–Li Power Projection on \(\tilde{f}(x)\) to obtain \(\widetilde{W}(y) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \tilde{w}_j y^j\), and apply a single 1D Taylor Shift \(W(y) = \widetilde{W}(y + c) \bmod y^n\) in \(O(n \log n)\) time: \[w_k = [x^{n-1}] g(x) (c + \tilde{f}(x))^k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \binom{k}{j} c^{k-j} \tilde{w}_j.\]
  2. Truncating \(Q_j(x, y)\) Too Aggressively in \(x\): At level \(j\) with target \(x\)-exponent \(N_j\), the next denominator \(Q_{j+1}(x, y)\) will be needed up to \(x^{N_{j+1}}\) where \(N_{j+1} = \lfloor N_j / 2 \rfloor\). Because \(Q_{j+1}(x^2, y) = Q_j(x, y) Q_j(-x, y)\), you must retain \(Q_j(x, y)\) modulo \(x^{N_j + 1}\) and compute the product \(Q_j(x, y) Q_j(-x, y)\) modulo \(x^{2 N_{j+1} + 2}\) before halving exponents.

Regime 4: Bostan–Christol–Dumas Frobenius Reduction Over \(\mathbb{F}_p\)

What if both target exponents \(N, M \le 10^{18}\) in \([x^N y^M] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)} \bmod p\) are huge, while the prime modulus \(p\) is small (e.g., \(p \le 500\))? Neither Regime 1 nor Regime 2 can truncate \(x\) or \(y\), and if \(M = N\) (a diagonal extraction), Furstenberg’s D-finite recurrence (§16.4) cannot run to \(N = 10^{18}\) because its division by \(n + 1\) hits multiples of \(p\) every \(p\) steps!

Instead, we exploit Freshman’s Dream in the characteristic-\(p\) polynomial ring \(\mathbb{F}_p[x, y]\):

\[Q(x, y)^p = \left(\sum_{i, j} q_{i, j} x^i y^j\right)^p = \sum_{i, j} q_{i, j}^p x^{p i} y^{p j} = Q(x^p, y^p) \pmod p,\]

where the last equality uses Fermat’s Little Theorem \(q_{i, j}^p \equiv q_{i, j} \pmod p\) for \(q_{i, j} \in \mathbb{F}_p\). Multiplying the numerator and denominator of \(\frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)}\) by \(Q(x, y)^{p-1}\) makes all exponents in the denominator multiples of \(p\):

\[\frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)} = \frac{P(x, y) Q(x, y)^{p-1}}{Q(x, y)^p} = \frac{U(x, y)}{Q(x^p, y^p)}, \qquad U(x, y) := P(x, y) Q(x, y)^{p-1} \in \mathbb{F}_p[x, y].\]

Now write \(N = p N' + r_x\) and \(M = p M' + r_y\) with base-\(p\) digits \(r_x = N \bmod p\) and \(r_y = M \bmod p\) in \(\{0, 1, \dots, p - 1\}\). Because every monomial in \(\frac{1}{Q(x^p, y^p)}\) has \(x\)-exponent and \(y\)-exponent divisible by \(p\), only monomials \(x^a y^b\) in \(U(x, y)\) with \((a, b) \equiv (r_x, r_y) \pmod p\) can contribute to \([x^N y^M]\). Defining the Cartier / sectioning operator \(\mathcal{S}_{r_x, r_y}\) by

\[\mathcal{S}_{r_x, r_y}\big(U(x, y)\big) := \sum_{i \ge 0, j \ge 0} \big([x^{p i + r_x} y^{p j + r_y}] U(x, y)\big) x^i y^j,\]

we obtain the single-step base-\(p\) reduction

\[[x^N y^M] \frac{P(x, y)}{Q(x, y)} = [x^{\lfloor N/p \rfloor} y^{\lfloor M/p \rfloor}] \frac{\mathcal{S}_{N \bmod p, \, M \bmod p}\big(P(x, y) Q(x, y)^{p-1}\big)}{Q(x, y)}.\]

  • Degree Invariant: Suppose \(\deg_x P < \deg_x Q = d_x\) and \(\deg_y P < \deg_y Q = d_y\). Then \(\deg_x U = \deg_x P + (p - 1) d_x \le p d_x - 1\), so after dividing exponents by \(p\), the new numerator \(P_{\text{new}} = \mathcal{S}_{r_x, r_y}(U)\) satisfies \[\deg_x P_{\text{new}} \le \left\lfloor \frac{p d_x - 1}{p} \right\rfloor = d_x - 1 < d_x, \qquad \deg_y P_{\text{new}} \le d_y - 1 < d_y.\] The numerator bidegree stays strictly below \((d_x, d_y)\) across all \(\lfloor \log_p \max(N, M) \rfloor + 1\) steps! Precomputing \(Q(x, y)^{p-1}\) once takes \(O(p^2 d_x d_y \log(p d_x d_y))\) (or \(O(p^2 d_x^2 d_y^2)\) for tiny \(d_x, d_y\)), and each base-\(p\) digit step multiplies \(P(x, y)\) of bidegree \((< d_x, < d_y)\) by the fixed polynomial \(Q(x, y)^{p-1}\) of bidegree \(((p-1)d_x, (p-1)d_y)\) (in fact, only the \((r_x, r_y) \bmod p\) congruence slices of \(Q^{p-1}\) are needed, costing \(O(d_x^2 d_y^2)\) per digit!).

16.3 Twisted Kronecker Substitution for \(d\)-Dimensional FPS (\(\text{mul}, \text{inv}, \ln, \exp\))

Suppose we need to multiply, invert, or take \(\ln\) and \(\exp\) of \(d\)-variate truncated formal power series in the quotient ring

\[\mathcal{R}_d := \mathbb{F}_p[x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d] \,/\, (x_1^{n_1}, x_2^{n_2}, \dots, x_d^{n_d}), \qquad N := \prod_{r=1}^{d} n_r.\]

If we use a naive \(d\)-dimensional FFT or standard zero-padded Kronecker substitution (padding each dimension \(r\) from \(n_r\) to \(2 n_r - 1\) to prevent carries between adjacent coordinates), the total grid volume blows up from \(N\) to \(\prod_{r=1}^d (2 n_r) = 2^d N\). When \(d = 3\) and \(n_1 = n_2 = n_3 = 51\) (as in UOJ 596, #214), padding each dimension to the next power of two (\(128^3 = 2,097,152\)) inflates the array size by \(16\times\) over \(N = 51^3 = 132,651\), causing immediate Time Limit Exceeded.

Can we pack \(\mathcal{R}_d\) into a flat 1D array of exact size \(N = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_d\) without any zero-padding between dimensions, and still prevent wrap-around carries from corrupting the product? Yes—by augmenting the mixed-radix Kronecker index with a mod-\(d\) carry-grading twist \(\chi(I)\) (a multidimensional generalization of subset convolution, popularized in competitive programming by Elegia / EI).

    3. Specifically, \[\left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{i}) + I(\mathbf{j})}{N_r} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{i})}{N_r} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{j})}{N_r} \right\rfloor = c_r \in \{0, 1\} \qquad \text{for every } 1 \le r \le d - 1.\] Summing over all \(r = 1, \dots, d - 1\) gives the total number of carries \(\sum_{r=1}^{d-1} c_r \in \{0, 1, \dots, d - 1\}\). Because \(0 \le \sum_{r=1}^{d-1} c_r \le d - 1 < d\), this sum vanishes if and only if it vanishes modulo \(d\)!

Theorem 16.1 (Carry-Detection via the \(\chi\)-Grading)

Define the carry-grading function \(\chi : [0, N - 1] \to \mathbb{Z} / d\mathbb{Z}\) by

\[\chi(I) := \left( \sum_{r=1}^{d-1} \left\lfloor \frac{I}{N_r} \right\rfloor \right) \bmod d = \left( \sum_{s=2}^{d} i_s \sum_{r=1}^{s-1} \frac{N_{s-1}}{N_r} \right) \bmod d.\]

Then for any two exponent tuples \(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j} \in \prod_{r=1}^d [0, n_r - 1]\) whose packed indices satisfy \(I(\mathbf{i}) + I(\mathbf{j}) = K < N\) (which automatically ensures \(i_d + j_d < n_d\), i.e., \(c_d = 0\)), the componentwise sum \(\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j}\) has no overflow in any coordinate (\(i_r + j_r < n_r\) for all \(1 \le r \le d\)) if and only if

\[\chi\big(I(\mathbf{i})\big) + \chi\big(I(\mathbf{j})\big) \equiv \chi(K) \pmod d.\]

Proof: For each \(r \in \{1, \dots, d - 1\}\), write \(I(\mathbf{i}) = q_{r, 1} N_r + u_{r, 1}\) and \(I(\mathbf{j}) = q_{r, 2} N_r + u_{r, 2}\) with remainders \(0 \le u_{r, 1}, u_{r, 2} < N_r\). Then \(K = I(\mathbf{i}) + I(\mathbf{j}) = (q_{r, 1} + q_{r, 2}) N_r + (u_{r, 1} + u_{r, 2})\), so

\[\left\lfloor \frac{K}{N_r} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{i})}{N_r} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{j})}{N_r} \right\rfloor = \left\lfloor \frac{u_{r, 1} + u_{r, 2}}{N_r} \right\rfloor := c_r \in \{0, 1\},\]

since \(0 \le u_{r, 1} + u_{r, 2} < 2 N_r\). By induction on \(r = 1, 2, \dots, d - 1\), all previous carries \(c_1 = \dots = c_{r-1} = 0\) vanish if and only if \(u_{r, 1} + u_{r, 2} < N_r\) is equivalent to \(i_r + j_r < n_r\). Summing the non-negative integers \(c_r \in \{0, 1\}\) over \(1 \le r \le d - 1\) gives

\[\sum_{r=1}^{d-1} \left\lfloor \frac{K}{N_r} \right\rfloor - \sum_{r=1}^{d-1} \left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{i})}{N_r} \right\rfloor - \sum_{r=1}^{d-1} \left\lfloor \frac{I(\mathbf{j})}{N_r} \right\rfloor = \sum_{r=1}^{d-1} c_r \in \{0, 1, \dots, d - 1\}.\]

Since a sum of \(d - 1\) numbers in \(\{0, 1\}\) lies strictly between \(0\) and \(d - 1\), it is congruent to \(0 \pmod d\) if and only if every single \(c_r = 0\), i.e., \(i_r + j_r < n_r\) for all \(1 \le r \le d\). \(\blacksquare\)

The Radial Euler Derivation \(\mathfrak{D}\) for Multivariate \(\ln\) and \(\exp\)

Once we have \(d\)-dimensional multiplication \(A \cdot_\chi B\) in \(O(d N \log N)\) time (\(2d\) forward NTTs of length \(2^{\lceil \log_2(2N) \rceil}\), \(O(d^2 N)\) pointwise multiply-adds in the frequency domain, and \(d\) inverse NTTs), how do we compute \(G^{-1}\), \(\ln G\), and \(\exp F\) in \(\mathcal{R}_d\)?

  1. Multivariate Inversion \(G^{-1}\) via 1D Kronecker-Index Newton Doubling: Because every valid componentwise sum \(\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}\) satisfies \(I(\mathbf{i}) + I(\mathbf{j}) = I(\mathbf{k})\) in the packed 1D order, the \(\chi\)-graded multiplication \(A \cdot_\chi B\) is lower-triangular with respect to the 1D Kronecker index \(I \in [0, N - 1]\)! Therefore, we can run standard 1D Newton doubling \(H_{2M} = H_M \cdot_\chi (2 - G \cdot_\chi H_M) \bmod z^{2M}\) directly on the 1D packed prefix \(I < M\) (\(M = 1, 2, 4, \dots, 2^{\lceil \log_2 N \rceil}\)), computing \(G^{-1} \in \mathcal{R}_d\) in \[T_{\text{inv}}(N) = \sum_{k=0}^{\lceil \log_2 N \rceil} O(d \cdot 2^k \log 2^k) = O(d N \log N).\]

  2. The Radial Euler Derivation \(\mathfrak{D}\) for \(\ln G\) and \(\exp F\): In 1D, we compute \(\ln G(x) = \int \frac{G'(x)}{G(x)} \, dx\) because \(x \frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = n x^n\) multiplies the \(n\)-th coefficient by \(n\). In \(d\) variables, if we used partial derivatives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_r}\) one variable at a time, we would have to recurse on the \((d-1)\)-variate boundary \(x_1 = 0\). Instead, define the Radial Euler Derivation \(\mathfrak{D} : \mathcal{R}_d \to \mathcal{R}_d\) with weights \(w_r = N_{r-1}\) matching our Kronecker mixed radices: \[\mathfrak{D} := \sum_{r=1}^{d} N_{r-1} \, x_r \frac{\partial}{\partial x_r}.\] How does \(\mathfrak{D}\) act on a packed monomial \(\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}} = x_1^{i_1} \cdots x_d^{i_d}\)? By linearity of partial differentiation, \[\mathfrak{D}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}}) = \left( \sum_{r=1}^{d} i_r N_{r-1} \right) \mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}} = I(\mathbf{i}) \, \mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}}.\] That is, \(\mathfrak{D}\) simply multiplies the \(I\)-th packed coefficient by its 1D Kronecker index \(I \in [0, N - 1]\), and remains a true algebraic derivation (\(\mathfrak{D}(A \cdot_\chi B) = \mathfrak{D}(A) \cdot_\chi B + A \cdot_\chi \mathfrak{D}(B)\) and \(\mathfrak{D}(\ln G) = \mathfrak{D}(G) \cdot_\chi G^{-1}\))! Consequently, whenever the prime modulus satisfies \(p > N - 1\) so that every \(I \in \{1, 2, \dots, N - 1\}\) is invertible modulo \(p\): \[\ln G(\mathbf{x}) = \mathfrak{D}^{-1}\!\Big( \mathfrak{D}(G) \cdot_\chi G^{-1} \Big), \qquad \exp F(\mathbf{x}) \text{ via Newton on } \ln H - F = 0.\]

16.4 Furstenberg’s Diagonal Theorem (\(\operatorname{Diag}(R) \leftrightarrow\) Algebraic) & Failure Mode #15

Suppose a bivariate generating function \(F(x, y) = \frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)} \in K[[x, y]]\) is rational, and we want to extract its main diagonal

\[\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} F(x, y) := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \big([x^n y^n] F(x, y)\big) t^n \in K[[t]].\]

In 1D, rational generating functions correspond to linear recurrences with constant coefficients. In 2D, the diagonal of a rational function is almost never rational—for example, \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} \frac{1}{1 - x - y} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \binom{2n}{n} t^n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 4t}}\) has a square-root singularity! A landmark theorem of Harry Furstenberg (1967) explains why: in characteristic \(0\) (and over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) for large primes), the diagonals of bivariate rational power series are precisely the univariate algebraic power series.

TipDiagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Furstenberg Diagonal Extraction
  • Diagnostic Signal: You need \(d_n = [x^n y^n] \frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)}\) (or a shifted diagonal \([x^n y^{n+s}] \frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)}\)) for all \(0 \le n \le N\) (\(N \le 10^6\)), where \(B(x, y)\) has small bidegree (especially quadratic after substituting \(y = t / x\)).
  • Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    1. Convert Diagonal to Residue in \(K((z))[[t]]\): Substitute \(x = z\) and \(y = t / z\). By §16.1, for any \(s \in \mathbb{Z}\), \[\sum_{n} \big([x^n y^{n+s}] F(x, y)\big) t^{n+s} = \operatorname{CT}_z\!\Big(z^s F(z, t / z)\Big) = \operatorname{Res}_z\!\Big(z^{s - 1} F(z, t / z)\Big).\]
    2. Clear Negative Powers of \(z\) in the Denominator: Let \(d = \deg_y B(x, y)\). Multiply numerator and denominator of \(z^{s-1} \frac{A(z, t/z)}{B(z, t/z)}\) by \(z^d\) so the denominator becomes a polynomial \(Q(z, t) := z^d B(z, t/z) \in K[t, z]\).
    3. Factor into Small Roots vs. Large Roots: Over the algebraic closure \(\overline{K((t))}\), factor \(Q(z, t)\) in \(z\). A root \(z = z_i(t)\) is called a small root if \(\lim_{t \to 0} z_i(t) = 0\) (i.e., \(\operatorname{val}_t(z_i) > 0\)), and a large root if \(\operatorname{val}_t(z_i) \le 0\).
      • For a small root \(z_i(t) \in t \overline{K}[[t^\alpha]]\), expanding \(\frac{1}{z - z_i(t)} = \frac{1}{z(1 - z_i(t)/z)} = \sum_{k \ge 0} z_i(t)^k z^{-k-1}\) in \(K((z))[[t]]\) produces negative powers of \(z\), contributing \(\operatorname{Res}_z \frac{1}{z - z_i(t)} = 1\).
      • For a large root \(z_j(t)\) with \(\operatorname{val}_t(z_j) \le 0\), expanding \(\frac{1}{z - z_j(t)} = -\frac{1}{z_j(t)(1 - z/z_j(t))} = -\sum_{n \ge 0} z_j(t)^{-n-1} z^n\) produces only non-negative powers of \(z \ge 0\), contributing \(0\) to \([z^{-1}]\)!
    4. Sum Formal Residues Over Small Poles Only (Plus \(z = 0\) if Improper!): Check whether \(\deg_y A < \deg_y B\) (so no extra pole at \(z = 0\) exists beyond small roots of \(Q(z, t)\)), and sum the algebraic residues at the small roots \(z_i(t)\).

Direction 1 (Reverse Extraction): \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y}\!\left(\frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)}\right) \mapsto\) Algebraic Series

Theorem 16.2 (Furstenberg’s Rational Diagonal Residue Formula & 1-Line Quadratic Shortcut)

Let \(F(x, y) = \frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)} \in K[[x, y]]\) with \(B(0, 0) \neq 0\), and suppose \(\deg_y A(x, y) < \deg_y B(x, y) = d\) (so that \(z^{-1} F(z, t/z) = \frac{z^{d-1} A(z, t/z)}{z^d B(z, t/z)}\) has no leftover pole at \(z = 0\) outside the roots of \(Q(z, t) := z^d B(z, t/z)\)). If all small roots \(z_1(t), \dots, z_r(t)\) of \(Q(z, t) = 0\) (those satisfying \(\operatorname{val}_t(z_i) > 0\)) are simple, then

\[\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y}\!\left(\frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)}\right) = \operatorname{Res}_z\!\left(\frac{1}{z} \frac{A(z, t / z)}{B(z, t / z)}\right) = \sum_{\substack{Q(z_i(t), t) = 0 \\ \operatorname{val}_t(z_i) > 0}} \frac{z_i(t)^{d-1} A\big(z_i(t), t / z_i(t)\big)}{\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}\big(z_i(t), t\big)}.\]

The 1-Line Quadratic Furstenberg Shortcut: Assume \(\operatorname{char}(K) \neq 2\) (and \(p > N\) when evaluating the resulting algebraic series or its D-finite P-recursion up to \(t^N\); for small primes \(p \le N\), use the Frobenius reduction of §16.2–§16.3 instead). In the most common case where \(z^{-1} F(z, t/z)\) has a quadratic denominator in \(z\) of the form

\[\frac{1}{z} F(z, t / z) = \frac{P(z, t)}{\alpha(t) z^2 + \beta(t) z + \gamma(t)}\]

with \(\beta(0) = B(0, 0) \neq 0\) and \(\operatorname{val}_t(\gamma) \ge 1\) (so \(Q(z, t) = \alpha(t) z^2 + \beta(t) z + \gamma(t)\) has literally one small root \(z_1(t) = \frac{-\beta(t) + \beta(0)\sqrt{1 - 4\alpha(t)\gamma(t)/\beta(t)^2}}{2\alpha(t)} = -\frac{\gamma(t)}{\beta(t)} + O(t^2)\) and one large root \(z_2(t) = -\frac{\beta(t)}{\alpha(t)} + O(1)\)), the derivative at \(z_1(t)\) is \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}(z_1(t), t) = 2\alpha(t) z_1(t) + \beta(t) = \sqrt{\beta(t)^2 - 4\alpha(t)\gamma(t)}\). In particular, when \(P(z, t) = 1\), we get the 1-line quadratic shortcut:

\[\operatorname{Res}_z\!\left(\frac{1}{\alpha(t) z^2 + \beta(t) z + \gamma(t)}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(t)^2 - 4 \alpha(t) \gamma(t)}},\]

where the square root branch is normalized so that \(\sqrt{\beta(0)^2} = \beta(0)\).

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 4\)): Delannoy Numbers via the Quadratic Furstenberg Shortcut

Consider the Delannoy numbers \(d_n = [x^n y^n] \frac{1}{1 - x - y - x y}\), which count lattice paths from \((0, 0)\) to \((n, n)\) using steps \((1, 0)\), \((0, 1)\), and \((1, 1)\).

  1. Direct Grid Check (\(0 \le n \le 4\)):

    • \(n = 0\): \(d_0 = 1\).
    • \(n = 1\): paths \((1,0)(0,1)\), \((0,1)(1,0)\), \((1,1) \implies d_1 = 3\).
    • \(n = 2\): \(\sum_{k=0}^2 \binom{2}{k}\binom{4-k}{2} = 1(6) + 2(3) + 1(1) = 13\).
    • \(n = 3\): \(\sum_{k=0}^3 \binom{3}{k}\binom{6-k}{3} = 1(20) + 3(10) + 3(4) + 1(1) = 63\).
    • \(n = 4\): \(\sum_{k=0}^4 \binom{4}{k}\binom{8-k}{4} = 1(70) + 4(35) + 6(15) + 4(5) + 1(1) = 321\).

  2. Furstenberg Substitution: Substitute \(x = z\) and \(y = t / z\) into \(z^{-1} F(z, t/z)\): \[\frac{1}{z} \frac{1}{1 - z - t/z - t} = \frac{1}{-z^2 + (1 - t)z - t}.\] Here \(\alpha(t) = -1\), \(\beta(t) = 1 - t\) (\(\beta(0) = 1\)), and \(\gamma(t) = -t\) (\(\operatorname{val}_t(\gamma) = 1\)). Applying the 1-line quadratic shortcut gives \[D(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} d_n t^n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - t)^2 - 4(-1)(-t)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 6t + t^2}}.\]

  3. Verify Expansion & P-Recursion up to \(n = 4\): Differentiating \((1 - 6t + t^2) D(t)^2 = 1\) gives \((1 - 6t + t^2) D'(t) - (3 - t) D(t) = 0\). Extracting \([t^n]\) yields the 3-term recurrence \((n + 1) d_{n+1} = 3(2n + 1) d_n - n d_{n-1}\):

    • \(n = 0\): \(1 \cdot d_1 = 3(1)(1) - 0 = 3\).
    • \(n = 1\): \(2 \cdot d_2 = 3(3)(3) - 1(1) = 26 \implies d_2 = 13\).
    • \(n = 2\): \(3 \cdot d_3 = 3(5)(13) - 2(3) = 189 \implies d_3 = 63\).
    • \(n = 3\): \(4 \cdot d_4 = 3(7)(63) - 3(13) = 1284 \implies d_4 = 321\).

Direction 2 (Forward Lift): Every Algebraic Series is a Rational Diagonal

Conversely, suppose \(F(t) \in K[[t]]\) is an algebraic power series with \(F(0) = 0\) satisfying a polynomial equation \(P(t, F(t)) = 0\) under the non-degeneracy (separability) condition

\[P(0, 0) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}(0, 0) \neq 0.\]

(By the Implicit Function Theorem for FPS, \(y = F(t)\) is the unique small root of \(P(t, y) = 0\) in \(t K[[t]]\).) How can we construct an explicit bivariate rational function \(R(x, y)\) whose diagonal is \(F(t)\)?

Theorem 16.3 (Furstenberg’s Forward Lift \(F(t) \mapsto \operatorname{Diag}(R)\))

Let \(P(t, y) \in K[t, y]\) satisfy \(P(0, 0) = 0\) and \(P_y(0, 0) \neq 0\), and let \(F(t) \in t K[[t]]\) be the unique formal power series with \(F(0) = 0\) and \(P(t, F(t)) = 0\). Then \(F(t)\) is the main diagonal of the bivariate rational function

\[F(t) = \operatorname{Diag}_{x, y}\!\left( \frac{y^2 P_y(x y, y)}{P(x y, y)} \right), \qquad \text{where } \frac{y^2 P_y(x y, y)}{P(x y, y)} \in K[[x, y]].\]

Proof (Single-Step Verbalized Derivation):

  1. First verify that \(R(x, y) := \frac{y^2 P_y(xy, y)}{P(xy, y)}\) actually belongs to \(K[[x, y]]\): since \(P(0, 0) = 0\) and \(P_y(0, 0) = c \neq 0\), we can write \(P(t, y) = y \big(c + A(t, y)\big) + t B(t)\) where \(A(0, 0) = 0\). Substituting \(t = x y\) divides out a common factor of \(y\) from numerator and denominator: \[R(x, y) = \frac{y^2 P_y(x y, y)}{y\big(c + A(x y, y) + x B(x y)\big)} = \frac{y P_y(x y, y)}{c + A(x y, y) + x B(x y)},\] whose denominator has constant term \(c \in K^\times\) at \((x, y) = (0, 0)\), so \(R(x, y) \in K[[x, y]]\).
  2. Now apply the diagonal-to-residue transformation in \(K((z))[[t]]\), where it is convenient to set \(y = z\) and \(x = t / z\) so that \(x y = t\): \[\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} R(x, y) &= \operatorname{CT}_z R(t / z, z) && \text{(diagonal substitution } x = t/z,\; y = z \text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{Res}_z\!\left( \frac{1}{z} \frac{z^2 P_y(t, z)}{P(t, z)} \right) && \text{(since } \operatorname{CT}_z L(z) = \operatorname{Res}_z(z^{-1} L(z)) \text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{Res}_z\!\left( \frac{z P_y(t, z)}{P(t, z)} \right) && \text{(cancel } z^{-1} \text{ against } z^2 \text{)}. \end{aligned}\]
  3. In the ring \(K((z))[[t]]\), \(P(t, z) = (z - F(t)) U(t, z)\) where \(z = F(t) \in t K[[t]]\) is the unique small root of \(P(t, z) = 0\), and \(U(t, z)\) has no small roots (\(U(0, 0) = P_y(0, 0) = c \neq 0\)). By the logarithmic derivative identity \(\frac{P_y(t, z)}{P(t, z)} = \frac{1}{z - F(t)} + \frac{U_z(t, z)}{U(t, z)}\) where \(\frac{U_z(t, z)}{U(t, z)} \in K[[z, t]]\) has only non-negative powers of \(z\), the residue at the simple pole \(z = F(t)\) evaluates to \[\operatorname{Res}_z\!\left( \frac{z P_y(t, z)}{P(t, z)} \right) = \operatorname{Res}_z\!\left( \frac{z}{z - F(t)} \right) = F(t). \qquad \blacksquare\]

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 4\)): Lifting Shifted Catalan Numbers to a Rational Diagonal

Let \(F(t) = t C(t) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4t}}{2} = t + t^2 + 2t^3 + 5t^4 + \dots\) be the shifted Catalan generating function, which satisfies \(F(0) = 0\) and \(P(t, F(t)) = 0\) where

\[P(t, y) = y - y^2 - t, \qquad P_y(t, y) = 1 - 2y \quad (P(0, 0) = 0,\; P_y(0, 0) = 1 \neq 0).\]

By Theorem 16.3, substitute \(t = x y\) and divide numerator and denominator by \(y\):

\[R(x, y) = \frac{y^2 P_y(x y, y)}{P(x y, y)} = \frac{y^2 (1 - 2y)}{y - y^2 - x y} = \frac{y (1 - 2y)}{1 - x - y}.\]

Let us check the diagonal coefficients \([x^n y^n] R(x, y)\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4\) using \([x^n y^k] \frac{1}{1 - x - y} = \binom{n+k}{n}\):

\[[x^n y^n] \frac{y - 2y^2}{1 - x - y} = [x^n y^{n-1}] \frac{1}{1 - x - y} - 2 [x^n y^{n-2}] \frac{1}{1 - x - y} = \binom{2n - 1}{n} - 2 \binom{2n - 2}{n}.\]

  • \(n = 0\): \(0 - 0 = 0\).
  • \(n = 1\): \(\binom{1}{1} - 2\binom{0}{1} = 1 - 0 = 1 = C_0\).
  • \(n = 2\): \(\binom{3}{2} - 2\binom{2}{2} = 3 - 2 = 1 = C_1\).
  • \(n = 3\): \(\binom{5}{3} - 2\binom{4}{3} = 10 - 8 = 2 = C_2\).
  • \(n = 4\): \(\binom{7}{4} - 2\binom{6}{4} = 35 - 30 = 5 = C_3\).

Every coefficient matches \(F(t) = t + t^2 + 2t^3 + 5t^4 + \dots\) on the nose!

Failure Mode #15: Hidden Pole at \(z = 0\) in Improper Rational Diagonals (\(\deg_y A \ge \deg_y B\))

WarningFailure Mode #15 & Remedy: Omitting the Residue at \(z = 0\) When \(\deg_y A \ge \deg_y B\)

What Goes Wrong: In Theorem 16.2, we required the strict degree bound \(\deg_y A(x, y) < \deg_y B(x, y) = d\). Why? Look at what happens when we substitute \(y = t / z\) into \(\frac{1}{z} \frac{A(z, t/z)}{B(z, t/z)}\) and clear the denominator by multiplying top and bottom by \(z^d\):

\[\frac{1}{z} \frac{A(z, t / z)}{B(z, t / z)} = \frac{z^{d - 1} A(z, t / z)}{z^d B(z, t / z)}.\]

If \(\deg_y A = m \ge d = \deg_y B\), then \(A(z, t/z)\) contains \(y^m = t^m / z^m\), so the numerator \(z^{d - 1} A(z, t / z)\) still contains negative powers of \(z\) up to \(z^{d - 1 - m} = z^{-(m - d + 1)}\)! That means \(z = 0\) is itself a small pole of order \(\le m - d + 1\) alongside the small roots \(z_i(t)\) of \(Q(z, t) = z^d B(z, t/z) = 0\). If you blindly sum residues only over the nonzero small roots \(z_i(t)\) of \(Q(z, t) = 0\) and forget \(\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\), your answer will be completely wrong!

Concrete Worked Failure (\(\frac{x + 2y}{1 - x - y}\)): Let \(F(x, y) = \frac{x + 2y}{1 - x - y}\), where \(\deg_y A = 1 \ge \deg_y B = 1\).

  • True Diagonal (\(0 \le n \le 3\)): Since \([x^n y^n] \frac{x + 2y}{1 - x - y} = [x^{n-1} y^n] \frac{1}{1 - x - y} + 2 [x^n y^{n-1}] \frac{1}{1 - x - y} = 3 \binom{2n - 1}{n - 1}\) for \(n \ge 1\) (and \(0\) for \(n = 0\)), the true diagonal sequence for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\) is: \[(0,\; 3,\; 9,\; 30,\; \dots), \qquad \operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} F(x, y) = \frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 4t}} - 1\right).\]
  • What Happens If You Forget \(z = 0\): Substituting \(x = z, y = t / z\) into \(z^{-1} F(z, t/z)\) gives \[\frac{1}{z} \frac{z + 2t/z}{1 - z - t/z} = \frac{z^2 + 2t}{z\big(-z^2 + z - t\big)}.\] Notice the extra factor of \(z\) in the denominator! The quadratic \(-z^2 + z - t = 0\) has unique small root \(z_1(t) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4t}}{2}\), at which the residue is \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Res}_{z = z_1(t)} \frac{z^2 + 2t}{z(-z^2 + z - t)} &= \frac{z_1(t)^2 + 2t}{z_1(t)\bigl(1 - 2z_1(t)\bigr)} && \text{(apply simple-pole residue formula } \frac{A(z_1)}{B'(z_1)}\text{)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{z_1(t) + t}{z_1(t)\sqrt{1 - 4t}} && \text{(use } z_1(t)^2 = z_1(t) - t \text{ and } 1 - 2z_1(t) = \sqrt{1 - 4t}\text{)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{1 + \frac{t}{z_1(t)}}{\sqrt{1 - 4t}} && \text{(divide numerator and denominator by } z_1(t)\text{)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{1 + \bigl(1 - z_1(t)\bigr)}{\sqrt{1 - 4t}} = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{1 - 4t}} + \frac{1}{2}. && \text{(since } z_1^2 - z_1 + t = 0 \implies \frac{t}{z_1} = 1 - z_1 = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1-4t}}{2}\text{)} \end{aligned} \] Expanding \(\frac{3}{2\sqrt{1-4t}} + \frac{1}{2}\) gives \((2,\; 3,\; 9,\; 30, \dots)\)—wrong at \(n = 0\) (and if \(\deg_y A - \deg_y B \ge 1\), wrong at every \(n \ge 0\))! Why? Because \(z = 0\) is also a simple pole of \(\frac{z^2 + 2t}{z(-z^2 + z - t)}\) with residue \[\operatorname{Res}_{z = 0} \frac{z^2 + 2t}{z(-z^2 + z - t)} = \left.\frac{z^2 + 2t}{-z^2 + z - t}\right|_{z = 0} = \frac{2t}{-t} = -2.\] Adding the missing residue at \(z = 0\) gives \(\left(\frac{3}{2\sqrt{1 - 4t}} + \frac{1}{2}\right) + (-2) = \frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 4t}} - 1\right)\), restoring the exact diagonal \((0, 3, 9, 30, \dots)\)!

Two Universal Remedies:

  1. Remedy A (Polynomial Division in \(y\) First): Before applying Furstenberg’s residue formula, perform Euclidean division of \(A(x, y)\) by \(B(x, y)\) as polynomials in \(y\): \[\frac{A(x, y)}{B(x, y)} = P_{\text{poly}}(x, y) + \frac{R(x, y)}{B(x, y)}, \qquad \deg_y R < \deg_y B.\] Extract \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y} P_{\text{poly}}(x, y) = \sum_{n} ([x^n y^n] P_{\text{poly}}(x, y)) t^n\) directly term-by-term from the finite polynomial \(P_{\text{poly}}(x, y)\), and apply Theorem 16.2 to the strictly proper fraction \(\frac{R(x, y)}{B(x, y)}\). (In our example, \(\frac{x + 2y}{1 - x - y} = -2 + \frac{2 - x}{1 - x - y}\) where \(\deg_y(2 - x) = 0 < 1 = \deg_y(1 - x - y)\)!)
  2. Remedy B (Include \(\operatorname{Res}_{z = 0}\) Explicitly): Keep \(z^{-(m - d + 1)}\) inside the residue integral and evaluate \(\operatorname{Res}_{z = 0} + \sum_{\operatorname{val}_t(z_i) > 0} \operatorname{Res}_{z = z_i(t)}\).

Lipshitz’s Theorem for 3D+ Diagonals: Why 3D Diagonals Leave the Algebraic World Yet Stay D-Finite

Does Furstenberg’s Theorem extend to three variables \(x, y, z\)? That is, if \(R(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{Q}[[x, y, z]]\) is a 3D rational power series, is its main diagonal \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x, y, z} R(x, y, z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} r_{n, n, n} t^n\) still an algebraic power series over \(\mathbb{Q}(t)\)?

No—not in characteristic \(0\)! Consider the simplest possible 3D rational generating function, the trinomial generator

\[R(x, y, z) = \frac{1}{1 - (x + y + z)} = \sum_{a, b, c \ge 0} \frac{(a + b + c)!}{a! \, b! \, c!} x^a y^b z^c.\]

Its main diagonal is the sequence of central trinomial / hexagonal lattice numbers

\[D_3(t) := \operatorname{Diag}_{x, y, z}\!\left(\frac{1}{1 - (x + y + z)}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(3n)!}{(n!)^3} t^n = 1 + 6t + 90t^2 + 1680t^3 + 34650t^4 + \dots\]

Why is \(D_3(t)\) transcendental over \(\mathbb{C}(t)\)?

  1. Asymptotic Proof via Stirling’s Formula: Applying Stirling’s approximation \(m! \sim \sqrt{2\pi m} (m / e)^m\) to \(u_n = \frac{(3n)!}{(n!)^3}\) gives \[u_n = \frac{(3n)!}{(n!)^3} \sim \frac{\sqrt{2\pi (3n)} (3n / e)^{3n}}{\big(\sqrt{2\pi n} (n / e)^n\big)^3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{27^n}{n}.\] By the Flajolet–Odlyzko Transfer Theorem (and Puiseux’s Theorem for algebraic functions), the coefficients of any algebraic series \(F(t) \in \mathbb{Q}[[t]]\) have dominant asymptotic growth of the form \(c \cdot \rho^n n^{-\alpha}\) where the exponent \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\) can never be a positive integer \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) (because a Puiseux branch \((1 - \rho t)^{\alpha - 1}\) with \(\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) is a smooth polynomial with \(0\) asymptotic contribution, while \(n^{-1}\) requires a logarithmic singularity \(-\ln(1 - 27t)\)!). Because \(u_n \sim \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi} 27^n n^{-1}\) has exponent \(\alpha = 1\), \(D_3(t)\) has a logarithmic singularity at \(t = 1/27\) and is transcendental over \(\mathbb{Q}(t)\).
  2. Lipshitz’s Theorem (1988) — Every \(d\)-Dimensional Rational Diagonal is D-Finite (P-Recursive): Even though \(D_3(t)\) is not algebraic, Leonard Lipshitz (1988) proved that for any number of variables \(d \ge 2\), the diagonal \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x_1, \dots, x_d} \frac{A(x_1, \dots, x_d)}{B(x_1, \dots, x_d)}\) of a rational power series is always D-Finite (holonomic)—meaning it satisfies a linear differential equation with polynomial coefficients in \(t\), and its coefficients \(u_n\) satisfy a bounded-order P-recursion with polynomial coefficients in \(n\)! For \(u_n = \frac{(3n)!}{(n!)^3}\), the consecutive ratio is \(\frac{u_n}{u_{n-1}} = \frac{3n(3n-1)(3n-2)}{n^3} = \frac{3(3n-1)(3n-2)}{n^2}\), giving the first-order P-recursion \[n^2 u_n - 3(3n - 1)(3n - 2) u_{n-1} = 0 \iff t(27t - 1) D_3''(t) + (54t - 1) D_3'(t) + 6 D_3(t) = 0,\] which computes \(u_0, u_1, \dots, u_N \bmod p\) in \(O(N)\) time!
  3. Characteristic \(p > 0\) Twist (Deligne 1984 / Christol): Remarkably, if we reduce the coefficients of a \(d\)-dimensional rational series modulo a prime \(p\), the diagonal \(\operatorname{Diag}_{x_1, \dots, x_d} R(\mathbf{x}) \bmod p\) becomes algebraic again over \(\mathbb{F}_p(t)\) for every dimension \(d \ge 2\), and its \(N\)-th coefficient for \(N \le 10^{18}\) can be extracted in \(O(p^d \log_p N)\) time via Regime 4 (Frobenius sectioning)!

Stepping Stone 1 (#048): Don’t Step on the Mines! — AtCoder ARC 012 D

  • Topic Tags: Laurent Ring \((K[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}])\), \(45^\circ\) Diagonal Coordinate Rotation, Composite Modulus Sieve.

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given \(N\) independent 2D lattice walks on \(\mathbb{Z}^2\), a step count \(T\), and an arbitrary (not necessarily prime) modulus \(M\). For each \(i = 1, 2, \dots, N\), the \(i\)-th walk starts at \((0, 0)\) and must end at \((x_i, y_i)\) after exactly \(T\) steps, where each step moves by one unit in one of the four cardinal directions:

\[\mathcal{S} = \{(+1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, +1), (0, -1)\}.\]

Let \(W_i\) be the number of valid \(T\)-step trajectories from \((0, 0)\) to \((x_i, y_i)\). Compute the product of \(W_i\) over all \(N\) walks modulo \(M\):

\[\prod_{i=1}^{N} W_i \bmod M.\]

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 10^5\), \(1 \le T \le 10^5\), \(|x_i|, |y_i| \le T\), \(1 \le M \le 10^9\) (\(M\) is not guaranteed to be prime). Required complexity: \(O(N + T)\) time and \(O(T)\) space.
  • Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(T = 2, N = 1, (x_1, y_1) = (1, 1)\)): After \(T = 2\) steps, \((1, 1)\) can be reached via \((0,0) \to (1,0) \to (1,1)\) or \((0,0) \to (0,1) \to (1,1)\), so \(W_1 = 2\). Indeed, \(\binom{2}{(2+1+1)/2} \binom{2}{(2+1-1)/2} = \binom{2}{2}\binom{2}{1} = 2\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Each unit step in \(\mathcal{S} = \{(\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\}\) corresponds to a term in the bivariate Laurent step polynomial

\[S(x, y) = x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} \in \mathbb{Z}[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}].\]

The number of \(T\)-step walks ending at \((X, Y)\) is the Laurent coefficient \(W = [x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^T\). Why is expanding \((x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1})^T\) directly by the multinomial theorem too slow (\(O(T)\) per query)? Because the four terms are added together! Look for a coordinate change that factors \(S(x, y)\) into a product of two binomials.

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Put \(S(x, y)\) over a common denominator \(x y\):

\[x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} = \frac{x^2 y + y + x y^2 + x}{x y} = \frac{(x + y)(1 + x y)}{x y}.\]

Raise this factored form to the \(T\)-th power:

\[[x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^T = [x^{T + X} y^{T + Y}] (1 + x y)^T (x + y)^T.\]

Expand \((1 + x y)^T = \sum_{a=0}^T \binom{T}{a} x^a y^a\) and \((x + y)^T = \sum_{b=0}^T \binom{T}{b} x^b y^{T - b}\), and solve the \(2 \times 2\) linear system \(a + b = T + X\) and \(a + T - b = T + Y\) for \((a, b)\).

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Composite Modulus Contract

Solving for \((a, b)\) gives \(a = \frac{T + X + Y}{2}\) and \(b = \frac{T + X - Y}{2}\), so \(W_i = \binom{T}{a_i} \binom{T}{b_i}\) whenever \(|X| + |Y| \le T\) and \(X + Y \equiv T \pmod 2\) (and \(W_i = 0\) otherwise). Since \(M \le 10^9\) is not necessarily prime, you cannot divide by factorials modulo \(M\)! Instead, express the entire product \(\prod_{i=1}^N \binom{T}{a_i} \binom{T}{b_i}\) as a product of factorials \(\prod_{k=1}^T (k!)^{e_k}\), sweep \(k\) from \(T\) down to \(2\) to convert factorial exponents into integer exponents \(\prod_{k=2}^T k^{c_k}\), and push composite bases \(k = p \cdot (k/p)\) down to primes using a linear sieve in \(O(T)\) time!

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Laurent Step Generator & Diagonal Rotation: In the Laurent polynomial ring \(\mathbb{Z}[x, x^{-1}, y, y^{-1}]\), one step of the walk multiplies the position monomial \(x^u y^v\) by \(S(x, y) = x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\). Factoring \(S(x, y)\) by grouping terms with \(x\) and \(y\) gives: \[\begin{aligned} S(x, y) &= x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} && \text{(cardinal step generator)} \\ &= x^{-1} y^{-1} (x^2 y + y + x y^2 + x) && \text{(factor out } (xy)^{-1} \text{)} \\ &= (x y)^{-1} (1 + x y)(x + y) && \text{(factor } (1 + xy)(x + y) \text{)}. \end{aligned}\] (Geometrically, setting \(u = x y\) and \(v = x / y\) rotates the 2D lattice by \(45^\circ\) so that each cardinal step \((dx, dy) \in \{(\pm 1, 0), (0, \pm 1)\}\) changes \(x + y\) by \(\pm 1\) and \(x - y\) by \(\pm 1\) independently!)

  2. Extracting \([x^X y^Y] S(x, y)^T\): Raising \(S(x, y)\) to the \(T\)-th power and expanding the two binomial factors yields: \[\begin{aligned} W_i = [x^{x_i} y^{y_i}] S(x, y)^T &= [x^{T + x_i} y^{T + y_i}] (1 + x y)^T (x + y)^T \\ &= [x^{T + x_i} y^{T + y_i}] \sum_{a=0}^{T} \sum_{b=0}^{T} \binom{T}{a} \binom{T}{b} x^{a + b} y^{a + T - b}. \end{aligned}\] A pair \((a, b)\) matches the target exponents \((T + x_i, T + y_i)\) if and only if \[\begin{cases} a + b = T + x_i, \\ a - b = y_i \end{cases} \iff a_i = \frac{T + x_i + y_i}{2}, \qquad b_i = \frac{T + x_i - y_i}{2}.\]

    • Boundary Feasibility Check: If \(T + x_i + y_i\) is odd, or if \(a_i \notin [0, T]\), or if \(b_i \notin [0, T]\) (equivalently \(|x_i| + |y_i| > T\)), then \(W_i = 0\), and the entire product \(\prod_{i=1}^N W_i \bmod M\) is immediately \(0\).
    • Otherwise, \(W_i = \binom{T}{a_i} \binom{T}{b_i}\).

  3. Division-Free Prime-Exponent Accumulation Modulo Composite \(M\): Writing \(\binom{T}{a_i} \binom{T}{b_i} = \frac{(T!)^2}{a_i! (T - a_i)! b_i! (T - b_i)!}\), we initialize an array \(E[0 \dots T] = 0\). For each walk \(i = 1, \dots, N\), we add \(+2\) to \(E[T]\) and subtract \(1\) from \(E[a_i]\), \(E[T - a_i]\), \(E[b_i]\), and \(E[T - b_i]\).

    • Next, since \(k! = k \cdot (k - 1)!\), a downward suffix sum \(c_k = \sum_{j=k}^T E[j]\) converts \(\prod_{k=1}^T (k!)^{E[k]}\) into \(\prod_{k=2}^T k^{c_k}\).
    • Finally, using the smallest prime factor spf[k] precomputed via a linear sieve up to \(T\), we iterate \(k\) from \(T\) down to \(2\): if spf[k] < k, we add \(c_k\) to both \(c_{\text{spf}[k]}\) and \(c_{k / \text{spf}[k]}\) and set \(c_k = 0\). Because the true product \(\prod_{i=1}^N W_i\) is an integer, every remaining prime \(p \le T\) is guaranteed to have a non-negative final exponent \(c_p \ge 0\), so we multiply \(\prod_{p \le T} p^{c_p} \bmod M\) via binary exponentiation with zero modular divisions!

Algorithm Contract

  • Input & Preconditions: Number of walks \(1 \le N \le 10^5\), step count \(0 \le T \le 10^5\), target coordinates \((x_i, y_i) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\) for \(1 \le i \le N\), and arbitrary modulus \(2 \le M \le 10^9\); no coprimality precondition on \(M\) (division-free prime-exponent accumulation handles composite \(M\) with \(\gcd(k!, M) > 1\)).
  • Postconditions (Output): Returns \(\prod_{i=1}^N W_i \bmod M\), where \(W_i = [x^{x_i} y^{y_i}] (x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1})^T\).
  • Index & Truncation Boundaries: Rotated step counts are \(a_i = (T + x_i + y_i)/2 \in [0, T]\) and \(b_i = (T + x_i - y_i)/2 \in [0, T]\); prime-exponent array \(c[0 \dots T]\) is pushed down from \(k = T\) down to \(2\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N + T)\) time and \(O(T)\) space.
  • Loop / Algebraic Invariant: After the downward suffix sum and composite-to-prime SPF pushdown from \(k = T\) down to \(2\), every prime \(p \le T\) holds its exact net exponent \(c_p = v_p\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^N \binom{T}{a_i}\binom{T}{b_i}\right) \ge 0\).
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: If any walk \(i\) violates \(|x_i| + |y_i| \le T\) or \((x_i + y_i) \equiv T \pmod 2\), then \(W_i = 0\) and the entire product is immediately \(0\) (must short-circuit before indexing negative factorials \(a_i < 0\) or \(T - a_i < 0\)); at \(T = 0\), \((x_i, y_i) = (0, 0)\) gives \(W_i = 1 \bmod M\).

Stepping Stone 2 (#129): Timber — Codeforces 1821F

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

On the integer segment \([0, n]\), you want to plant \(m\) trees of height \(k\) at strictly increasing integer coordinates \(1 \le x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_m \le n\). A set of positions \(\{x_1, \dots, x_m\}\) is called valid if there exists at least one choice of directions \(d_i \in \{\text{Left}, \text{Right}\}\) for \(i = 1, \dots, m\) such that:

  • If tree \(i\) falls Left, it occupies the closed interval \([x_i - k, x_i]\); if it falls Right, it occupies \([x_i, x_i + k]\).
  • All \(m\) felled intervals lie completely inside \([0, n]\).
  • No two felled intervals share even a single endpoint (they are pairwise disjoint as closed intervals).

Compute the number of valid sets of tree positions \(\{x_1, \dots, x_m\}\) modulo \(998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le n, m, k \le 3 \times 10^5\), modulus \(p = 998244353\). Required complexity: \(O(n + m)\) time and \(O(n + m)\) space.
  • Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(n = 4, m = 1, k = 2\)): For \(m = 1\) tree of height \(k = 2\) on \([0, 4]\): \(x_1 = 1\) can fall Right (\([1, 3]\)); \(x_1 = 2\) can fall Left (\([0, 2]\)) or Right (\([2, 4]\)); \(x_1 = 3\) can fall Left (\([1, 3]\)); \(x_1 = 4\) can fall Left (\([2, 4]\)). Thus all \(4\) positions \(\{1\}, \{2\}, \{3\}, \{4\}\) are valid (notice \(x_1 = 2\) has \(2\) valid orientations, but is counted once as a position set!), so the answer is \(4\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Notice that there are \(n + 1\) integer points \(\{0, 1, \dots, n\}\), and each felled tree covers \(k + 1\) consecutive integer points (either \(\{x_i - k, \dots, x_i\}\) or \(\{x_i, \dots, x_i + k\}\)). Why can’t we just count all valid pairs of (positions, orientations)? Because as seen at \(x_1 = 2\) in the sample trace, a single position set can admit multiple valid orientation assignments! Specifically, when can a tree have a choice between falling Left and falling Right?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

To count each valid position set \(\{x_1, \dots, x_m\}\) exactly once, assign each valid position set its canonical (lexicographically leftmost) orientation: push every tree to fall Left \([x_i - k, x_i]\) whenever possible, and only let tree \(i\) fall Right \([x_i, x_i + k]\) if falling Left is blocked because \(x_i - k\) is already occupied (either \(x_i - k < 0\) or the previous felled interval ends in \([x_i - k, x_i - 1]\)). Equivalently, a Left-felled tree is a block of \(k + 1\) integer points (\(x^{k+1}\)), and a Right-felled tree \([x_i, x_i + k]\) is only canonical if there are strictly fewer than \(k\) empty integer points immediately to its left—meaning we add \(2 x^{k+1}\) (Left or Right) and subtract \(x^{2k+1}\) (a Right-felled tree preceded by \(\ge k\) empty points, which could have fallen Left instead)! Set up the bivariate OGF \(F(x, y)\) where \(x\) marks the \(n\) spaces/points and \(y\) marks the \(m\) trees.

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

There are \(n + 1\) integer points in \([0, n]\), so if we pad one extra point at the end, each tree block plus its trailing separator uses \(x^{k+1}(2 - x^k)\), and each unused point contributes \(x\), giving

\[\text{Ans} = [x^n y^m] \frac{1}{1 - x - y x^{k+1}(2 - x^k)}.\]

Apply the Primary-Variable Selection Rule (主元法) from §16.1: because the denominator is linear in \(y\), extract \([y^m]\) first to get \(\frac{x^{m(k+1)}(2 - x^k)^m}{(1 - x)^{m+1}}\), and expand \((2 - x^k)^m\) via the binomial theorem in \(O(m)\) time!

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Canonical Orientation to Avoid Overcounting Position Sets: Every felled tree covers \(k + 1\) consecutive integer points in \(\{0, 1, \dots, n\}\), with the tree root located either at the right endpoint of its interval (if felled Left: \([x_i - k, x_i]\)) or at the left endpoint of its interval (if felled Right: \([x_i, x_i + k]\)). Suppose we fix the \(m\) disjoint intervals of length \(k + 1\) inside \(\{0, 1, \dots, n\}\) and choose for each interval whether its root is at its right endpoint (\(L\)) or left endpoint (\(R\)). When do two different choices of intervals/endpoints produce the same root position \(x_i\)? Only when interval \(i\) has its root at its left endpoint \(x_i\) (so it occupies \([x_i, x_i + k]\)), and all \(k\) points \(\{x_i - k, \dots, x_i - 1\}\) immediately to its left are uncovered! In that case, tree \(i\) could instead fall Left into \([x_i - k, x_i]\) without colliding with anything to its left. Therefore, to make the representation unique (canonical), we allow every tree block of \(k + 1\) points two choices of root endpoint (\(2 x^{k+1}\)), and forbid a Right-oriented block (\(x^{k+1}\)) from being immediately preceded by \(k\) uncovered points (\(x^k\)) by subtracting a fused forbidden block of size \((k + 1) + k = 2k + 1\) points (\(-x^{2k+1}\)).

  2. Bivariate Rational OGF: Notice that if we shift the coordinate system by associating each tree block of \(k + 1\) integer points with \(k\) of the \(n\) unit segments (plus \(1\) separator point between trees, so \(m\) trees require \(m(k+1) - 1\) segments, i.e., \([x^{n+1}]\) of \(\frac{x}{1 - x - y(2x^{k+1} - x^{2k+1})} = [x^n] \frac{1}{1 - x - y x^{k+1}(2 - x^k)}\)), the bivariate OGF is: \[F(x, y) = \frac{1}{(1 - x) - y x^{k+1}(2 - x^k)} \in \mathbb{F}_p[y][[x]].\]

  3. Extracting \([y^m]\) First (主元法): Following the Primary-Variable Selection Rule (§16.1), since the denominator is linear in \(y\), we extract \([y^m]\) first: \[\begin{aligned} [y^m] F(x, y) &= \frac{x^{m(k+1)} (2 - x^k)^m}{(1 - x)^{m+1}} && \text{(geometric series in } y \text{)} \\ &= x^{m(k+1)} \left( \sum_{j=0}^{m} (-1)^j \binom{m}{j} 2^{m - j} x^{j k} \right) \left( \sum_{r=0}^{\infty} \binom{m + r}{m} x^r \right) && \text{(binomial expansions)}. \end{aligned}\] Now extract \([x^n]\) by setting \(m(k + 1) + j k + r = n \iff r = n - m(k + 1) - j k\): \[\text{Ans} = [x^n y^m] F(x, y) = \sum_{j=0}^{m} (-1)^j \binom{m}{j} 2^{m - j} \binom{n - m k - j k}{m} \pmod{998244353},\] where \(\binom{A}{m} = 0\) whenever \(A < m\).

      • \(j = 1\): \((-1)^1 \binom{1}{1} 2^0 \binom{4 - 2 - 2}{1} = -1 \cdot \binom{0}{1} = 0\).
      • Total: \(4 + 0 = 4\).

Intermediate 1 (#025): Teleporting Takahashi — AtCoder ABC 240 G

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Takahashi is in 3D space \(\mathbb{Z}^3\), initially at \((0, 0, 0)\). In one teleportation step, he moves by \(\pm 1\) along exactly one of the three coordinate axes:

\[\mathcal{S}_3 = \{(\pm 1, 0, 0), (0, \pm 1, 0), (0, 0, \pm 1)\}.\]

Count the number of \(N\)-step trajectories starting at \((0, 0, 0)\) and ending at \((X, Y, Z)\), modulo \(998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 10^7\), \(|X|, |Y|, |Z| \le 10^7\), modulus \(p = 998244353\). Required complexity: \(O(N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space.
  • Hand-Checkable Sample Trace (\(N = 3, (X, Y, Z) = (2, 0, -1)\)): To reach \((2, 0, -1)\) in \(3\) steps, Takahashi must take two \(+x\) steps and one \(-z\) step, which can be ordered in \(\frac{3!}{2! 1!} = 3\) ways.
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

The 3D step Laurent polynomial is

\[S_3(x, y, z) = x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} + z + z^{-1},\]

and we want \([x^X y^Y z^Z] S_3(x, y, z)^N\). Why can’t we factor \(S_3(x, y, z)\) into three linear factors the way we factored \(S_2(x, y) = x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1} = (xy)^{-1}(1+xy)(x+y)\) in Stepping Stone 1? Because in 3D, a cross-polytope (octahedron) has \(6\) vertices, whereas a product of \(3\) binomials has \(2^3 = 8\) vertices! Since 3D cannot factor completely, how can we combine 1D binomial expansion with our 2D factorization?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Split \(S_3(x, y, z)\) into the 1D \(z\)-step generator \(z + z^{-1}\) and the 2D \((x, y)\)-step generator \(S_2(x, y) = x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\):

\[S_3(x, y, z)^N = \Big( (z + z^{-1}) + (x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}) \Big)^N = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \binom{N}{k} \big(z + z^{-1}\big)^k \big(x + x^{-1} + y + y^{-1}\big)^{N - k}.\]

Notice that \(z\) only appears in the first factor and \((x, y)\) only appear in the second factor!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

For each fixed number of \(z\)-steps \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, N\}\):

  • The \(z\)-factor contributes \([z^Z](z + z^{-1})^k = \binom{k}{(k + Z)/2}\) (when \(k \ge |Z|\) and \(k \equiv Z \pmod 2\)).
  • By Stepping Stone 1 (#048), the \((x, y)\)-factor of length \(m = N - k\) contributes \([x^X y^Y] S_2(x, y)^m = \binom{m}{(m + X + Y)/2} \binom{m}{(m + X - Y)/2}\) (when \(m \ge |X| + |Y|\) and \(m \equiv X + Y \pmod 2\)). Precompute factorials up to \(N = 10^7\) and sum over \(k\) in \(O(N)\) time!
NoteComplete Derivation & Solution
