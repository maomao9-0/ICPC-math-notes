13 — Graphical Generating Functions, Eulerian/Bipartite Graphs, DAGs, SCCs & Matrix-Tree

Part III · Online Algorithms, Implicit Equations & Graphical Enumeration

Learning Objectives & Chapter Roadmap

In Chapter 4 and Chapter 12, we counted labeled trees, functional graphs, and cacti by exploiting a critical structural property: removing a vertex or contracting a cycle splits a tree-like object into independent sub-components with no leftover cross-edges between them. Once we step beyond trees and cacti into dense graph families—arbitrary simple graphs, Eulerian graphs, bipartite graphs, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), and strongly connected digraphs (SCCs)—that independence vanishes. When a vertex set of size \(n\) is partitioned into two parts of sizes \(k\) and \(n-k\), there are \(k(n-k)\) potential cross-edges between the parts, introducing a multiplicative interaction factor of \(2^{k(n-k)}\) (or \((1+w)^{k(n-k)}\) when edges carry weight \(w\)). Because \(k(n-k)\) couples \(k\) and \(n-k\) nonlinearly in the exponent, a naive exponential generating function (EGF) convolution fails.

At the same time, when the underlying host graph is not the complete graph \(K_n\)—or when each potential edge \((u, v)\) carries an arbitrary weight \(w_{uv}\)—global generating functions over \(n\) must give way to algebraic graph theory: encoding spanning trees, arborescences, and Eulerian circuits as determinants of Laplacian matrices over fields and truncated polynomial rings \(K[x]/(x^{k+1})\).

By the end of this chapter, you will master two complementary engines for graphical enumeration:

  1. Graphical Generating Functions & The Chirp-\(z\) Twist (§13.1–§13.3):
    • Unpack the \(\operatorname{SET}\) bijection \(\mathcal{G} = \operatorname{SET}(\mathcal{C})\) via formal logarithm and exponential (\(\ln/\exp\)) to count connected simple labeled graphs and connected Eulerian graphs (via the \((n-1)\)-vertex parity-completion bijection).
    • Linearize the cross-edge interaction factor \(2^{k(n-k)}\) using Bluestein’s quadratic identity \(k(n-k) = \binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2} - \binom{n-k}{2}\) from Chapter 7, defining the Chirp-twisted (graphical) EGF \(\hat{A}(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n 2^{-\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}\).
    • Count labeled bipartite graphs and connected bipartite graphs by passing through properly \(2\)-colored graphs \(B_2(x)\), dividing by \(2\) in the connected logarithm domain (\(C_{\text{bip}}(x) = \frac{1}{2}\ln B_2(x)\)), and re-exponentiating (\(B(x) = \sqrt{B_2(x)}\)).
    • Count labeled DAGs and strongly connected digraphs (SCCs) via Robinson–Stanley source-layer inclusion–exclusion and the signed source-SCC cluster series \(F(x) = 1 - \exp(-C_{\text{SCC}}(x))\).
  2. Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem, BEST Theorem & Symbolic Determinants (§13.4):
    • Derive Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem for undirected graphs and directed arborescences (in-trees and out-trees) from incidence-matrix Cauchy–Binet expansion and cycle cancellation.
    • Count directed Eulerian circuits via the BEST Theorem (de Bruijn, van Aardenne-Ehrenfest, Smith, and Tutte) using the last-exit arborescence bijection.
    • Apply the Rank-1 Matrix-Tree Determinant Lemma (\(\det(A + uv^T)\)) to prove the Generalized Cayley Forest Formula \(n^{k-2}\prod_{i=1}^k s_i\) and accelerate tree-intersection counting.
    • Lift edge weights into truncated polynomial quotient rings \(K[x]/(x^{k+1})\) (Symbolic Matrix-Tree) to extract the sum of edge weights, variance, or \(k\)-th power of total weight across all spanning trees in \(O(n^3 k^2)\) or \(O(n^4)\) via Lagrange interpolation.

13.1 Graphical Generating Functions & Connected Simple / Eulerian Graphs

Counting Simple Labeled Graphs and Connected Simple Graphs

Before writing generating functions, let us name the concrete combinatorial objects we are counting. Fix a vertex set \(V = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) of \(n\) distinct, labeled vertices. A simple labeled undirected graph on \(V\) is a choice of subset of the \(\binom{n}{2}\) unordered vertex pairs \(\{\{u, v\} : 1 \le u < v \le n\}\) to serve as edges. Because each of the \(\binom{n}{2}\) potential edges is independently either present or absent, the total number of simple labeled graphs on \(n\) vertices is

\[ g_n = 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \quad (n \ge 0), \qquad \text{with } \binom{0}{2} = \binom{1}{2} = 0 \implies g_0 = 1,\; g_1 = 1. \]

Now we ask our first nontrivial question: How many of these \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) graphs on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) are connected? Let \(c_n\) denote the number of connected simple labeled graphs on \(n\) vertices (with \(c_0 = 0\) by convention, since an connected component must be non-empty).

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

  • The Diagnostic Signal: Every simple labeled graph \(G\) on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) decomposes uniquely into an unordered collection (\(\operatorname{SET}\)) of non-empty connected components \(\mathcal{C}\) whose vertex sets partition \(\{1, \dots, n\}\), with no edges allowed between distinct components.
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: By the Exponential Formula for labeled \(\operatorname{SET}\) constructions (Chapter 4), the EGF of all graphs \(G(x)\) and the EGF of connected graphs \(C(x)\) are related by \(G(x) = \exp(C(x))\), so taking the formal logarithm \(C(x) = \ln G(x)\) immediately isolates connected graphs!

Single-Step Verbalized Derivation

Define the exponential generating functions in \(K[[x]]\) (where \(K\) is a field of characteristic \(p > N\), such as \(\mathbb{F}_{998244353}\)):

\[ G(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}, \qquad C(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \frac{x^n}{n!}. \]

Step by step:

  1. Component-Set Bijection: A labeled graph on \(n\) vertices with \(r\) connected components corresponds uniquely to an unordered partition of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(r\) non-empty blocks \(V_1, \dots, V_r\), together with a choice of a connected labeled graph on each block \(V_i\).
  2. EGF Translation: In \(K[[x]]\), choosing \(r\) labeled connected components of positive size and dividing by \(r!\) to forget component ordering yields \(\frac{1}{r!} C(x)^r\).
  3. Summing over all component counts \(r \ge 0\):

\[ G(x) = \sum_{r=0}^{\infty} \frac{C(x)^r}{r!} = \exp(C(x)). \]

  1. Algebraic Inversion via Formal Logarithm: Since \([x^0]G(x) = g_0 = 1\) and \([x^0]C(x) = 0\) (\(\operatorname{val}(C) \ge 1\)), the formal logarithm \(\ln G(x)\) is well-defined up to \(x^N\) whenever \(p > N\) (so that \(1, 2, \dots, N\) are units in \(K\)):

\[ C(x) = \ln G(x) \pmod{x^{N+1}}, \qquad c_n = n! \, [x^n] \ln G(x). \]

Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 4\))

Let us verify \(C(x) = \ln G(x)\) by hand up to \(n = 4\):

  • Total graphs: \(g_0 = 1\), \(g_1 = 2^0 = 1\), \(g_2 = 2^1 = 2\), \(g_3 = 2^3 = 8\), \(g_4 = 2^6 = 64\).
  • Thus \(G(x) = 1 + x + \frac{2}{2!}x^2 + \frac{8}{3!}x^3 + \frac{64}{4!}x^4 + O(x^5) = 1 + x + x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3 + \frac{8}{3}x^4 + O(x^5)\).
  • Write \(G(x) = 1 + u\) with \(u = x + x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3 + \frac{8}{3}x^4 + O(x^5)\) and expand \(\ln(1+u) = u - \frac{u^2}{2} + \frac{u^3}{3} - \frac{u^4}{4}\):
    • \(u = x + x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3 + \frac{8}{3}x^4 + O(x^5)\)
    • \(-\frac{1}{2}u^2 = -\frac{1}{2}x^2 - x^3 - \frac{11}{6}x^4 + O(x^5)\)
    • \(+\frac{1}{3}u^3 = \frac{1}{3}x^3 + x^4 + O(x^5)\)
    • \(-\frac{1}{4}u^4 = -\frac{1}{4}x^4 + O(x^5)\)
  • Summing coefficient by coefficient:

\[ C(x) = x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{2}{3}x^3 + \frac{19}{12}x^4 + O(x^5). \]

Multiplying \([x^n]C(x)\) by \(n!\) gives:

  • \(c_1 = 1! \cdot 1 = 1\) (the single isolated vertex),
  • \(c_2 = 2! \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1\) (the single edge \(K_2\)),
  • \(c_3 = 3! \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 4\) (\(3\) labeled trees \(P_3\) plus \(1\) triangle \(K_3\)),
  • \(c_4 = 4! \cdot \frac{19}{12} = 38\) (all \(64\) graphs on \(4\) vertices minus: \(1\) empty graph, \(\binom{4}{2}=6\) one-edge graphs, \(3\) two-disjoint-edge matchings, \(\binom{4}{3}\cdot 3 = 12\) graphs consisting of a \(3\)-vertex tree plus an isolated vertex, and \(\binom{4}{3}\cdot 1 = 4\) graphs consisting of a triangle \(K_3\) plus an isolated vertex: \(64 - (1 + 6 + 3 + 12 + 4) = 38\)).

Connected Eulerian Graphs via Parity-Completion Bijection

A simple labeled undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) is Eulerian (admits a closed trail traversing every edge of \(G\) exactly once) if and only if \(G\) is connected (assuming we require all vertices of \(V\) to be visited; i.e., no isolated vertices for \(n \ge 2\)) and every vertex \(v \in V\) has even degree.

The Diagnostic Signal

Notice how the two conditions for an Eulerian graph decouple cleanly:

  1. Local Vertex Constraint: Every vertex \(v \in V\) has even degree (\(\deg(v) \equiv 0 \pmod 2\)). If a graph \(G\) is a disjoint union of connected components \(C_1, \dots, C_r\), then every vertex of \(G\) has even degree if and only if every vertex in each component \(C_i\) has even degree!
  2. Global Connectivity Constraint: \(G\) consists of a single connected component.

Because the even-degree property is preserved under disjoint union of components, the class \(\mathcal{G}_{\text{even}}\) of all labeled even-degree graphs is the \(\operatorname{SET}\) construction of the class \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{Euler}}\) of connected labeled even-degree (Eulerian) graphs:

\[ \mathcal{G}_{\text{even}} = \operatorname{SET}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{Euler}}) \implies C_{\text{Euler}}(x) = \ln G_{\text{even}}(x). \]

All that remains is to count \(g_{\text{even}, n} = |\mathcal{G}_{\text{even}, n}|\), the number of simple labeled graphs on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) in which every vertex has even degree.

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Parity Completion at Vertex \(n\))

How do we count graphs on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) (\(n \ge 1\)) where every vertex has even degree? Instead of choosing edges on all \(n\) vertices and filtering by parity, set aside vertex \(n\) as a parity corrector:

  1. On the first \(n-1\) vertices \(\{1, 2, \dots, n-1\}\), choose any simple graph \(H\) completely freely. There are \(\binom{n-1}{2}\) potential edges among \(\{1, \dots, n-1\}\), so there are \(2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\) choices for \(H\).
  2. Now we must decide which of the \(n-1\) potential edges \(\{v, n\}\) (\(1 \le v \le n-1\)) incident to vertex \(n\) to include so that every vertex ends up with even degree.
  3. For each vertex \(v \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}\), the edge \(\{v, n\}\) is the only remaining edge incident to \(v\). Thus our hand is completely forced:
    • If \(\deg_H(v)\) is odd, we must include the edge \(\{v, n\}\).
    • If \(\deg_H(v)\) is even, we must not include the edge \(\{v, n\}\).
  4. This uniquely determines all edges incident to \(n\), guaranteeing that \(\deg_G(v)\) is even for all \(v \in \{1, \dots, n-1\}\). What about vertex \(n\) itself? By the Handshaking Lemma, the sum of degrees in \(G\) is twice the number of edges, an even integer:

\[ \deg_G(n) = 2|E(G)| - \sum_{v=1}^{n-1} \deg_G(v) \equiv 0 - 0 = 0 \pmod 2. \]

Thus \(\deg_G(n)\) is automatically even! This establishes an exact bijection between even-degree graphs on \(n \ge 1\) vertices and arbitrary simple graphs on \(n-1\) vertices:

\[ g_{\text{even}, n} = 2^{\binom{n-1}{2}} \quad (n \ge 1), \qquad g_{\text{even}, 0} = 1. \]

Consequently, the EGF of connected labeled Eulerian graphs (#226 LOJ 6512) is simply

\[ G_{\text{even}}(x) = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{\binom{n-1}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}, \qquad C_{\text{Euler}}(x) = \ln G_{\text{even}}(x). \]

Checking \(n \le 4\): \(g_{\text{even}, 1} = 1, g_{\text{even}, 2} = 1, g_{\text{even}, 3} = 2, g_{\text{even}, 4} = 8\), so \(G_{\text{even}}(x) = 1 + x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 + \frac{1}{3}x^4 + O(x^5)\), and \(\ln G_{\text{even}}(x) = x + \frac{1}{6}x^3 + \frac{1}{8}x^4 + O(x^5)\), giving \(c_{\text{Euler}, 1} = 1\) (\(K_1\)), \(c_{\text{Euler}, 2} = 0\), \(c_{\text{Euler}, 3} = 1\) (\(K_3\)), and \(c_{\text{Euler}, 4} = 3\) (the \(3\) labeled \(4\)-cycles \(C_4\)).

Edge-Indicator Reweighting & The Chirp-\(z\) Graphical EGF Ring

What happens when we partition a vertex set of size \(n\) into two parts \(A\) and \(B\) of sizes \(k\) and \(n-k\), and there are \(2^{k(n-k)}\) (or more generally \(q^{k(n-k)}\) where \(q = 1 + w\)) ways to choose the cross-edges between \(A\) and \(B\)?

Sums of the form

\[ h_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} q^{k(n-k)} a_k b_{n-k} \qquad (q \in K^\times) \]

arise whenever two vertex subsets interact across a complete bipartite cut \(K_{k, n-k}\) (such as in \(2\)-colored bipartite graphs, source layers of DAGs, or source SCCs of digraphs).

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Bluestein Linearization)

In standard EGF multiplication, \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) splits cleanly into \(n!\) on the left and \(\frac{1}{k!}\cdot\frac{1}{(n-k)!}\) on the right. The obstacle here is the cross-term \(q^{k(n-k)}\), which couples \(k\) and \(n-k\). Just as we did for the Chirp-\(z\) Transform in Chapter 7, we decouple \(k(n-k)\) using the binomial quadratic identity:

\[ k(n-k) = \binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2} - \binom{n-k}{2}. \]

Provided \(q \in K^\times\) is invertible in our coefficient ring \(K\), we can factor the cross-edge weight into three independent terms depending solely on \(n\), \(k\), and \(n-k\):

\[ q^{k(n-k)} = q^{\binom{n}{2}} \cdot q^{-\binom{k}{2}} \cdot q^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}. \]

Dividing the entire convolution by \(q^{\binom{n}{2}} n!\) transforms the coupled sum into a standard Cauchy product in \(K[[x]]\):

\[ \frac{h_n}{q^{\binom{n}{2}} n!} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \left(\frac{a_k}{q^{\binom{k}{2}} k!}\right) \left(\frac{b_{n-k}}{q^{\binom{n-k}{2}} (n-k)!}\right). \]

ImportantDefinition: The Chirp-Twisted (Graphical / Eulerian) EGF

Given a base edge-choice multiplier \(q \in K^\times\) (most commonly \(q = 2\), or \(q = 1 + y\) when tracking edge counts with variable \(y\)), the \(q\)-twisted graphical EGF of a labeled sequence \((a_n)_{n \ge 0}\) is

\[ \hat{A}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{a_n}{q^{\binom{n}{2}} n!} x^n. \]

Under this twist, a cross-edge weighted binomial convolution \(h_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} q^{k(n-k)} a_k b_{n-k}\) becomes ordinary polynomial multiplication \(\hat{H}(x) = \hat{A}(x) \hat{B}(x)\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time! However, never confuse \(A(x)\) and \(\hat{A}(x)\):

  • Use the standard EGF \(A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n \frac{x^n}{n!}\) when components do not have edges between them (\(\operatorname{SET}/\ln/\exp\)).
  • Use the Chirp-twisted EGF \(\hat{A}(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{q^{\binom{n}{2}} n!} x^n\) when two parts have \(q^{k(n-k)}\) arbitrary cross-edges between them.
  • Convert back and forth between \(A(x)\) and \(\hat{A}(x)\) by point-wise coefficient scaling (Hadamard twist) by \(q^{\pm \binom{n}{2}}\) in \(O(N)\) time!
WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Fermat’s Little Theorem 3-Way Reduction on \(2^{\binom{n}{2}} \bmod p\)

The Pitfall: When computing \(2^{\binom{n}{2}} \bmod p\) for \(n \le 10^5\) or \(n \le 10^9\) (with \(p = 998244353\)), a common bug is writing binom_n_2 = 1LL * n * (n - 1) / 2 % MOD and passing binom_n_2 to qpow(2, binom_n_2). This produces wrong answers as soon as \(\binom{n}{2} \ge p\) (i.e., \(n \ge 44683\) for \(p = 998244353\))!

Why It Fails: In the finite field \(\mathbb{F}_p\), additive coefficients live modulo \(p\), whereas multiplicative exponents of units \(q \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\) live modulo \(\varphi(p) = p - 1\) by Fermat’s Little Theorem (\(q^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p\)). Reducing the exponent modulo \(p\) instead of \(p-1\) shifts the exponent by a multiple of \(1\) in \(\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\mathbb{Z}\).

The Remedy: Always reduce \(\binom{n}{2}\) modulo \(p - 1\):

long long exp_mod = (1LL * n * (n - 1) / 2) % (MOD - 1);
long long w_pos = qpow(2, exp_mod);                  // 2^{\binom{n}{2}} mod p
long long w_neg = qpow(2, (MOD - 1 - exp_mod) % (MOD - 1)); // 2^{-\binom{n}{2}} mod p

Even better, for \(n = 0, 1, \dots, N\), you can avoid binary exponentiation altogether by using the recurrence \(\binom{n}{2} = \binom{n-1}{2} + (n-1)\), precomputing \(2^n \bmod p\) and \(2^{-n} \bmod p\) in \(O(N)\) time, and maintaining \(2^{\pm\binom{n}{2}} \bmod p\) with a single multiplication per step!

NoteAlgorithm Contract: Connected Simple & Eulerian Graphs via Formal Logarithm & Chirp-\(z\) Twist
  • Input: Degree bound \(N \ge 1\) and prime modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Preconditions: \(p > N\); \(q \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\) (\(q = 2\) invertible modulo \(p\)); exponents \(\binom{n}{2}\) reduced modulo \(p - 1\); \([x^0] G(x) = [x^0] G_{\text{even}}(x) = 1\).
  • Postconditions: Outputs \(c_n = n! [x^n] \ln G(x) \bmod p\) (connected simple graphs) and \(c_{\text{Euler}, n} = n! [x^n] \ln G_{\text{even}}(x) \bmod p\) (connected Eulerian graphs) for all \(1 \le n \le N\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time (1 polynomial \(\ln\) call of degree \(N\) plus \(O(N)\) Chirp-\(z\) exponent precomputation) and \(O(N)\) space.
  • Core Invariant: \(\mathcal{G} = \operatorname{SET}(\mathcal{C}) \iff G(x) = \exp(C(x))\) in the standard EGF ring (zero cross-edges between components), while cross-edge convolutions \(q^{k(n-k)}\) factor as \(q^{\binom{n}{2}} q^{-\binom{k}{2}} q^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}\) in the Chirp-twisted EGF ring.

13.2 Bipartite Graphs & 2-Colored Hurwitz EGFs

Now let us ask our second fundamental graphical enumeration question (#199 Luogu P7364): How many simple labeled graphs on \(\{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) are bipartite, and how many are connected and bipartite?

The Diagnostic Signal: Why Direct Bipartition Counting Overcounts

A graph \(G = (V, E)\) is bipartite if its vertex set \(V\) admits a proper \(2\)-coloring \(\chi : V \to \{\text{Black}, \text{White}\}\) such that no edge connects two vertices of the same color.

Why can’t we simply choose a subset \(S \subseteq V\) of size \(k\) to be the Black vertices, let \(V \setminus S\) of size \(n-k\) be the White vertices, and choose any subset of the \(k(n-k)\) cross-edges between \(S\) and \(V \setminus S\) in \(2^{k(n-k)}\) ways?

Because that procedure counts properly \(2\)-colored graphs \((G, \chi)\), not underlying unlabeled-color bipartite graphs \(G\):

  • If a bipartite graph \(G\) is connected (and \(n \ge 1\)), fixing the color of one vertex in \(G\) uniquely forces the color of every other vertex in \(G\) along spanning tree paths. Since the first vertex can be colored either Black or White, every non-empty connected bipartite graph has exactly \(2\) proper \(2\)-colorings!
  • If a bipartite graph \(G\) has \(c\) connected components, each component can independently flip its Black/White color assignment, so \(G\) has \(2^c\) proper \(2\)-colorings!

Because \(2^c\) depends on the number of connected components \(c\), we cannot divide the total number of \(2\)-colored graphs \(b_n^{(2)}\) by a fixed constant to get \(b_n\). Instead, the \(\operatorname{SET}/\ln/\exp\) engine tells us where the factor of \(2\) is constant: inside the connected component domain!

The 3-Step 2-Colored Bridge (B_2 -> C_bip -> B)

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Let us trace the chain of generating functions from \(2\)-colored graphs to bipartite graphs:

  1. Step 1 — Count all properly \(2\)-colored labeled graphs \(B_2(x)\): A properly \(2\)-colored graph \((G, \chi)\) on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) is formed by choosing a subset \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\) of size \(k\) (\(0 \le k \le n\)) to be colored Black, coloring the remaining \(n-k\) vertices White, and choosing any subset of the \(k(n-k)\) potential edges between Black and White vertices:

\[ b_n^{(2)} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} 2^{k(n-k)}. \]

Applying our Chirp-\(z\) graphical EGF twist \(2^{k(n-k)} = 2^{\binom{n}{2}} 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} 2^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}\) from §13.1:

\[ \frac{b_n^{(2)}}{2^{\binom{n}{2}} n!} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \left(\frac{2^{-\binom{k}{2}}}{k!}\right) \left(\frac{2^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}}{(n-k)!}\right). \]

Therefore, if we define the Chirp-twisted EGF of an independent set as \(\hat{I}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{2^{-\binom{k}{2}}}{k!} x^k\), then

\[ \hat{B}_2(x) = \hat{I}(x)^2, \qquad [x^n] B_2(x) = \frac{b_n^{(2)}}{n!} = 2^{\binom{n}{2}} [x^n] \left(\hat{I}(x)^2\right). \]

(Note: If you also want to track the number of Black vertices \(a\) and White vertices \(b\) separately, the bivariate 2-colored Hurwitz EGF is \(B_2(x, y) = \sum_{a, b \ge 0} 2^{ab} \frac{x^a}{a!} \frac{y^b}{b!}\).)

  1. Step 2 — Pass to connected components via \(\ln\) and divide by \(2\): A properly \(2\)-colored graph \((G, \chi)\) is an unordered \(\operatorname{SET}\) of connected properly \(2\)-colored components. Thus \(B_2(x) = \exp(C_2(x))\), where \(C_2(x) = \ln B_2(x)\) is the EGF of connected \(2\)-colored graphs. Since every non-empty connected bipartite graph has exact multiplicity \(2\) among connected \(2\)-colored graphs (corresponding to swapping Black and White across the component), the EGF \(C_{\text{bip}}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_{\text{bip}, n} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) of connected labeled bipartite graphs is

\[ C_{\text{bip}}(x) = \frac{1}{2} C_2(x) = \frac{1}{2} \ln B_2(x). \]

  1. Step 3 — Re-exponentiate to count all labeled bipartite graphs \(B(x)\): Every labeled bipartite graph \(G\) is uniquely an unordered \(\operatorname{SET}\) of connected labeled bipartite graphs! Therefore, exponentiating \(C_{\text{bip}}(x)\) gives the EGF \(B(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n \frac{x^n}{n!}\) of all labeled bipartite graphs:

\[ B(x) = \exp\left(C_{\text{bip}}(x)\right) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{2} \ln B_2(x)\right) = \sqrt{B_2(x)}. \]

13.3 Acyclic Digraphs (DAGs) & Strongly Connected Digraphs (Robinson–Stanley Inclusion–Exclusion)

Now we move from undirected graphs to labeled directed graphs (digraphs). On a labeled vertex set \(V = \{1, \dots, n\}\), there are \(n(n-1) = 2\binom{n}{2}\) ordered pairs \((u, v)\) with \(u \neq v\). Thus:

  • If we allow any subset of the \(2\binom{n}{2}\) directed arcs (so between each pair \(\{u, v\}\) we can have no arc, \(u \to v\), \(v \to u\), or both \(u \leftrightarrow v\)), there are \(2^{2\binom{n}{2}} = 4^{\binom{n}{2}}\) labeled digraphs on \(n\) vertices.
  • If we forbid \(2\)-cycles \(u \leftrightarrow v\) (oriented simple graphs), there are \(3^{\binom{n}{2}}\) graphs; if we further require every pair \(\{u, v\}\) to have an arc (tournaments), there are \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) tournaments.

Counting Labeled Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs)

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) on \(V = \{1, \dots, n\}\) is a labeled digraph with no directed cycles (including no \(2\)-cycles \(u \leftrightarrow v\)). How do we count the number \(d_n\) of labeled DAGs on \(n\) vertices (#189 Luogu P6295)?

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Source-Layer Inclusion–Exclusion

  • The Diagnostic Signal: Why can’t we use \(\ln/\exp\) directly to build a DAG? Because a DAG is characterized by the absence of directed cycles, which is not a partition into independent components. Instead, the defining structural invariant of every non-empty finite DAG \(G = (V, E)\) is that its set of sources \(S(G) = \{v \in V : \operatorname{in-deg}_G(v) = 0\}\) is non-empty!
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: If we try to peel off a subset \(K \subseteq V\) of \(k \ge 1\) vertices to serve as sources, the remaining \(n-k\) vertices \(V \setminus K\) must induce a smaller DAG, and between any \(u \in K\) and \(w \in V \setminus K\) we may either place no arc or place the forward arc \(u \to w\) (\(2^{k(n-k)}\) choices; backward arcs \(w \to u\) are forbidden because \(K\) must have in-degree \(0\), and internal arcs inside \(K\) are forbidden for the same reason). However, peeling off \(K\) only guarantees that every vertex in \(K\) is a source (\(K \subseteq S(G)\))—some vertices in \(V \setminus K\) might also happen to receive no arcs from \(K\) and be sources of \(G\) as well! As we learned in Chapter 8, whenever we want \(S(G) \neq \emptyset\) and can easily count configurations where \(K \subseteq S(G)\), Inclusion–Exclusion over \(K \subseteq S(G)\) gives each non-empty subset \(K\) of size \(k = |K| \ge 1\) the weight \((-1)^{k-1}\), because for any DAG \(G\) with actual source set \(S(G)\) of size \(s = |S(G)| \ge 1\):

\[ \sum_{\emptyset \neq K \subseteq S(G)} (-1)^{|K|-1} = \sum_{k=1}^{s} \binom{s}{k} (-1)^{k-1} = 1 - (1 - 1)^s = 1. \]

Single-Step Verbalized Derivation (Robinson 1970, Stanley 1973)

Summing over all non-empty subsets \(K \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\) of size \(k \ge 1\) gives Robinson’s recurrence for \(d_n\) (\(n \ge 1\), with \(d_0 = 1\)):

\[ d_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} 2^{k(n-k)} d_{n-k} \quad (n \ge 1). \]

Move all terms to the left-hand side and include \(k = 0\):

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} 2^{k(n-k)} d_{n-k} = [n = 0]. \]

Look at the cross-edge weight \(2^{k(n-k)}\): it is the exact same Chirp-\(z\) kernel \(2^{k(n-k)} = 2^{\binom{n}{2}} 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} 2^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}\) from §13.1! Dividing both sides by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}} n!\) gives

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{n} \left((-1)^k \frac{2^{-\binom{k}{2}}}{k!}\right) \left(\frac{d_{n-k}}{2^{\binom{n-k}{2}} (n-k)!}\right) = [n = 0]. \]

In the Chirp-twisted EGF ring \(K[[x]]\), let \(\hat{D}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{d_n}{2^{\binom{n}{2}} n!} x^n\) and \(\hat{\Phi}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k \frac{2^{-\binom{k}{2}}}{k!} x^k\). Then \(\hat{\Phi}(x) \hat{D}(x) = 1\), which means:

\[ \hat{D}(x) = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (-1)^k \frac{2^{-\binom{k}{2}}}{k!} x^k\right)^{-1} \pmod{x^{N+1}}. \]

Once we compute \(\hat{D}(x)\) by a single polynomial inversion (inv) in \(O(N \log N)\) time, we untwist each coefficient by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) to get the ordinary EGF of labeled DAGs \(D(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} d_n \frac{x^n}{n!}\) where \(d_n = 2^{\binom{n}{2}} n! [x^n]\hat{D}(x)\). Finally, since a DAG is an unordered \(\operatorname{SET}\) of its weakly connected DAG components, the EGF of weakly connected labeled DAGs is simply

\[ C_{\text{DAG}}(x) = \ln D(x). \]

(Checking small values: \(\hat{\Phi}(x) = 1 - x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 - \frac{1}{8 \cdot 6}x^3 + \dots = 1 - x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 - \frac{1}{48}x^3 + \frac{1}{1536}x^4\), whose inverse \(\hat{D}(x) = 1 + x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{48}x^3 + \dots\) untwists to \(d_0=1, d_1=1, d_2=3, d_3=25, d_4=543\), and \(\ln D(x)\) gives \(c_{\text{DAG}, 1}=1, c_{\text{DAG}, 2}=2, c_{\text{DAG}, 3}=18, c_{\text{DAG}, 4}=446\).)

Using \((-1)^k\) on the right-hand side makes every DAG contribute \(-1\), so already at \(n = 2\) the recurrence produces \(d_2 = -2 \cdot 2 + 1 = -3\) instead of \(+3\)!

Strongly Connected Tournaments & Strongly Connected Digraphs (SCCs)

A labeled digraph \(G = (V, E)\) is strongly connected if for every ordered pair of vertices \((u, v)\), there is a directed path from \(u\) to \(v\). Every digraph \(G\) decomposes uniquely into its strongly connected components (SCCs), and contracting each SCC into a single super-vertex yields a condensation DAG!

Warmup: Strongly Connected Tournaments (SEQ Condensation)

In a tournament \(T\) on \(n\) vertices (where every unordered pair \(\{u, v\}\) has exactly one directed arc \(u \to v\) or \(v \to u\), so \(t_n = 2^{\binom{n}{2}}\)), what does the condensation DAG of its SCCs look like?

Because every pair of vertices in distinct SCCs has an arc between them in the same direction, the condensation DAG of any tournament is a transitive tournament—i.e., a strict linear order (\(\operatorname{SEQ}\)) of non-empty strongly connected tournaments \(S_1 \to S_2 \to \dots \to S_r\) where every vertex in \(S_i\) beats every vertex in \(S_j\) for all \(i < j\)!

Notice the miracle: because the cross-edges between \(S_i\) and \(S_j\) (\(i < j\)) are completely forced (\(1\) choice: all directed from \(S_i\) to \(S_j\)), there is no \(2^{k(n-k)}\) cross-edge factor! Thus, in the standard EGF ring \(K[[x]]\), let \(T(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) be the EGF of non-empty tournaments and \(S_{\text{tour}}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} s_{\text{tour}, n} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) be the EGF of strongly connected tournaments. By the \(\operatorname{SEQ}\) construction:

\[ 1 + T(x) = \frac{1}{1 - S_{\text{tour}}(x)} \implies S_{\text{tour}}(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + T(x)}. \]

General Strongly Connected Digraphs (CF 1556F Engine)

Now consider general labeled digraphs (where each ordered pair \((u, v)\) with \(u \neq v\) is independently present or absent, so \(g_n = 4^{\binom{n}{2}} = 2^{2\binom{n}{2}}\)). How many of these \(4^{\binom{n}{2}}\) digraphs are strongly connected (c_scc, n)?

Here the condensation of \(G\) into SCCs is an arbitrary DAG of SCCs, not a linear sequence. Let us combine the Robinson–Stanley source-layer IE with the \(\operatorname{SET}\) construction of SCCs:

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Source-SCC Layer IE
  1. The Signed Source-SCC Cluster Series \(F(x)\): In the condensation DAG of any non-empty digraph \(G\), there is a non-empty set of source SCCs (SCCs with no incoming arcs from any other SCC). By the exact same Inclusion–Exclusion principle as for DAGs, we choose a non-empty unordered collection of \(r \ge 1\) disjoint strongly connected components \(C_1, \dots, C_r\) to serve as a designated subset of source SCCs, weighting a choice of \(r\) source SCCs by \((-1)^{r-1}\) because \(\sum_{r=1}^s \binom{s}{r}(-1)^{r-1} = 1\). Within the chosen \(r\) source SCCs, there are no arcs at all between distinct \(C_i\) and \(C_j\). Therefore, the EGF of a signed cluster of \(r \ge 1\) disjoint source SCCs of total size \(k \ge 1\) is simply:

\[ F(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f_k \frac{x^k}{k!} = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{r-1} \frac{C_{\text{SCC}}(x)^r}{r!} = 1 - \exp\left(-C_{\text{SCC}}(x)\right), \]

where \(C_{\text{SCC}}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_{\text{SCC}, n} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) is the EGF of strongly connected digraphs!

  1. Counting Edges Between the Source-SCC Layer \(K\) and the Rest \(V \setminus K\): Once we choose a signed cluster of source SCCs on a vertex subset \(K \subseteq V\) of total size \(k = |K| \ge 1\) (weight \(f_k\)), what happens on the remaining \(n-k\) vertices \(V \setminus K\)?

    • Between \(K\) and \(V \setminus K\), no arc may enter \(K\) from \(V \setminus K\), while each of the \(k(n-k)\) forward arcs from \(K\) to \(V \setminus K\) may be independently present or absent (\(2^{k(n-k)}\) choices).
    • Inside \(V \setminus K\), we may choose any labeled digraph on \(n-k\) vertices! There are \(g_{n-k} = 4^{\binom{n-k}{2}} = 2^{2\binom{n-k}{2}}\) such digraphs.

    Therefore, for every \(n \ge 1\), summing over the total size \(k \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) of the designated source-SCC cluster yields:

\[ g_n = 2^{2\binom{n}{2}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \binom{n}{k} f_k \, 2^{k(n-k)} \, 2^{2\binom{n-k}{2}}. \]

  1. Linearizing the Exponent via the Quadratic Identity: Look at the combined power of \(2\) on the right-hand side: \(k(n-k) + 2\binom{n-k}{2}\). Since \(\binom{n}{2} = \binom{k}{2} + \binom{n-k}{2} + k(n-k)\), we can rewrite the exponent purely in terms of \(\binom{n}{2}\), \(\binom{k}{2}\), and \(\binom{n-k}{2}\):

\[ k(n-k) + 2\binom{n-k}{2} = \left(\binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2} - \binom{n-k}{2}\right) + 2\binom{n-k}{2} = \binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2} + \binom{n-k}{2}. \]

Substitute this identity into the recurrence for \(g_n = 2^{2\binom{n}{2}}\) and divide both sides by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}} n!\):

\[ \frac{2^{\binom{n}{2}}}{n!} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\frac{f_k}{2^{\binom{k}{2}} k!}\right) \left(\frac{2^{\binom{n-k}{2}}}{(n-k)!}\right) \quad (n \ge 1). \]

  1. Two-Step Inversion (\(\text{inv}\) then \(\ln\)): Define the Chirp-twisted EGFs

\[ \hat{G}(x) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \frac{2^{\binom{m}{2}}}{m!} x^m, \qquad \hat{F}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{f_k}{2^{\binom{k}{2}} k!} x^k. \]

Then the recurrence for \(n \ge 1\) says \(\hat{G}(x) - 1 = \hat{F}(x) \hat{G}(x)\), which solves immediately to:

\[ \hat{F}(x) = 1 - \hat{G}(x)^{-1}. \]

After computing \(\hat{F}(x)\) by one polynomial inversion, we untwist its coefficients by multiplying \([x^k]\hat{F}(x)\) by \(2^{\binom{k}{2}}\) to recover \(F(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f_k \frac{x^k}{k!}\). Finally, since \(F(x) = 1 - \exp(-C_{\text{SCC}}(x))\), we take one formal logarithm to recover the EGF of strongly connected digraphs:

\[ C_{\text{SCC}}(x) = -\ln\left(1 - F(x)\right). \]

Hand-Checkable Trace: Strongly Connected Digraphs (\(n \le 3\))

Let us trace the two-stage pipeline (Chirp-twisted inversion \(\hat{F} = 1 - \hat{G}^{-1}\) followed by untwisted logarithm \(C_{\text{SCC}} = -\ln(1 - F)\)) up to \(n = 3\):

  1. Twisted EGF \(\hat{G}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^3 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}\): Since \(2^{\binom{n}{2}} = 1, 1, 2, 8\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\): \[\hat{G}(x) = 1 + x + x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3 + O(x^4).\]
  2. Invert to get \(\hat{G}(x)^{-1}\) and \(\hat{F}(x) = 1 - \hat{G}(x)^{-1}\): Writing \(\hat{G}(x) = 1 + U\) with \(U = x + x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3\), we have \(\hat{G}(x)^{-1} = 1 - U + U^2 - U^3 + O(x^4) = 1 - x + \frac{2}{3}x^3 + O(x^4)\), so \(\hat{F}(x) = x - \frac{2}{3}x^3 + O(x^4)\). Multiplying by \(n!\) gives the signed subset-sum coefficients \(\psi_n = -f_n = n!\,[x^n]\hat{G}(x)^{-1}\): \[\psi_1 = -1, \quad \psi_2 = 0, \quad \psi_3 = 4 \qquad \bigl(f_1 = 1, \quad f_2 = 0, \quad f_3 = -4\bigr).\]
  3. Untwist by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\): Multiplying \(f_n = -\psi_n\) by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) gives the untwisted EGF \(1 - F(x) = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^3 \psi_n 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}\): \[1 - F(x) = 1 - 1\cdot\frac{x}{1!} + 0\cdot\frac{x^2}{2!} + (4 \cdot 8)\frac{x^3}{3!} + O(x^4) = 1 - x + \frac{16}{3}x^3 + O(x^4).\]
  4. Formal Logarithm \(C_{\text{SCC}}(x) = -\ln(1 - F(x))\): Setting \(F(x) = x - \frac{16}{3}x^3\), we expand \(-\ln(1 - F) = F + \frac{1}{2}F^2 + \frac{1}{3}F^3 + O(x^4)\): \[C_{\text{SCC}}(x) = \left(x - \frac{16}{3}x^3\right) + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 + O(x^4) = x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 - 5x^3 + \dots?\] Sign check: In step 2, \(U^2 = x^2 + 2x^3\) and \(U^3 = x^3\), so \(1 - U + U^2 - U^3 = 1 - (x + x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3) + (x^2 + 2x^3) - x^3 = 1 - x - \frac{1}{3}x^3\)! Thus \(\psi_3 = -2\) (\(f_3 = +2\)), untwisting gives \(1 - F(x) = 1 - x - 16\frac{x^3}{3!} = 1 - x - \frac{8}{3}x^3\) (\(F(x) = x + \frac{8}{3}x^3\)), and \(-\ln(1 - F(x)) = \left(x + \frac{8}{3}x^3\right) + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 = x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + 3x^3 + O(x^4)\). Multiplying by \(n!\) gives \(s_1 = 1\), \(s_2 = 2!\cdot\frac{1}{2} = 1\) (the bidirectional edge \(1 \leftrightarrow 2\)), and \(s_3 = 3!\cdot 3 = 18\) strongly connected digraphs on \(3\) vertices!

13.4 Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem, The BEST Theorem, Rank-1 Determinant Lemma & Symbolic Matrix-Tree

Sections 13.1–13.3 counted graphs globally when every pair of vertices \(\{u, v\}\) had the same symmetry. Now suppose we are given a specific host graph \(G = (V, E)\) on \(n\) vertices \(V = \{1, \dots, n\}\) (possibly with edge weights \(w_e \in R\) in a commutative ring \(R\)), and we want to compute the sum over all spanning trees \(T\) of \(G\) of the product of their edge weights:

\[ \tau(G) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(G)} \prod_{e \in E(T)} w_e. \]

Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem (Undirected & Directed Arborescences)

1. Undirected Graphs

Given an undirected weighted graph \(G = (V, E, w)\) on \(V = \{1, \dots, n\}\), its Laplacian matrix \(L(G) = D(G) - A(G) \in R^{n \times n}\) has entries:

\[ L_{u, v} = \begin{cases} \sum_{z \neq u} w_{\{u, z\}} & \text{if } u = v, \\[4pt] -w_{\{u, v\}} & \text{if } u \neq v. \end{cases} \]

Notice that every row and every column of \(L(G)\) sums to \(0\), so \(\det L(G) = 0\). However, if we delete any row \(r\) and the corresponding column \(r\) (\(1 \le r \le n\)) to obtain the \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) principal minor \(L^{(r, r)}\), a remarkable identity holds:

ImportantTheorem 13.1 (Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem — Undirected Version)

For any commutative ring \(R\) and any choice of root vertex \(r \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), the determinant of the \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) principal minor \(L^{(r, r)}\) equals the weighted sum of all spanning trees of \(G\):

\[ \det L^{(r, r)}(G) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(G)} \prod_{e \in E(T)} w_e. \]

Why It Works (Single-Step Cauchy–Binet Proof)

Orient each undirected edge \(e = \{u, v\} \in E\) arbitrarily, and form the \((n-1) \times |E|\) reduced incidence matrix \(B\) (with row \(r\) deleted), where column \(e = (u \to v)\) has \(-1\) in row \(u\), \(+1\) in row \(v\) (omitting row \(r\)), and \(0\) elsewhere. Let \(W = \operatorname{diag}(w_e)_{e \in E}\). Then \(L^{(r, r)} = B W B^T\). By the Cauchy–Binet Formula,

\[ \det L^{(r, r)} = \det(B W B^T) = \sum_{\substack{S \subseteq E \\ |S| = n-1}} \det(B_S) \det(W_S B_S^T) = \sum_{\substack{S \subseteq E \\ |S| = n-1}} (\det B_S)^2 \prod_{e \in S} w_e. \]

For any subset \(S \subseteq E\) of \(n-1\) edges:

  • If \(S\) contains a cycle, the columns of \(B_S\) along that cycle are linearly dependent, so \(\det B_S = 0\).
  • If \(S\) forms a spanning tree \(T\), rooting \(T\) at \(r\) and ordering vertices by decreasing depth from \(r\) makes \(B_S\) upper triangular with diagonal entries \(\pm 1\), so \(\det B_S = \pm 1\) and \((\det B_S)^2 = 1\)!

2. Directed Graphs (In-Trees and Out-Trees)

In a directed weighted graph \(G = (V, E, w)\) where \(w_{u \to v}\) is the weight of the directed arc \(u \to v\), there are two kinds of directed spanning trees (arborescences) rooted at a fixed vertex \(r \in V\):

  • In-Tree (Rooted Arborescence toward \(r\)) \(\mathcal{T}_r^{\text{in}}(G)\): A spanning subgraph of \(n-1\) arcs in which every vertex \(v \neq r\) has out-degree \(1\) and a unique directed path \(v \to \dots \to r\) into the root \(r\) (while \(\operatorname{out-deg}_T(r) = 0\)).
  • Out-Tree (Rooted Arborescence away from \(r\)) \(\mathcal{T}_r^{\text{out}}(G)\): A spanning subgraph of \(n-1\) arcs in which every vertex \(v \neq r\) has in-degree \(1\) and a unique directed path \(r \to \dots \to v\) out of the root \(r\) (while \(\operatorname{in-deg}_T(r) = 0\)).
ImportantTheorem 13.2 (Directed Matrix-Tree Theorem — Tutte 1948)

Define the out-degree Laplacian \(L^{\text{in}}(G) = D^{\text{out}}(G) - A(G)\) and the in-degree Laplacian \(L^{\text{out}}(G) = D^{\text{in}}(G) - A(G)\) by:

\[ L^{\text{in}}_{u, v} = \begin{cases} \sum_{z \neq u} w_{u \to z} & \text{if } u = v, \\ -w_{u \to v} & \text{if } u \neq v, \end{cases} \qquad L^{\text{out}}_{u, v} = \begin{cases} \sum_{z \neq u} w_{z \to u} & \text{if } u = v, \\ -w_{u \to v} & \text{if } u \neq v. \end{cases} \]

Deleting row \(r\) and column \(r\) (the root’s row and column):

  1. \(\det (L^{\text{in}})^{(r, r)}\) counts in-trees rooted at \(r\) (arcs directed toward \(r\)): \(\sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_r^{\text{in}}(G)} \prod_{e \in T} w_e\).
  2. \(\det (L^{\text{out}})^{(r, r)}\) counts out-trees rooted at \(r\) (arcs directed away from \(r\)): \(\sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_r^{\text{out}}(G)} \prod_{e \in T} w_e\).

Mnemonic to Never Confuse Them: In an in-tree toward \(r\), every non-root vertex \(u \neq r\) chooses \(1\) outgoing arc toward its parent, so the diagonal records out-degrees \(D^{\text{out}}\). In an out-tree away from \(r\), every non-root vertex \(v \neq r\) chooses \(1\) incoming arc from its parent, so the diagonal records in-degrees \(D^{\text{in}}\)!

The BEST Theorem (Counting Directed Eulerian Circuits)

Let \(G = (V, E)\) be a strongly connected directed graph (multi-arcs allowed, treated as distinct labeled edges) that is Eulerian, meaning every vertex \(v \in V\) is balanced:

\[ \operatorname{in-deg}(v) = \operatorname{out-deg}(v) = d_v \ge 1 \quad (\forall v \in V). \]

How many directed Eulerian circuits (closed directed trails that traverse every arc in \(E\) exactly once, considered up to cyclic shift of the arc sequence) does \(G\) possess?

ImportantTheorem 13.3 (The BEST Theorem — de Bruijn, van Aardenne-Ehrenfest, Smith, Tutte)

For any fixed root vertex \(r \in V\), let \(t_r^{\text{in}}(G) = \det (L^{\text{in}})^{(r, r)}\) be the number of directed in-trees of \(G\) rooted at \(r\). Then the number of directed Eulerian circuits of \(G\) (up to cyclic rotation) is

\[ \operatorname{ec}(G) = t_r^{\text{in}}(G) \prod_{v \in V} (d_v - 1)!. \]

(In particular, \(t_r^{\text{in}}(G)\) is independent of the choice of root \(r\) whenever \(G\) is a balanced Eulerian digraph!)

Single-Step Bijective Proof (The Last-Exit Arborescence Bijection)

Fix a root vertex \(r \in V\) and fix a designated starting/ending arc \(e^* = (r \to u_1)\) outgoing from \(r\). Any Eulerian circuit of \(G\) corresponds uniquely to a linear walk starting with \(e^*\) and ending at \(r\) after traversing all \(|E|\) arcs.

  1. Extracting an In-Tree \(T \in \mathcal{T}_r^{\text{in}}(G)\) and Local Orderings: Follow the Eulerian walk from \(e^*\) to the end. For every non-root vertex \(v \neq r\), let \(e_{\text{last}}(v)\) be the very last outgoing arc used by the walk when leaving \(v\).

    • The collection of last-exit arcs \(T = \{e_{\text{last}}(v) : v \neq r\}\) has one outgoing arc per \(v \neq r\) and cannot contain any directed cycle (if \(C\) were a directed cycle of last-exit arcs, look at the last vertex \(w \in C\) visited by the Eulerian walk: when leaving \(w\) for the last time along \(e_{\text{last}}(w) = (w \to z)\) with \(z \in C\), the walk enters \(z \neq r\) and must subsequently leave \(z\), contradicting that \(w\) was the last vertex of \(C\) visited!). Thus \(T\) is an in-tree rooted at \(r\)!
    • Furthermore, at each vertex \(v \neq r\), the remaining \(d_v - 1\) outgoing arcs from \(v\) are traversed in some linear order before \(e_{\text{last}}(v)\) ($ (d_v - 1)!$ choices). At the root \(r\), the first outgoing arc is fixed to be \(e^*\), and the remaining \(d_r - 1\) outgoing arcs from \(r\) are traversed in some linear order after \(e^*\) (\((d_r - 1)!\) choices).

  2. Reconstructing the Unique Eulerian Walk from \((T, (\pi_v)_{v \in V})\): Conversely, given any in-tree \(T \in \mathcal{T}_r^{\text{in}}(G)\) and any ordering \(\pi_v\) of the outgoing arcs at each \(v \in V\) such that \(e_{\text{last}}(v)\) is placed last for each \(v \neq r\) and \(e^*\) is placed first at \(r\), simulate the walk greedily: starting at \(r\) along \(e^*\), whenever you arrive at a vertex \(v\), exit along the next unused arc in \(\pi_v\).

    • Because \(\operatorname{in-deg}(v) = \operatorname{out-deg}(v)\), the walk can never get stuck at any \(v \neq r\), so it must eventually get stuck at \(r\) after using all \(d_r\) outgoing arcs from \(r\).
    • Could the walk terminate at \(r\) while leaving some arcs of \(G\) unused? Suppose some vertex \(v\) still has unused outgoing arcs when the walk stops at \(r\). Then the last outgoing arc in \(\pi_v\)—which is the tree arc \(e_{\text{last}}(v) = (v \to p(v)) \in T\)—was never used! Because \(\operatorname{in-deg}(p(v)) = \operatorname{out-deg}(p(v))\), having an unused incoming arc into \(p(v)\) implies \(p(v)\) also has an unused outgoing arc, so \(e_{\text{last}}(p(v)) \in T\) was also never used. Following the unique path in \(T\) from \(v\) to \(r\) implies \(r\) has an unused incoming (and hence outgoing) arc, a contradiction!

The Rank-1 Matrix-Tree Determinant Lemma & Generalized Cayley Forest Formula

Frequently, our graph is either the complete graph \(K_n\) or a perturbation of \(K_n\) obtained by contracting or reweighting a known tree/forest. Computing an \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) determinant from scratch in \(O(n^3)\) time is far too slow when \(n \le 10^5\), and unnecessary when the Laplacian is a rank-1 perturbation of a diagonal matrix!

ImportantLemma 13.4 (Rank-1 Matrix Determinant Lemma)

Let \(A \in K^{m \times m}\) be an invertible matrix over a field \(K\), and let \(u, v \in K^m\) be column vectors. Then

\[ \det(A + u v^T) = \det(A) \cdot \left(1 + v^T A^{-1} u\right). \]

Application: The Generalized Cayley Forest Formula (CF 156D, CF 1109D)

Suppose a vertex set \(V = \{1, \dots, n\}\) is already partitioned into \(k\) fixed connected components \(C_1, C_2, \dots, C_k\) of sizes \(s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k\) (\(\sum_{i=1}^k s_i = n\)). How many ways are there to add \(k - 1\) edges between these components so that the entire \(n\)-vertex graph becomes a single connected spanning tree of \(K_n\)?

Contracting each component \(C_i\) into a super-vertex \(i\) creates a multigraph on \(\{1, \dots, k\}\) where the number of potential edges between super-vertices \(i\) and \(j\) (\(i \neq j\)) is \(w_{ij} = s_i s_j\).

  1. Write down the \(k \times k\) Laplacian \(L\) of the super-vertex multigraph: For \(i \neq j\), \(L_{i, j} = -s_i s_j\). On the diagonal, \(L_{i, i} = \sum_{j \neq i} s_i s_j = s_i(n - s_i) = n s_i - s_i^2\).
  2. Delete row \(k\) and column \(k\) to get the \((k-1) \times (k-1)\) minor \(L^{(k, k)}\): Let \(s = (s_1, s_2, \dots, s_{k-1})^T\). Then \(L^{(k, k)}\) is a diagonal matrix minus a rank-1 matrix:

\[ L^{(k, k)} = \operatorname{diag}(n s_1, n s_2, \dots, n s_{k-1}) - s s^T. \]

  1. Apply the Rank-1 Matrix Determinant Lemma: With \(A = \operatorname{diag}(n s_1, \dots, n s_{k-1})\), \(u = -s\), and \(v = s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \det L^{(k, k)} &= \left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1} n s_i\right) \left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \frac{s_i^2}{n s_i}\right) && \text{(apply the rank-1 matrix determinant lemma)} \\[4pt] &= n^{k-1} \left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1} s_i\right) \left(1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} s_i}{n}\right) && \text{(factor } n^{k-1} \text{ and cancel } s_i \text{ in each fraction)} \\[4pt] &= n^{k-1} \left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1} s_i\right) \left(\frac{s_k}{n}\right) = n^{k-2} \prod_{i=1}^{k} s_i. && \text{(use } \sum_{i=1}^{k} s_i = n \implies n - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} s_i = s_k\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

TipCorollary 13.5 (Generalized Cayley Formulas)
  1. Fixed Component Sizes \((s_1, \dots, s_k)\): The number of ways to connect \(k\) fixed components of sizes \(s_1, \dots, s_k\) (\(\sum s_i = n\)) into a spanning tree is

\[ n^{k-2} \prod_{i=1}^{k} s_i \quad (\text{and } 1 \text{ when } k = 1). \]

  1. Rooted Forest on \(k\) Fixed Roots (CF 1109D): The number of ways to choose a forest of \(k\) trees on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) such that \(k\) specified vertices \(\{1, \dots, k\}\) lie in distinct components (equivalently, connecting \(\{1, \dots, k\}\) into a single super-vertex of size \(k\) and \(n-k\) isolated vertices of size \(1\)) is

\[ k \cdot n^{n - k - 1}. \]

Symbolic Matrix-Tree over Polynomial Quotient Rings \(K[x]/(x^{k+1})\)

What if each edge \(e \in E\) of \(G\) carries a weight \(v_e\), and instead of the product of edge weights \(\prod_{e \in T} v_e\), a problem asks for the sum of edge weights \(\sum_{e \in T} v_e\) across all spanning trees \(T \in \mathcal{T}(G)\), or more generally the \(k\)-th power of the sum of edge weights (#208 Luogu P6624, #093 CF 917D):

\[ S_k(G) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(G)} \left(\sum_{e \in E(T)} v_e\right)^k \text{ ?} \]

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Converting Sums of Edge Weights into Products of Jets

Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem only knows how to compute \(\sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(G)} \prod_{e \in T} w_e(x)\). Because Theorem 13.1 holds over any commutative ring \(R\), we can choose \(R = K[x]/(x^{k+1})\), the ring of truncated polynomials (jets) of degree \(\le k\), and design edge weights \(w_e(x) \in K[x]/(x^{k+1})\) so that multiplication in \(R\) translates into addition of \(v_e\):

  1. Case \(k = 1\) (Sum of Edge Weights over all Spanning Trees — \(R = K[x]/(x^2)\), Dual Numbers): Assign each edge \(e \in E\) the linear polynomial weight \(w_e(x) = 1 + v_e x \pmod{x^2}\). Then for any spanning tree \(T\):

\[ \prod_{e \in E(T)} (1 + v_e x) \equiv 1 + \left(\sum_{e \in E(T)} v_e\right) x \pmod{x^2}. \]

Therefore, running Gaussian elimination over \(K[x]/(x^2)\) on the Laplacian minor \(L^{(r, r)}(x)\) produces a degree-\(1\) polynomial \(P_0 + P_1 x\):

  • \(P_0 = [x^0] \det L^{(r, r)}(x) = |\mathcal{T}(G)|\) is the number of spanning trees,
  • \(P_1 = [x^1] \det L^{(r, r)}(x) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(G)} \sum_{e \in T} v_e\) is the total edge-weight sum across all spanning trees!
  1. General \(k \ge 1\) (\(k\)-th Power of Sum of Weights — Exponential Jet Weighting): Assign each edge \(e \in E\) the truncated exponential weight

\[ w_e(x) = \exp(v_e x) \bmod x^{k+1} = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{v_e^j}{j!} x^j \in K[x]/(x^{k+1}). \]

Because \(\exp(a x) \exp(b x) = \exp((a+b)x)\), the product of edge weights along any spanning tree \(T\) is

\[ \prod_{e \in E(T)} \exp(v_e x) = \exp\left(\left(\sum_{e \in E(T)} v_e\right) x\right) \equiv \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{1}{j!} \left(\sum_{e \in E(T)} v_e\right)^j x^j \pmod{x^{k+1}}. \]

Summing over all \(T \in \mathcal{T}(G)\) and extracting \([x^k]\) via Kirchhoff’s Theorem over \(K[x]/(x^{k+1})\) gives

\[ S_k(G) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(G)} \left(\sum_{e \in E(T)} v_e\right)^k = k! \, [x^k] \det L^{(r, r)}(x) \pmod{x^{k+1}}. \]

  1. Counting Spanning Trees by Exact Intersection / Edge Count (CF 917D): If edge \(e\) has weight \(w_e(x) = x\) (for edges in a special set \(S\)) or \(w_e(x) = 1\) (for other edges), then \(\det L^{(r, r)}(x)\) is a polynomial of degree \(\le n-1\) in \(K[x]\) whose coefficient \([x^j] \det L^{(r, r)}(x)\) counts spanning trees containing exactly \(j\) edges from \(S\)! Here you have two algorithmic choices:

    • Direct Elimination over \(K[x]/(x^{k+1})\): Each ring multiplication/division takes \(O(k^2)\) time (or \(O(k \log k)\)), so Gaussian elimination takes \(O(n^3 k^2)\) time.
    • Evaluation + Lagrange Interpolation: Evaluate the scalar determinant \(y_i = \det L^{(r, r)}(x_i) \in K\) at \(n\) distinct field points \(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{n-1} \in K\) in \(n \times O(n^3) = O(n^4)\) time, and interpolate the degree-\((n-1)\) polynomial in \(O(n^2)\) time (Chapter 6).

Hand-Checkable Trace: Symbolic Matrix-Tree on \(K_3\) (\(n = 3, k = 2\))

Suppose \(G = K_3\) on \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) with edge weights \(v_{12} = 1, v_{23} = 2, v_{13} = 3\). Its \(3\) spanning trees have weight sums \(1 + 2 = 3\), \(1 + 3 = 4\), and \(2 + 3 = 5\), so \(S_0 = 3\), \(S_1 = 3 + 4 + 5 = 12\), and \(S_2 = 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 = 50\). Working in \(R = \mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3)\), assign each edge \(e\) the jet \(w_e(x) = 1 + v_e x + \frac{v_e^2}{2}x^2\): \[w_{12}(x) = 1 + x + \frac{1}{2}x^2, \qquad w_{23}(x) = 1 + 2x + 2x^2, \qquad w_{13}(x) = 1 + 3x + \frac{9}{2}x^2.\] Deleting row \(3\) and column \(3\) gives the \(2 \times 2\) minor \(L^{(3, 3)}(x) = \begin{pmatrix} w_{12} + w_{13} & -w_{12} \\ -w_{12} & w_{12} + w_{23} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 4x + 5x^2 & -1 - x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 \\ -1 - x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 & 2 + 3x + \frac{5}{2}x^2 \end{pmatrix}\). Taking the determinant modulo \(x^3\): \[\det L^{(3, 3)}(x) \equiv (2 + 4x + 5x^2)\left(2 + 3x + \frac{5}{2}x^2\right) - \left(1 + x + \frac{1}{2}x^2\right)^2 \equiv (4 + 14x + 27x^2) - (1 + 2x + 2x^2) = 3 + 12x + 25x^2 \pmod{x^3}.\] Multiplying \([x^j]\) by \(j!\) recovers \(S_0 = 0!\cdot 3 = 3\), \(S_1 = 1!\cdot 12 = 12\), and \(S_2 = 2!\cdot 25 = 50\)!

WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Singular Constant Term \(L^{(r, r)}(0)\) in \(K[x]/(x^{k+1})\) Elimination

The Pitfall: When performing Gaussian elimination over \(R = K[x]/(x^{k+1})\), a pivot element \(P(x) = p_0 + p_1 x + \dots + p_k x^k \in K[x]/(x^{k+1})\) is a unit (invertible in \(R\)) if and only if its constant term \(p_0 = P(0) \neq 0\)! If some edges carry weight \(x\) (so \(P(0) = 0\) when the \(0\)-weight edges do not span \(G\)), every candidate pivot in a column may have constant term \(p_0 = 0\) (valuation \(\operatorname{val}(P) \ge 1\)), causing division by \(P(x)\) to crash with a division-by-zero error even when \(\det L^{(r, r)}(x) \not\equiv 0\)!

The Remedy:

  1. Pivot by Minimum Valuation: When choosing a pivot in column \(c\) among rows \(i \ge c\), always pick a non-zero row \(i\) with the smallest valuation \(\operatorname{val}(L_{i, c}(x))\) (the index of its first non-zero coefficient).
  2. Factor out \(x^v\) or Use Euclidean Division: If the minimum valuation in column \(c\) is \(v = \operatorname{val}(L_{\text{piv}, c}) \ge 1\), you can either:
    • Factor \(x^v\) out of column \(c\) (dividing all entries in column \(c\) by \(x^v\), accumulating \(x^v\) into an overall power-of-\(x\) shift of the determinant, and tracking precision accordingly) so that the pivot’s constant term becomes non-zero and invertible; or
    • Perform fraction-free Euclidean row reduction on the leading coefficient of the lowest-valuation row until all other rows in column \(c\) have strictly higher valuation, repeating until they vanish modulo \(x^{k+1}\)!
  3. Alternative (Evaluation–Interpolation): Whenever \(k\) is comparable to \(n\) (such as \(k = n - 1\) in CF 917D), evaluating \(\det L^{(r, r)}(x_i)\) at \(n\) scalar points \(x_i \in K\) completely avoids polynomial ring division and valuation issues!

In Chapter 24 (Bitwise FWT, Set Power Series & Dirichlet GFs), we will diagonalize the group algebra via the Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transform (FWT), evaluating \(\det L\) pointwise at each character in \(O(2^d n^3)\) time and inverting FWT once at the end!

  • Input: Number of vertices \(n \ge 1\), truncation degree \(k \ge 0\), and an \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) Laplacian minor \(M(x) = L^{(n, n)}(x)\) whose entries are polynomials of degree \(\le k\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^{k+1})\) (\(p > k\)).

        for (int j = c; j < n - 1; ++j)
            M[i][j] = sub_poly(M[i][j], mul_trunc(factor, M[c][j], k));
    }
}

  • Source: Luogu P5219 — 无聊的水题 I

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

Find the number of labeled undirected trees on \(N\) vertices \(\{1, 2, \dots, N\}\) such that the maximum degree of any vertex in the tree is strictly equal to \(M\). Output the answer modulo \(998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(2 \le M < N \le 10^5\), time limit \(1.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space.
Small Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N \le 4\))
  • \(N = 3, M = 2\): \(\mathbf{3}\) (all \(3\) labeled paths \(P_3\) have maximum degree \(2\)).
  • \(N = 4, M = 2\): \(\mathbf{12}\) (all \(4! / 2 = 12\) labeled paths \(P_4\) have maximum degree \(2\)).
  • \(N = 4, M = 3\): \(\mathbf{4}\) (all \(4\) labeled stars \(K_{1, 3}\) have maximum degree \(3\); note \(12 + 4 = 16 = 4^{4-2}\)).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

First decouple the “strictly equal to \(M\)” maximum-degree condition by writing the count of trees with \(\max_i d_i = M\) as the difference between trees with \(\max_i d_i \le M\) and trees with \(\max_i d_i \le M - 1\). How can you translate a vertex-degree tuple \((d_1, \dots, d_N)\) on a labeled tree into a sequence of length \(N - 2\) where each vertex \(i\) appears \(d_i - 1\) times?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

By the Prüfer Sequence bijection (Chapter 4), a labeled tree on \(N\) vertices with degrees \((d_1, d_2, \dots, d_N)\) corresponds bijectively to a sequence in \(\{1, \dots, N\}^{N-2}\) in which vertex \(i\) appears \(c_i = d_i - 1\) times. Let \(f(K)\) be the number of labeled trees on \(N\) vertices where every vertex \(i\) has degree \(1 \le d_i \le K\). Setting \(c_i = d_i - 1 \in \{0, 1, \dots, K-1\}\), Prüfer’s theorem gives

\[ f(K) = \sum_{\substack{c_1 + \dots + c_N = N - 2 \\ 0 \le c_i \le K - 1}} \frac{(N-2)!}{c_1! c_2! \cdots c_N!}. \]

Recognizing the multinomial coefficient as the EGF product of \(N\) identical factors, write \(f(K)\) as \((N-2)! [x^{N-2}] E_{K-1}(x)^N\) for a truncated exponential polynomial \(E_{K-1}(x)\).

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Define \(E_d(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{d} \frac{x^j}{j!}\). Then \(f(K) = (N-2)! [x^{N-2}] E_{K-1}(x)^N\). Since \([x^0]E_{K-1}(x) = 1\), compute \(E_{K-1}(x)^N = \exp(N \ln E_{K-1}(x)) \bmod x^{N-1}\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time for \(K = M\) and \(K = M - 1\), and subtract!

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Reduction from Exact Maximum Degree to Bounded Maximum Degree

Let \(f(K)\) denote the number of labeled trees on \(N\) vertices \(\{1, \dots, N\}\) in which every vertex has degree at most \(K\):

\[ f(K) = \left|\left\{ T \in \mathcal{T}_N : \max_{1 \le i \le N} \deg_T(i) \le K \right\}\right|. \]

Then the number of labeled trees whose maximum vertex degree is exact \(M\) is simply the difference

\[ \text{Ans} = f(M) - f(M - 1) \pmod{998244353}. \]

2. Prüfer Sequence Bijection & EGF Formulation

By Prüfer’s bijection (Chapter 4), the number of labeled trees on \(N\) vertices with a fixed degree sequence \((d_1, d_2, \dots, d_N)\) (where \(d_i \ge 1\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^N d_i = 2N - 2\)) is the multinomial coefficient

\[ \binom{N-2}{d_1 - 1, d_2 - 1, \dots, d_N - 1} = \frac{(N-2)!}{(d_1 - 1)! (d_2 - 1)! \cdots (d_N - 1)!}. \]

Substitute \(c_i = d_i - 1\). The condition \(1 \le d_i \le K\) becomes \(0 \le c_i \le K - 1\), and the sum condition becomes \(c_1 + c_2 + \dots + c_N = N - 2\). Summing over all valid tuples \((c_1, \dots, c_N)\) gives

\[ f(K) = (N-2)! \sum_{\substack{c_1 + \dots + c_N = N - 2 \\ 0 \le c_i \le K - 1}} \prod_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{c_i!}. \]

The inner sum is the coefficient of \(x^{N-2}\) in the \(N\)-th power of the truncated exponential series \(E_{K-1}(x)\):

\[ E_{K-1}(x) = \sum_{c=0}^{K-1} \frac{x^c}{c!}, \qquad f(K) = (N-2)! \, [x^{N-2}] \left(E_{K-1}(x)\right)^N. \]

3. Boundary Cases & Polynomial Exponentiation

  • Boundary Case \(N = 2, M = 1\): Here \(N - 2 = 0\), \(f(1) = 1\), \(f(0) = 0\), and the answer is \(1\).
  • General Case \(2 \le M < N\): Since \(M \ge 2\), both \(K = M\) and \(K = M - 1\) satisfy \(K - 1 \ge 0\), so the constant term is \([x^0] E_{K-1}(x) = \frac{1}{0!} = 1\). Therefore, we can compute \(\left(E_{K-1}(x)\right)^N \bmod x^{N-1}\) via formal logarithm and exponential:

\[ \left(E_{K-1}(x)\right)^N \equiv \exp\left(N \cdot \ln E_{K-1}(x)\right) \pmod{x^{N-1}}. \]

NoteAlgorithm Contract
  • Input & Preconditions: Integers \(N, M\) with \(2 \le M < N \le 10^5\) and prime modulus \(p = 998244353 > N\), ensuring \([x^0] E_{K-1}(x) = 1\) for \(K \in \{M - 1, M\}\).
  • Postconditions: Outputs \((f(M) - f(M-1)) \bmod 998244353\), the exact number of labeled trees on \(N\) vertices with maximum degree \(M\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: Two calls to polynomial ln and exp of degree \(N - 2\), running in \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space.
  • Core Invariant: For each \(K \ge 1\), \((N-2)! [x^{N-2}] E_{K-1}(x)^N\) counts Prüfer sequences in \(\{1, \dots, N\}^{N-2}\) in which every label appears at most \(K - 1\) times.
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: When \(N = 2, M = 1\), \(N - 2 = 0 \implies f(1) = 1, f(0) = 0\); for \(K = 1\), \(E_0(x) = 1\) has degree \(0\), so \([x^{N-2}] E_0(x)^N = [N = 2]\) without needing \(\ln/\exp\).

Stepping Stone 2 (#103) — CF 1109D: Sasha and Interesting Fact from Graph Theory

  • Source: Codeforces 1109D — Sasha and Interesting Fact from Graph Theory
  • Prerequisite Tags: Generalized Cayley Forest Formula (§13.4), Stars and Bars (Ch 06).

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given four integers \(n, m, a, b\). A weighted undirected tree on \(n\) labeled vertices \(\{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) is good if every edge has an integer weight in \(\{1, 2, \dots, m\}\), and the sum of edge weights along the unique path between vertex \(a\) and vertex \(b\) in the tree equals \(m\). Find the number of good weighted labeled trees modulo \(10^9 + 7\).

  • Constraints: \(2 \le n \le 10^6\), \(1 \le m \le 10^6\), \(1 \le a, b \le n\) (\(a \neq b\)), time limit \(2.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(n + m)\) time and \(O(n + m)\) space.
Small Hand-Checkable Trace (\(n = 3\))
  • \(n = 3, m = 1, a = 1, b = 2\): \(\mathbf{2}\) (path \(1 - 2\) has \(k = 1\) edge of weight \(1\); vertex \(3\) attaches to either \(1\) or \(2\) with edge weight \(1\)).
  • \(n = 3, m = 2, a = 1, b = 2\): \(\mathbf{5}\) (\(k = 1\) edge \(1 - 2\) of weight \(2\), \(2\) ways to attach vertex \(3\), and \(2\) weight choices for the second edge \(\implies 4\) trees; plus \(k = 2\) edges \(1 - 3 - 2\) with weights \((1, 1) \implies 1\) tree; total \(4 + 1 = 5\)).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Every tree on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) has a unique simple path between \(a\) and \(b\). Suppose that path has \(k\) edges (\(1 \le k \le \min(n-1, m)\)) and therefore contains \(k + 1\) vertices (including \(a\) and \(b\)). Once the \(k+1\) ordered vertices and \(k\) edge weights on the \(a\)–\(b\) path are fixed, what does the rest of the tree look like when you contract the entire \(a\)–\(b\) path into a single component of size \(k + 1\)?

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

For a fixed path length \(k\) (\(1 \le k \le \min(n - 1, m)\)), decompose the choices into four independent multiplicative factors:

  1. Internal vertices of the \(a\)–\(b\) path: Choose and order \(k - 1\) distinct vertices from the remaining \(n - 2\) vertices in \(\binom{n-2}{k-1}(k-1)! = \frac{(n-2)!}{(n-1-k)!}\) ways.
  2. Edge weights on the \(a\)–\(b\) path: \(k\) positive integers in \(\{1, \dots, m\}\) summing to \(m\): \(\binom{m-1}{k-1}\) ways by Stars and Bars.
  3. Edge weights off the \(a\)–\(b\) path: The remaining \((n - 1) - k\) tree edges can each take any weight in \(\{1, \dots, m\}\): \(m^{n - 1 - k}\) ways.
  4. Forest completion: How many ways to attach the remaining \(n - (k + 1)\) vertices to the \(k + 1\) path vertices to form a tree? Use Corollary 13.5!
Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

By Corollary 13.5 (Generalized Cayley Forest Formula with \(k + 1\) components of sizes \(1, 1, \dots, 1\), or \(1\) component of size \(k+1\) and \(n-k-1\) components of size \(1\) with no internal edges among the \(k+1\) path vertices), the number of ways to complete the tree is \((k+1) n^{n - k - 2}\) (with the convention \((n-1 + 1) n^{-1} = 1\) when \(k = n - 1\)). Precompute factorials and powers of \(n\) and \(m\) to sum over \(k = 1, \dots, \min(n-1, m)\) in \(O(n + m)\) time.

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Decomposition by the Number of Edges \(k\) on the \(a\)–\(b\) Path

Let \(k\) be the number of edges on the unique simple path \(P\) between \(a\) and \(b\) in the tree \(T\). Since each edge on \(P\) has weight at least \(1\) and their weights sum to \(m\), we must have \(1 \le k \le m\). Since \(P\) is a simple path on an \(n\)-vertex tree, we also have \(1 \le k \le n - 1\). Thus \(1 \le k \le \min(n - 1, m)\).

For a fixed \(k\), every valid weighted tree is uniquely determined by four independent choices:

  1. Sequence of Internal Path Vertices: The path \(P = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k)\) has \(v_0 = a\) and \(v_k = b\), and \(k - 1\) ordered distinct internal vertices \((v_1, \dots, v_{k-1})\) chosen from \(V \setminus \{a, b\}\) (\(n - 2\) available vertices):

\[ W_{\text{path-verts}}(k) = \binom{n - 2}{k - 1} (k - 1)! = \frac{(n - 2)!}{(n - 1 - k)!}. \]

  1. Weights of the \(k\) Path Edges: We need a tuple \((w_1, \dots, w_k) \in \{1, \dots, m\}^k\) such that \(w_1 + \dots + w_k = m\). By Stars and Bars, there are

\[ W_{\text{path-weights}}(k) = \binom{m - 1}{k - 1} \]

such tuples (note that \(w_i \ge 1\) and \(\sum w_i = m\) automatically implies \(w_i \le m\)).

  1. Tree Completion off the Path \(P\): Once the \(k\) edges of \(P\) are fixed, \(P\) forms a single connected component of size \(s_1 = k + 1\), while the remaining \(n - k - 1\) vertices are isolated components of sizes \(s_2 = \dots = s_{n-k} = 1\). By the Generalized Cayley Forest Formula (§13.4 Corollary 13.5), the number of ways to add \(n - 1 - k\) edges to connect these \(n - k\) components into a single spanning tree is:

\[ W_{\text{forest}}(k) = \begin{cases} (k + 1) \, n^{n - k - 2} & \text{if } 1 \le k \le n - 2, \\[4pt] 1 & \text{if } k = n - 1. \end{cases} \]

(Notice that for \(k = n - 1\), \((k + 1) n^{n - k - 2} = n \cdot n^{-1} = 1 \pmod{10^9+7}\), so using the modular inverse of \(n\) unifies both cases into a single formula!)

  1. Weights of the \(n - 1 - k\) Non-Path Edges: Each of the \(n - 1 - k\) edges not on \(P\) can independently take any integer weight in \(\{1, 2, \dots, m\}\):

\[ W_{\text{off-weights}}(k) = m^{n - 1 - k}. \]

2. Final Closed-Form Sum

Multiplying the four independent counts and summing over \(k\) yields

\[ \text{Ans} = \sum_{k=1}^{\min(n - 1, m)} \binom{n - 2}{k - 1} (k - 1)! \, \binom{m - 1}{k - 1} \, (k + 1) n^{n - k - 2} \, m^{n - 1 - k} \pmod{10^9 + 7}. \]

NoteAlgorithm Contract
  • Input & Preconditions: Integers \(n, m, a, b\) (\(2 \le n \le 10^6, 1 \le m \le 10^6, a \neq b\)) and prime modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7 > \max(n, m)\).
  • Postconditions: Outputs the number of good weighted labeled trees modulo \(10^9 + 7\).
  • Time & Space Complexity: Precomputing factorials up to \(\max(n, m)\) takes \(O(n + m)\) time and \(O(n + m)\) space; the loop over \(k\) runs in \(O(\min(n, m))\) time.
  • Core Invariant: For each path length \(k \in [1, \min(n-1, m)]\), contracting the \((k+1)\)-vertex \(a\)–\(b\) path leaves \(n - k\) components of sizes \((k+1, 1, \dots, 1)\), which extend to \((k+1) n^{n - k - 2}\) spanning trees by Corollary 13.5.
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: At \(k = n - 1\) (when the \(a\)–\(b\) path spans all \(n\) vertices), the exponent \(n - k - 2 = -1\) is negative; either branch explicitly to \(W_{\text{forest}}(n - 1) = 1\) or evaluate \(n^{-1} \bmod p\).

Intermediate 1 (#093) — CF 917D: Stranger Trees

  • Source: Codeforces 917D — Stranger Trees

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given a tree \(T_0\) on \(n\) labeled vertices \(\{1, 2, \dots, n\}\). For each integer \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n - 1\}\), compute the number of labeled spanning trees \(T\) of the complete graph \(K_n\) that share exactly \(k\) edges with \(T_0\) (i.e., \(|E(T) \cap E(T_0)| = k\)), modulo \(10^9 + 7\).

  • Constraints: \(2 \le n \le 100\) (solvable in \(O(n^4)\) via Symbolic Matrix-Tree + Lagrange Interpolation, and in \(O(n^2)\) via Generalized Cayley + Tree DP!), time limit \(1.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

Introduce an indeterminant \(x\) that marks edges belonging to \(T_0\), so that we seek the coefficients of \(P(x) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(K_n)} x^{|E(T) \cap E(T_0)|}\). Then \(P(x) = \det L^{(n, n)}(x)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n - 1\). Once we know \(W_j = \sum_{\substack{F \subseteq E(T_0) \\ |F| = j}} n^{n - j - 2} \prod_{r=1}^{n-j} s_r\) for each \(j = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1\) (with \(W_{n-1} = 1\)), the exact-intersection count \(ans_k\) is given by binomial inversion (Inclusion–Exclusion):

\[ ans_k = [x^k] \sum_{j=k}^{n-1} W_j (x - 1)^j = \sum_{j=k}^{n-1} (-1)^{j - k} \binom{j}{k} W_j. \]

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

We present both canonical solutions, as they bridge the two halves of §13.4: Symbolic Matrix-Tree over \(K[x]\) and the Rank-1 Generalized Cayley Forest Formula.

Method 1: Symbolic Matrix-Tree + Lagrange Interpolation in \(O(n^4)\)

Assign each edge \(e\) of the complete graph \(K_n\) the polynomial weight

\[ w_e(x) = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } e \in E(T_0), \\[4pt] 1 & \text{if } e \notin E(T_0). \end{cases} \]

By Kirchhoff’s Matrix-Tree Theorem (Theorem 13.1) over the polynomial ring \(\mathbb{F}_p[x]\), the \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) principal minor \(L^{(n, n)}(x)\) has determinant

\[ P(x) = \det L^{(n, n)}(x) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(K_n)} \prod_{e \in E(T)} w_e(x) = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}(K_n)} x^{|E(T) \cap E(T_0)|} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} ans_k \, x^k. \]

Because \(P(x)\) has degree \(n - 1\), it is uniquely determined by its values at the \(n\) distinct field elements \(x_0 = 0, x_1 = 1, \dots, x_{n-1} = n - 1 \in \mathbb{F}_p\):

  1. For each \(m \in \{0, 1, \dots, n - 1\}\), build the scalar \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) Laplacian minor \(L^{(n, n)}(m)\) and compute \(y_m = \det L^{(n, n)}(m) \bmod p\) via Gaussian elimination in \(O(n^3)\) time. Total evaluation time: \(O(n^4)\).
  2. Reconstruct the coefficient array \((ans_0, \dots, ans_{n-1})\) of \(P(x)\) from \((y_0, \dots, y_{n-1})\) using standard \(O(n^2)\) Lagrange interpolation: divide the master polynomial \(M(x) = \prod_{m=0}^{n-1} (x - m)\) by \((x - m)\) in \(O(n)\) time per point and accumulate \(\frac{y_m}{\prod_{j \neq m}(m - j)} \cdot \frac{M(x)}{x - m}\).

Rewrite \(x^m = (1 + (x - 1))^m = \sum_{j=0}^{m} \binom{m}{j} (x - 1)^j\). Then for any spanning tree \(T \in \mathcal{T}(K_n)\),

\[ x^{|E(T) \cap E(T_0)|} = \sum_{F \subseteq E(T) \cap E(T_0)} (x - 1)^{|F|}. \]

\[ P(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (x - 1)^j \underbrace{\sum_{\substack{F \subseteq E(T_0) \\ |F| = j}} \left|\left\{ T \in \mathcal{T}(K_n) : F \subseteq E(T) \right\}\right|}_{W_j}. \]

Any subset \(F \subseteq E(T_0)\) of \(j\) edges of the tree \(T_0\) is an acyclic forest with \(c = n - j\) connected components of sizes \(s_1, s_2, \dots, s_{n-j}\).   • Combinatorial Interpretation of \(\prod_{r=1}^{n-j} s_r\): It is the number of ways to choose exactly \(1\) marked vertex inside each of the \(n - j\) connected components of \(F\)!
  • Square-of-Size Tree Knapsack DP: Define \(\text{dp}[u][c][0/1]\) as the number of ways to delete \(c - 1\) edges inside the subtree of \(u\) (forming \(c\) components in \(u\)’s subtree) and mark \(1\) vertex in each completed component, where the flag \(0/1\) indicates whether the component currently containing \(u\) has already chosen its marked vertex.

  • There are no arcs inside \(K\) and no arcs from \(V \setminus K\) into \(K\).
  • Each of the \(k(n-k)\) forward arcs from \(K\) to \(V \setminus K\) can be independently present or absent (\(2^{k(n-k)}\) choices).

\[ d_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} 2^{k(n-k)} d_{n-k} \quad (n \ge 1) \iff \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} 2^{k(n-k)} d_{n-k} = [n = 0]. \]

1. Counting Properly 2-Colored Labeled Graphs via Chirp-\(z\) Squaring

A properly \(2\)-colored labeled graph \((G, \chi)\) on \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) consists of a partition of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into an ordered pair of color classes \((V_{\text{Black}}, V_{\text{White}})\) of sizes \((k, n-k)\), together with any subset of the \(k(n-k)\) potential edges between \(V_{\text{Black}}\) and \(V_{\text{White}}\). \[ b_n^{(2)} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} 2^{k(n-k)}. \]

\[ \frac{b_n^{(2)}}{2^{\binom{n}{2}} n!} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \left(\frac{2^{-\binom{k}{2}}}{k!}\right) \left(\frac{2^{-\binom{n-k}{2}}}{(n-k)!}\right). \]

\[ B_2(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{b_n^{(2)}}{n!} x^n = \sum_{n=0}^{N} 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \left([x^n] \hat{I}(x)^2\right) x^n. \]

Every properly \(2\)-colored graph is uniquely a \(\operatorname{SET}\) of connected properly \(2\)-colored graphs, so \(\ln B_2(x) = C_2(x)\) is the EGF of connected \(2\)-colored graphs. \[ C_2(x) = 2 C_{\text{bip}}(x) \implies C_{\text{bip}}(x) = \frac{1}{2} \ln B_2(x). \]

\[ B(x) = \exp\left(C_{\text{bip}}(x)\right) = \exp\left(\frac{1}{2} \ln B_2(x)\right) = \sqrt{B_2(x)} \pmod{x^{N+1}}. \]

Extracting \(b_n = n! [x^n] B(x) \bmod 998244353\) for \(n = 1, \dots, N\) completes the solution.

1. The Combinatorial Invariant: Bipartiteness vs. Odd Cycles

In a connected graph \(G = (V, E)\), two vertices \(u\) and \(w\) can have their states coupled by even-length walks without disturbing intermediate vertices if and only if they are connected by a walk of even length.

  • In a connected bipartite graph \(G\) with bipartition \(V = V_{\text{Black}} \sqcup V_{\text{White}}\), every edge connects \(V_{\text{Black}}\) and \(V_{\text{White}}\), so flipping two monochromatic endpoints across an edge increments or decrements \(|R \cap V_{\text{Black}}|\) and \(|R \cap V_{\text{White}}|\) simultaneously by \(\pm 1\). Thus the signed difference \(|R \cap V_{\text{Black}}| - |R \cap V_{\text{White}}| \in \{-|V_{\text{White}}|, \dots, |V_{\text{Black}}|\}\) is invariant (\(n + 1\) equivalence classes).
  • Conversely, if \(G\) is connected and non-bipartite (contains at least one odd cycle), an odd cycle connects \(V_{\text{Black}}\) and \(V_{\text{White}}\), collapsing all colorings of the same global parity \(|R| \bmod 2\) into a single equivalence class (\(2\) classes total).

Thus, counting good connected graphs reduces directly to computing the connected bipartite graph count \(c_{\text{bip}, n}\), and the complementary count is \(c_n - c_{\text{bip}, n}\).

2. Solution via Graphical EGFs in \(O(N \log N)\)

We combine §13.1 and §13.2 directly:

  1. Connected Simple Graphs \(C(x)\): Form \(G(x) = \sum_{n=0}^N 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) and compute \(C(x) = \ln G(x) \bmod x^{N+1}\). Then \(c_n = n! [x^n] C(x)\) is the total number of connected labeled graphs on \(n\) vertices.
  2. Connected Bipartite Graphs \(C_{\text{bip}}(x)\): Form \(\hat{I}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^N 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} \frac{x^k}{k!}\), square it to get \(\hat{B}_2(x) = \hat{I}(x)^2 \bmod x^{N+1}\), untwist by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) to get \(B_2(x)\), and compute

\[ C_{\text{bip}}(x) = \frac{1}{2} \ln B_2(x) \pmod{x^{N+1}}. \]

Then \(c_{\text{bip}, n} = n! [x^n] C_{\text{bip}}(x)\) is the number of connected labeled bipartite graphs on \(n\) vertices, and \(c_n - c_{\text{bip}, n}\) is the number of connected labeled non-bipartite graphs!

  1. For each subset \(S \subseteq V\), let \(e(S)\) be the number of edges of \(E_0\) with both endpoints in \(S\).
  2. A \(2\)-coloring of \(S\) into \(A \sqcup (S \setminus A)\) can only use edges of \(E_0\) that cross between \(A\) and \(S \setminus A\). The number of such cross-edges is \(e(S) - e(A) - e(S \setminus A)\). Thus the number of \(2\)-colored subgraphs on \(S\) is

\[ b_2[S] = \sum_{A \subseteq S} 2^{e(S) - e(A) - e(S \setminus A)} = 2^{e(S)} \sum_{A \subseteq S} 2^{-e(A)} 2^{-e(S \setminus A)}, \]

which is computed for all \(S \subseteq V\) in \(O(3^N)\) time by iterating over submasks \(A \subseteq S\)! 3. Finally, apply the rooted-component recurrence (the set-function analogue of \(\ln\), fixing the minimum element \(v = \min S\) inside its connected component \(T \subseteq S\)) to pass from \(b_2[S]\) to connected \(2\)-colored subgraphs \(c_2[S]\) in \(O(3^N)\) time, and divide by \(2\): \(c_{\text{bip}}[V] = \frac{1}{2} c_2[V]\).

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

There are \(N\) players numbered \(1, 2, \dots, N\) with positive integer skill values \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_N\). In a round-robin tournament, every pair of distinct players \((i, j)\) plays exactly one match, where player \(i\) defeats player \(j\) (creating a directed arc \(i \to j\)) independently with probability

\[ P_{i \to j} = \frac{a_i}{a_i + a_j}, \qquad P_{j \to i} = \frac{a_j}{a_i + a_j}. \]

After all \(\binom{N}{2}\) matches are played, a player \(u\) is declared a potential winner if for every other player \(v\), either \(u\) defeated \(v\) directly or there is a directed chain of players \(u \to w_1 \to \dots \to w_k \to v\) where each player defeated the next. Compute the expected number of potential winners modulo \(10^9 + 7\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 14\), \(1 \le a_i \le 10^6\), time limit \(4.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\).
  • Required Complexity: \(O(3^N \cdot N)\) time (or \(O(3^N)\) with incremental cut maintenance) and \(O(2^N)\) space; in the symmetric case \(a_1 = \dots = a_N\), the underlying strongly connected tournament and digraph counts generalize to \(N \le 10^5\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time via §13.3.
Small Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 2, 3\) with uniform skills \(a = (1, \dots, 1)\))

  • \(N = 3, a = (1, 1, 1)\): \(2\) of the \(8\) tournament outcomes are directed \(3\)-cycles (\(3\) winners each) and \(6\) outcomes are transitive (\(1\) winner each), so \(\mathbb{E}[\text{winners}] = \frac{2 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 1}{8} = \frac{3}{2} \equiv \mathbf{500000005} \pmod{10^9 + 7}\).

Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

When we condense a directed graph into its strongly connected components (SCCs), the resulting condensation graph is a DAG.

  • In a tournament \(T\), there is an arc between every pair of distinct SCCs, so the condensation DAG is a linear order (a chain) \(S_1 \to S_2 \to \dots \to S_r\). Which players can reach every vertex in \(T\)? Precisely the vertices in the unique source SCC \(S_1\)!
  • In a general digraph \(G\), the condensation DAG is an arbitrary DAG, which may have multiple source SCCs (\(r \ge 1\) disjoint strongly connected components with no incoming arcs from the rest of the graph), governed by Inclusion–Exclusion over a chosen non-empty subset of \(r\) source SCCs (§13.3).

Let \(\operatorname{cut}(X, Y) = \prod_{u \in X, v \in Y} \frac{a_u}{a_u + a_v}\) be the probability that every player in \(X\) beats every player in \(Y\). Then

\[ \text{dp}[S] = 1 - \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subsetneq S} \text{dp}[T] \cdot \operatorname{cut}(T, S \setminus T), \]

and the expected number of winners is \(\sum_{\emptyset \neq S \subseteq V} |S| \cdot \text{dp}[S] \cdot \operatorname{cut}(S, V \setminus S)\).

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Non-Uniform Tournament Winners (CF 1556F, \(N \le 14\))

In CF 1556F, each arc \(u \to v\) has a distinct probability \(P_{u \to v} = \frac{a_u}{a_u + a_v}\), so subsets of the same size \(k\) are not symmetric. We lift the tournament condensation identity \(1 + T = \frac{1}{1 - S_{\text{tour}}}\) from the EGF ring \(K[[x]]\) to the subset lattice \(2^V\):

  1. For any disjoint subsets \(X, Y \subseteq V\), define the cut probability that every player in \(X\) beats every player in \(Y\):

\[ \operatorname{cut}(X, Y) = \prod_{u \in X} \prod_{v \in Y} \frac{a_u}{a_u + a_v}. \]

By precomputing \(W(u, \text{mask}) = \prod_{v \in \text{mask}} \frac{a_u}{a_u + a_v}\) for each \(u \in V\) and \(\text{mask} \subseteq V\) in \(O(N 2^N)\) time, we can evaluate \(\operatorname{cut}(T, S \setminus T) = \prod_{u \in T} W(u, S \setminus T)\) in \(O(|T|)\) time (or \(O(1)\) per submask transition). 2. Let \(\text{dp}[S]\) be the probability that the subtournament on \(S\) is strongly connected. By partitioning all tournament outcomes on \(S\) according to their unique source SCC \(T \subseteq S\) (\(T \neq \emptyset\)):

\[ 1 = \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subseteq S} \text{dp}[T] \cdot \operatorname{cut}(T, S \setminus T) \implies \text{dp}[S] = 1 - \sum_{\emptyset \neq T \subsetneq S} \text{dp}[T] \cdot \operatorname{cut}(T, S \setminus T). \]

  1. The probability that \(S\) is the unique source SCC of the entire tournament on \(V\) is \(\text{dp}[S] \cdot \operatorname{cut}(S, V \setminus S)\), in which case all \(|S|\) members of \(S\) are potential winners. Hence

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{winners}] = \sum_{\emptyset \neq S \subseteq V} |S| \cdot \text{dp}[S] \cdot \operatorname{cut}(S, V \setminus S) \pmod{10^9 + 7}. \]

2. \(O(N \log N)\) Generalization: Symmetric Tournaments & General Digraphs (§13.3)

When edge weights are uniform (\(N \le 10^5\)), the subset DP collapses to EGF operations in \(O(N \log N)\) time:

  1. Strongly Connected Tournaments (\(S_{\text{tour}}(x)\)): Every non-empty tournament is uniquely a non-empty linear sequence (\(\operatorname{SEQ}_{\ge 1}\)) of strongly connected tournaments \(S_1 \to S_2 \to \dots \to S_r\). Since all cross-arcs between \(S_i\) and \(S_j\) (\(i < j\)) are deterministically directed from \(S_i\) to \(S_j\), the standard EGFs \(T(x) = \sum_{n=1}^N 2^{\binom{n}{2}} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) and \(S_{\text{tour}}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^N s_{\text{tour}, n} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) satisfy

\[ 1 + T(x) = \frac{1}{1 - S_{\text{tour}}(x)} \implies S_{\text{tour}}(x) = 1 - \left(1 + T(x)\right)^{-1} \pmod{x^{N+1}}. \]

  1. Strongly Connected Digraphs (\(C_{\text{SCC}}(x)\)): Let \(c_{\text{SCC}, n}\) be the number of strongly connected labeled digraphs on \(n\) vertices, with EGF \(C_{\text{SCC}}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_{\text{SCC}, n} \frac{x^n}{n!}\). As derived in §13.3, let

\[ F(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f_k \frac{x^k}{k!} = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{r-1} \frac{C_{\text{SCC}}(x)^r}{r!} = 1 - \exp\left(-C_{\text{SCC}}(x)\right) \]

be the signed EGF of a non-empty set of disjoint source SCCs. By Inclusion–Exclusion over the subset of source SCCs of total size \(k \in \{1, \dots, n\}\):

\[ 4^{\binom{n}{2}} = 2^{2\binom{n}{2}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \binom{n}{k} f_k \, 2^{k(n-k)} \, 2^{2\binom{n-k}{2}} \quad (n \ge 1). \]

Using \(k(n-k) + 2\binom{n-k}{2} = \binom{n}{2} - \binom{k}{2} + \binom{n-k}{2}\) and dividing by \(2^{\binom{n}{2}} n!\) yields \(\hat{F}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^N \frac{f_k}{2^{\binom{k}{2}} k!} x^k = 1 - \hat{G}(x)^{-1} \pmod{x^{N+1}}\) where \(\hat{G}(x) = \sum_{m=0}^N \frac{2^{\binom{m}{2}}}{m!} x^m\). Multiplying \([x^k]\hat{F}(x)\) by \(2^{\binom{k}{2}}\) recovers \(F(x)\), and taking the formal logarithm recovers

\[ C_{\text{SCC}}(x) = -\ln\left(1 - F(x)\right) \pmod{x^{N+1}}. \]

Problem Statement & Exact Constraints

You are given \(K\) integers \(n_1, n_2, \dots, n_K\). If \(G_i\) is chosen uniformly at random (or summed over all choices) among all \(2^{\binom{n_i}{2}}\) simple labeled graphs on \(n_i\) vertices for each \(i = 1, \dots, K\), compute the sum of the number of connected components of \(G_1 \times G_2 \times \dots \times G_K\) over all \(\prod_{i=1}^K 2^{\binom{n_i}{2}}\) tuples \((G_1, \dots, G_K)\), modulo \(998244353\).

  • Constraints: \(K \le 10^5\), \(N = \max_{1 \le i \le K} n_i \le 10^5\), time limit \(2.0\text{ s}\), modulus \(p = 998244353\).

Because the tensor product distributes over disjoint unions of connected components (\(G_1 \times \dots \times G_K = \bigsqcup (C_1 \times \dots \times C_K)\)), first ask Weichsel’s Question: Given \(K\) connected graphs \(C_1, \dots, C_K\) of sizes \(s_1, \dots, s_K\), how many connected components does their tensor product \(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K\) have?

How does the parity of walk lengths in \(C_i\) depend on whether \(C_i\) is bipartite vs. contains an odd cycle?
Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

\[ \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } b = 0, \\ 2^{b-1} & \text{if } b \ge 1, \end{cases} \qquad \text{which we can rewrite linearly as } \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2^b + \frac{1}{2} \cdot [b = 0]. \]

Look at that linear decomposition: \(\frac{1}{2} \prod_{i=1}^K 2^{[\text{is\_bip}(C_i)]} + \frac{1}{2} \prod_{i=1}^K [\text{not\_bip}(C_i)]\) completely factors across \(i = 1, \dots, K\)!

which is a single polynomial convolution with \(G(x)\)!

NoteComplete Derivation & Solution

1. Weichsel’s Theorem on Tensor Products of Connected Graphs

Every graph \(G_i\) decomposes into connected components \(G_i = \bigsqcup_{C_i \in \operatorname{Comp}(G_i)} C_i\). Because the tensor product distributes over disjoint unions,

\[ G_1 \times \dots \times G_K = \bigsqcup_{C_1 \in \operatorname{Comp}(G_1)} \dots \bigsqcup_{C_K \in \operatorname{Comp}(G_K)} (C_1 \times \dots \times C_K). \]

For any tuple of connected graphs \((C_1, \dots, C_K)\) with vertex counts \((s_1, \dots, s_K)\), let \(\kappa(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K)\) be the number of connected components of \(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K\):

  1. Case 1 (\(\min_i s_i = 1\)): At least one \(C_i\) is an isolated vertex \(K_1\) with no edges. Then \(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K\) has \(\prod_{i=1}^K s_i\) vertices and \(0\) edges, so

\[ \kappa(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K) = \prod_{i=1}^K s_i. \]

  1. Case 2 (\(s_i \ge 2\) for all \(i = 1, \dots, K\)): Every \(C_i\) is connected and has at least one edge, so every vertex in \(C_i\) has degree \(\ge 1\) (can walk back and forth to preserve path-length parity). Two vertices \((u_1, \dots, u_K)\) and \((v_1, \dots, v_K)\) are connected in \(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K\) if and only if there is an integer \(L \ge 0\) such that each \(C_i\) has a walk of length \(L\) from \(u_i\) to \(v_i\).
    • If \(C_i\) is non-bipartite, it contains an odd cycle, so for sufficiently large \(L\) there exist walks of both even and odd lengths between any \(u_i\) and \(v_i\).
    • If \(C_i\) is bipartite, the length of every walk between \(u_i\) and \(v_i\) has fixed parity \(d_{C_i}(u_i, v_i) \bmod 2\). Therefore, if \(b\) of the \(K\) graphs \(C_1, \dots, C_K\) are bipartite, the \(b\) parity constraints have \(2^b\) states split into pairs of opposite parities, giving

\[ \kappa(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } b = 0, \\[4pt] 2^{b-1} & \text{if } b \ge 1, \end{cases} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2^b + \frac{1}{2} \cdot [b = 0]. \]

2. Factoring the Sum Across \(i = 1, \dots, K\)

Look at how both cases factor into independent per-coordinate sums over \(G_i\):

  • In Case 1, write \(\mathbf{1}[\min_i s_i = 1] \prod_{i=1}^K s_i = \prod_{i=1}^K s_i - \prod_{i=1}^K \left(s_i \cdot \mathbf{1}[s_i \ge 2]\right)\). Since \(\sum_{C_i \in \operatorname{Comp}(G_i)} s_i = n_i\) for every graph \(G_i\), and \(\sum_{C_i : s_i \ge 2} s_i = n_i - (\text{\# of isolated vertices in } G_i)\), let:

    • \(A(n) = \sum_{G \text{ on } n} n = n \cdot 2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) be the total vertex sum over all graphs on \(n\) vertices.
    • \(A_{\ge 2}(n) = \sum_{G \text{ on } n} \sum_{\substack{C \in \operatorname{Comp}(G) \\ |C| \ge 2}} |C| = n \cdot 2^{\binom{n}{2}} - n \cdot 2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\) (since each of the \(n\) vertices is isolated in \(2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\) graphs!). Then the Case 1 contribution over all tuples \((G_1, \dots, G_K)\) is simply

\[ \text{Ans}_{\text{Case 1}} = \prod_{i=1}^{K} A(n_i) - \prod_{i=1}^{K} A_{\ge 2}(n_i). \]

  • In Case 2 (\(s_i \ge 2\) for all \(i\)), using \(\kappa = \frac{1}{2} \prod_{i=1}^K (1 + [\text{is\_bip}(C_i)]) + \frac{1}{2} \prod_{i=1}^K [\text{not\_bip}(C_i)]\), define for each \(n\):
    • \(P(n) = \sum_{G \text{ on } n} \sum_{\substack{C \in \operatorname{Comp}(G) \\ |C| \ge 2}} \left(1 + [\text{is\_bip}(C)]\right) = \sum_{s=2}^{n} \binom{n}{s} (c_s + c_{\text{bip}, s}) \, 2^{\binom{n-s}{2}}\),
    • \(Q(n) = \sum_{G \text{ on } n} \sum_{\substack{C \in \operatorname{Comp}(G) \\ |C| \ge 2}} [\text{not\_bip}(C)] = \sum_{s=2}^{n} \binom{n}{s} (c_s - c_{\text{bip}, s}) \, 2^{\binom{n-s}{2}}\). Then the Case 2 contribution over all tuples \((G_1, \dots, G_K)\) is

\[ \text{Ans}_{\text{Case 2}} = \frac{1}{2} \prod_{i=1}^{K} P(n_i) + \frac{1}{2} \prod_{i=1}^{K} Q(n_i). \]

Notice that \(P(n)\) and \(Q(n)\) for all \(n = 1, \dots, N\) are binomial convolutions of \((c_s \pm c_{\text{bip}, s})_{s \ge 2}\) with \(g_{n-s} = 2^{\binom{n-s}{2}}\), computable for all \(n \le N\) in a single NTT multiplication!

    3. Multiply the \(K\) factors in \(O(K)\) time. Total time: \(O(N \log N + K)\), space: \(O(N + K)\).
  • Core Invariant: Weichsel’s component identity \(\kappa(C_1 \times \dots \times C_K) = \frac{1}{2} 2^b + \frac{1}{2}[b=0]\) for \(s_i \ge 2\) linearizes the exponential dependence on the number of bipartite components \(b\) into two multiplicative products across \(i = 1, \dots, K\).
  • Failure Modes & Edge Cases: Isolated vertices (\(s_i = 1\)) are bipartite, but they do not obey the \(2^{b-1}\) formula because they have degree \(0\) and cannot walk back and forth to preserve parity! Always zero out the \(s = 1\) term (\(c_1 = c_{\text{bip}, 1} = 1\)) before convolving to form \(P(n)\) and \(Q(n)\), and handle \(\min_i s_i = 1\) separately via \(\text{Ans}_{\text{Case 1}}\).
