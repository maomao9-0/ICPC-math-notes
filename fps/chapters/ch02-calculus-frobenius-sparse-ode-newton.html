02 — Formal Calculus, 1D Newton Iteration (sqrt, ln, exp, pow) & The O(NK) Sparse Engine

Part I · Foundations of Formal Power Series & The Symbolic Method

In Chapter 01, we built the algebraic foundations of the formal power series ring \(K[[x]]\): Cauchy convolution, valuation, formal topology, and multiplicative inversion via the degree-doubling recurrence \(G_{k+1} = G_k(2 - F G_k) \bmod x^{2^{k+1}}\). Yet inversion solves only linear equations \(F(x) G(x) = 1\). Most combinatorial structures and non-linear recurrences lead to richer algebraic or differential relations:

  • What if a combinatorial class of rooted binary trees satisfies the quadratic equation \(C(x) F(x)^2 - F(x) + 1 = 0\)?
  • What if we need to raise a 10-term polynomial \(A(x) = \sum_{d=0}^9 x^d\) to the \(10^5\)-th power, or compute \(P(x)^K \bmod x^N\) when \(K = 10^{18}\) exceeds the prime modulus \(p = 998244353\)?
  • How can we take derivatives and integrals when \(x\) is just a formal placeholder with no real-number neighborhood or \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) limit?
  • Why do standard generating function recipes suddenly crash with division-by-zero when the modulus \(p\) is smaller than the target degree \(N\), and how does the Frobenius identity \(A(x)^p \equiv A(x^p) \pmod p\) turn that obstacle into an algorithmic speedup?

This chapter answers all four questions. We first define formal calculus—differentiation \(D_x\), the pointing operator \(\vartheta = x D_x\), and formal integration \(\int_0^x\)—purely as coefficient-shifting linear maps on \(K[[x]]\). We then audit characteristic-\(p\) obstructions, proving why division by \(1, 2, \dots, N\) separates \(p > N\) from \(p \le N\). Next, we lift linear inversion to 1D Newton-Raphson iteration on \(K[[x]]\), deriving the \(O(N \log N)\) engines for sqrt, ln, exp, and pow, alongside the two subtle modular arithmetic traps (Canonical Failure Modes #2 and #3) that break naive polynomial power templates. Finally, we construct the \(O(N \cdot K)\) Sparse Differential Equation Engine, showing how logarithmic derivatives let us compute \(P(x)^\alpha\), \(\exp P(x)\), \(\ln P(x)\), and \(\sqrt{P(x)}\) for \(K\)-sparse polynomials in pure \(O(N K)\) loops without a single Number-Theoretic Transform (NTT).

2.1 Formal Differentiation \(D_x\), Pointing Operator \(\vartheta = x D_x\) & Formal Integration \(\int\)

Calculus Without Limits

In real or complex analysis, the derivative of a function \(f(x)\) is defined via the limit \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\). In a formal power series ring \(K[[x]]\) over a finite field \(\mathbb{F}_p\) or an arbitrary commutative ring \(K\), there is no Euclidean metric on \(K\), and dividing by a continuous increment \(h \to 0\) makes no sense.

Instead, what does term-by-term differentiation actually do to a sequence of coefficients \((a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots)\)? It multiplies the \(n\)-th coefficient \(a_n\) by its index \(n\) and shifts it one position to the left (from \(x^n\) to \(x^{n-1}\)). We adopt this direct coefficient action as our definition.

NoteDefinition 2.1: Formal Derivative, Pointing Operator, and Formal Integral

Let \(K\) be a commutative ring with identity \(1_K\), and let \(A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n \in K[[x]]\).

  1. Formal Derivative (\(D_x\)): The formal derivative map \(D_x : K[[x]] \to K[[x]]\) (also written \(A'(x)\) or \(\frac{d}{dx} A(x)\)) is the \(K\)-linear operator defined by \[D_x A(x) = A'(x) := \sum_{n \ge 1} n a_n x^{n-1} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (n+1) a_{n+1} x^n.\] Here \(n a_n\) denotes the scalar \((1_K + 1_K + \dots + 1_K) a_n\) (\(n\) times) in the ring \(K\). Notice that \(D_x\) requires no division in \(K\) and is well-defined over any commutative ring \(K\).

  2. Pointing (Theta / Euler) Operator (\(\vartheta = x D_x\)): The pointing operator \(\vartheta : K[[x]] \to K[[x]]\) applies \(D_x\) and then multiplies by \(x\), preserving every monomial’s degree: \[\vartheta A(x) := x D_x A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} n a_n x^n.\] Combinatorially, if \(a_n\) counts unlabeled structures of size \(n\) (each built from \(n\) distinguishable atoms of weight \(1\)), then \(n a_n\) counts ways to choose a structure of size \(n\) and point at (distinguish) one of its \(n\) atoms.

  3. Formal Integral (\(\int_0^x\)): Suppose \(A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n\) is truncated modulo \(x^N\). Provided the integers \(1, 2, \dots, N\) are invertible units in \(K\) (i.e., \(1, 2, \dots, N \in K^\times\)), the formal integral operator \(\int_0^x : K[[x]] / (x^N) \to K[[x]] / (x^{N+1})\) is defined by \[\int_0^x A(t)\,dt := \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{a_n}{n+1} x^{n+1} \pmod{x^{N+1}}.\] By convention, the constant term \([x^0] \int_0^x A(t)\,dt\) is always set to \(0\).

Why is the unit condition \(1, 2, \dots, N \in K^\times\) attached to \(\int_0^x\) but not to \(D_x\)? Because multiplying \(a_n\) by \(n\) is always valid in any ring, whereas dividing \(a_n\) by \(n+1\) requires \((n+1)^{-1} \in K^\times\). When \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) is a prime field, \(1, 2, \dots, N \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\) holds if and only if \(p > N\).

Algebraic Proofs of the Rules of Calculus

To use calculus safely over \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), we must verify that the familiar rules of differentiation—sum, product, power, quotient, and chain rule—hold as exact algebraic identities between coefficient sequences, without invoking limits.

TipTheorem 2.2: Rules of Formal Differentiation in \(K[[x]]\)

For any commutative ring \(K\) and any formal power series \(A(x), B(x) \in K[[x]]\):

  1. \(K\)-Linearity: \(D_x(c A + d B) = c D_x A + d D_x B\) for all \(c, d \in K\).
  2. Leibniz Product Rule: \(D_x(A(x) B(x)) = A'(x) B(x) + A(x) B'(x)\).
  3. Power Rule: For every integer \(k \ge 1\), \(D_x(A(x)^k) = k A(x)^{k-1} A'(x)\).
  4. Quotient Rule: If \(B(x) \in K[[x]]^\times\) (i.e., \([x^0] B(x) \in K^\times\)), then \[D_x\!\left(\frac{A(x)}{B(x)}\right) = \frac{A'(x) B(x) - A(x) B'(x)}{B(x)^2}.\]
  5. Formal Chain Rule: If \(F(x) \in K[[x]]\) has strictly positive valuation \(\operatorname{val}(F) \ge 1\) (so \([x^0]F(x) = 0\) and the formal composition \(G(F(x))\) is a well-defined finite sum at every degree), then for every \(G(y) \in K[[y]]\), \[D_x\big(G(F(x))\big) = (D_y G)(F(x)) \cdot D_x F(x).\]

Proof (by Direct Coefficient Extraction):

  1. Linearity is immediate from \([x^n] D_x(cA + dB) = (n+1)(c a_{n+1} + d b_{n+1}) = c(n+1)a_{n+1} + d(n+1)b_{n+1}\).
  2. Product Rule: Extract the coefficient of \(x^n\) on the left-hand side of \(D_x(A \cdot B)\). By the definition of \(D_x\) and Cauchy convolution, \[[x^n] D_x(A(x) B(x)) = (n+1) [x^{n+1}](A(x) B(x)) = (n+1) \sum_{i+j=n+1} a_i b_j.\] Now split the multiplier \((n+1)\) into \((i + j)\) inside the sum: \[\sum_{i+j=n+1} (i + j) a_i b_j = \sum_{i+j=n+1} (i a_i) b_j + \sum_{i+j=n+1} a_i (j b_j).\] In the first sum, the \(i=0\) term vanishes because \(0 \cdot a_0 = 0\); re-indexing \(i' = i-1\) gives \(\sum_{i'+j=n} (i'+1) a_{i'+1} b_j = [x^n](A'(x) B(x))\). By symmetry, the second sum equals \([x^n](A(x) B'(x))\). Since the coefficients of \(x^n\) agree for all \(n \ge 0\), \(D_x(AB) = A'B + AB'\).
  3. Power Rule: Follows by induction on \(k \ge 1\) using the Product Rule on \(A(x)^k = A(x)^{k-1} \cdot A(x)\).
  4. Quotient Rule: First consider \(A(x) = 1\). Differentiating the identity \(B(x) \cdot B(x)^{-1} = 1\) via the Product Rule yields \(B'(x) B(x)^{-1} + B(x) D_x(B(x)^{-1}) = D_x(1) = 0\). Since \(B(x)\) is invertible, multiplying by \(B(x)^{-1}\) gives \(D_x(B(x)^{-1}) = -B'(x) / B(x)^2\). Applying the Product Rule to \(A(x) \cdot B(x)^{-1}\) then yields the general Quotient Rule.
  5. Formal Chain Rule: Write \(G(y) = \sum_{k \ge 0} g_k y^k\). Because \(\operatorname{val}(F) \ge 1\), we have \(\operatorname{val}(F(x)^k) \ge k\), so the coefficient of \(x^n\) in \(G(F(x))\) depends only on the finite truncation \(\sum_{k=0}^{n+1} g_k F(x)^k\). Applying linearity and the Power Rule to this finite sum modulo \(x^{n+1}\) gives \[D_x\big(G(F(x))\big) \equiv \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} g_k \cdot k F(x)^{k-1} F'(x) \equiv (D_y G)(F(x)) \cdot F'(x) \pmod{x^{n+1}}.\] Since this congruence holds for every \(n \ge 0\), the two series are identical in \(K[[x]]\). \(\blacksquare\)

The Fundamental Theorem of Formal Calculus

How do \(D_x\) and \(\int_0^x\) interact when \(1, 2, \dots, N \in K^\times\)?

  • Differentiating an integral recovers the exact series: For any \(A(x) \in K[[x]] / (x^N)\), \[D_x \left(\int_0^x A(t)\,dt\right) = D_x \left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \frac{a_n}{n+1} x^{n+1}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} (n+1)\frac{a_n}{n+1} x^n = A(x) \pmod{x^N}.\]
  • Integrating a derivative recovers all terms of degree \(\ge 1\), zeroing out the constant term: For any \(F(x) = \sum_{n=0}^N f_n x^n\), \[\int_0^x F'(t)\,dt = \int_0^x \left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} (n+1) f_{n+1} t^n\right) dt = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f_{n+1} x^{n+1} = F(x) - f_0 \pmod{x^{N+1}}.\]
ImportantThe Constant-of-Integration Rule

Whenever you solve a differential equation \(F'(x) = H(x)\) by integrating both sides, \(\int_0^x H(t)\,dt\) always has constant term \(0\). You must explicitly add back the initial condition \(f_0 = [x^0] F(x)\): \[F(x) \equiv [x^0]F(x) + \int_0^x F'(t)\,dt \pmod{x^{N+1}}.\]

TipThe Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Index-Multiplier Alignment

The Diagnostic Signal: You encounter a linear or non-linear recurrence where each term \(a_{n-d}\) is multiplied by a linear or polynomial function of \(n\), such as \((n+1) a_{n+1} = \alpha a_n - (n-1) a_{n-1}\). Ordinary convolution cannot handle variable multipliers like \(n\), because Cauchy convolution only sees the difference of indices.

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Golden Rule of Index Alignment): Always rewrite every multiplier so that the integer multiplying \(a_m\) is its own index \(m\):

  1. If you want to generate \(m a_m\) sitting at power \(x^n\), first look at \(A'(x) = \sum_{m \ge 1} m a_m x^{m-1}\), where \(m a_m\) sits at power \(x^{m-1}\).
  2. Multiply \(A'(x)\) by whichever power of \(x\) shifts \(x^{m-1}\) to \(x^n\): specifically, multiply by \(x^{n - m + 1}\).
    • For \(m = n+1\): multiply \(A'(x)\) by \(x^0 = 1 \implies [x^n] A'(x) = (n+1) a_{n+1}\).
    • For \(m = n\): multiply \(A'(x)\) by \(x^1 = x \implies [x^n] (x A'(x)) = n a_n\).
    • For \(m = n-d\): multiply \(A'(x)\) by \(x^{d+1} \implies [x^n] (x^{d+1} A'(x)) = (n-d) a_{n-d}\).
  3. What if the recurrence has \((n + c) a_{n-d}\) instead of \((n - d) a_{n-d}\)? Split the multiplier into the aligned index plus a constant remainder: \[(n + c) a_{n-d} = \underbrace{(n - d) a_{n-d}}_{[x^n]\big(x^{d+1} A'(x)\big)} + \underbrace{(c + d) a_{n-d}}_{(c+d)\,[x^n]\big(x^d A(x)\big)}.\]

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4\)): The Generalized Binomial Series via ODE

Suppose we want to compute the coefficients \(f_n = [x^n] F(x)\) of \(F(x) = (1 + x)^\alpha\) for an arbitrary scalar \(\alpha \in K\) (assuming \(1, 2, 3, 4 \in K^\times\)).

  1. Form the Differential Equation: Differentiate \(F(x) = (1+x)^\alpha\) using the Power/Chain rule: \[F'(x) = \alpha (1+x)^{\alpha - 1}.\] Notice that \((1+x)^{\alpha-1}\) is just \(\frac{F(x)}{1+x}\). Multiply both sides by \((1+x)\) to clear the denominator and keep the polynomial sparse: \[(1 + x) F'(x) = \alpha F(x) \implies F'(x) + x F'(x) = \alpha F(x).\]
  2. Extract \([x^n]\) using the Operator Dictionary:
    • \([x^n] F'(x) = (n+1) f_{n+1}\)
    • \([x^n] (x F'(x)) = n f_n\)
    • \([x^n] (\alpha F(x)) = \alpha f_n\) Combining these gives the 2-term recurrence: \[(n+1) f_{n+1} + n f_n = \alpha f_n \implies f_{n+1} = \frac{\alpha - n}{n+1} f_n.\]
  3. Trace for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\) starting from \(f_0 = (1+0)^\alpha = 1\):
    • \(n = 0\): \(f_1 = \frac{\alpha - 0}{1} f_0 = \alpha\).
    • \(n = 1\): \(f_2 = \frac{\alpha - 1}{2} f_1 = \frac{\alpha(\alpha - 1)}{2}\).
    • \(n = 2\): \(f_3 = \frac{\alpha - 2}{3} f_2 = \frac{\alpha(\alpha - 1)(\alpha - 2)}{6}\).
    • \(n = 3\): \(f_4 = \frac{\alpha - 3}{4} f_3 = \frac{\alpha(\alpha - 1)(\alpha - 2)(\alpha - 3)}{24}\).

In four elementary steps—with no Taylor limits—formal differentiation and index alignment recover Newton’s generalized binomial coefficients \(\binom{\alpha}{n} = \frac{\alpha^{\underline{n}}}{n!}\).

2.2 Characteristic-\(p\) Obstructions, The Frobenius Endomorphism & Lucas Breakdown

In competitive programming, almost all generating function calculations happen over \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\) where \(p\) is a prime modulus. Most of the time, \(p = 998244353\) or \(10^9 + 7\) while \(N \le 5 \times 10^5\), so \(p > N\). However, a dedicated class of problems sets \(p\) to a small prime (\(p = 2, 3, 5, \text{ or } 10007 \le N\)). When \(p \le N\), the bridge between derivatives and integrals collapses. Understanding why it collapses—and what algebraic tools replace it—is essential.

Canonical Failure Mode #4: Characteristic-\(p\) Division Breakdown for \(p \le N\)

WarningCanonical Failure Mode #4: Characteristic-\(p\) Division Breakdown (\(p \le N\))

What Goes Wrong: Over \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), every multiple of \(p\) is \(0\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p\). When \(N \ge p\), the integer \(p\) is not invertible in \(\mathbb{F}_p\). Consequently:

  1. Non-trivial Kernel of \(D_x\): Over \(\mathbb{Q}[[x]]\), \(F'(x) = 0\) implies \(F(x) = f_0\) is a constant series. Over \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), differentiating \(x^{kp}\) gives \(kp x^{kp-1} \equiv 0 \pmod p\) for every integer \(k \ge 1\)! Thus the kernel of \(D_x\) is the entire subring of \(p\)-th powers: \[\ker\big(D_x : \mathbb{F}_p[[x]] \to \mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\big) = \mathbb{F}_p[[x^p]] = \left\{\sum_{k \ge 0} c_k x^{kp} : c_k \in \mathbb{F}_p\right\}.\] Differentiation completely destroys the coefficients \(f_p, f_{2p}, f_{3p}, \dots\), making it impossible to recover \(f_{kp}\) from \(F'(x)\) alone.
  2. Cokernel Obstruction of \(\int_0^x\): In the formal integral \(\int_0^x A(t)\,dt = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n+1} x^{n+1}\), setting \(n = p - 1\) requires dividing \(a_{p-1}\) by \(p \equiv 0 \pmod p\). Even if \(a_{p-1} = 0\), the coefficient of \(x^p\) in any integrated series is indeterminate because \(D_x(x^p) = 0\).
  3. Radius of Definition for \(\ln\), \(\exp\), and ODEs:
    • \(\ln(1 + x) = \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n} x^n\) crashes at \([x^p]\) because \(1/p\) does not exist in \(\mathbb{F}_p\).
    • \(\exp(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{x^n}{n!}\) crashes at \([x^p]\) because \(p! \equiv 0 \pmod p\).
    • Any ODE method (sparse_ln, sparse_exp, sparse_pow, or dense Newton exp) that divides by the loop index \(n\) at step \(n = p\) triggers a division-by-zero error when \(N \ge p\).

How to Diagnose & Remedy: Always check whether \(p > N\) before invoking \(\int_0^x\), \(\ln\), \(\exp\), or EGF division by \(n!\). When \(p \le N\), switch to derivative-free or Frobenius-aware tools (summarized in the Remedy Table at the end of this section).

The Frobenius Endomorphism: Turning the Obstacle into a Speedup

While characteristic \(p\) breaks integration at degree \(p\), it simultaneously grants \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\) an extraordinary algebraic symmetry that does not exist over \(\mathbb{Q}\) or \(\mathbb{R}\): raising a polynomial or formal power series to the \(p\)-th power requires zero convolutions!

TipTheorem 2.3: The Frobenius Endomorphism in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\)

Let \(p\) be a prime and let \(A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n \in \mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\). Then: \[A(x)^p = A(x^p) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^{np} \quad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_p[[x]].\] More generally, for any integer \(k \ge 1\) and \(q = p^k\), \(A(x)^q = A(x^q)\).

Proof (Two-Step Verification):

  1. Binomial Divisibility Lemma: For any \(1 \le j \le p-1\), the binomial coefficient \(\binom{p}{j} = \frac{p!}{j!(p-j)!}\) has a factor of \(p\) in the numerator, while neither \(j!\) nor \((p-j)!\) is divisible by the prime \(p\). Therefore, \(\binom{p}{j} \equiv 0 \pmod p\) for all \(1 \le j \le p-1\). Consequently, for any two series \(U, V \in \mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), \[(U + V)^p = U^p + \sum_{j=1}^{p-1} \binom{p}{j} U^{p-j} V^j + V^p = U^p + V^p \quad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_p[[x]].\]
  2. Coefficient Action via Fermat’s Little Theorem: Applying \((U+V)^p = U^p + V^p\) inductively to any finite truncation \(\sum_{n=0}^N a_n x^n\) (which determines \([x^m] A(x)^p\) for all \(m \le N\)) gives \[\left(\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n\right)^p = \sum_{n \ge 0} (a_n x^n)^p = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n^p x^{np}.\] By Fermat’s Little Theorem, every element \(a_n \in \mathbb{F}_p\) satisfies \(a_n^p = a_n\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p\). Substituting \(a_n^p = a_n\) yields \(\sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^{np} = A(x^p)\). Induction on \(k\) immediately gives \(A(x)^{p^k} = A(x^{p^k})\). \(\blacksquare\)
NoteCaution: General Ring vs. Prime Field \(\mathbb{F}_p\)

Notice the two distinct steps in the proof above:

  • In any commutative ring \(R\) of characteristic \(p\) (for instance, a polynomial ring \(\mathbb{F}_p[t]\) or a finite extension field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}\)), the Frobenius map raises each coefficient to its \(p\)-th power: \(\left(\sum a_n x^n\right)^p = \sum a_n^p x^{np}\).
  • Only when the coefficients lie in the prime field \(\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\) does Fermat’s Little Theorem simplify \(a_n^p\) to \(a_n\), giving \(A(x)^p = A(x^p)\).

Algorithmic Application: Base-\(p\) Frobenius Decomposition of \(A(x)^K \bmod (p, x^N)\)

Suppose \(p \le N\) and we need to compute \(A(x)^K \bmod x^N\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\). We cannot use \(\exp(K \ln A(x))\) because \(\ln\) and \(\exp\) fail at degree \(p\). Instead, divide \(K\) by \(p\): write \(K = q p + r\) with \(0 \le r < p\), or expand \(K\) in base \(p\) as \(K = \sum_{i \ge 0} k_i p^i\) (\(0 \le k_i < p\)). By Theorem 2.3: \[A(x)^K = A(x)^{q p + r} = \big(A(x)^p\big)^q \cdot A(x)^r = A(x^p)^q \cdot A(x)^r \pmod p,\] and in full base-\(p\) form: \[A(x)^K = \prod_{i \ge 0} \Big(A\big(x^{p^i}\big)\Big)^{k_i} \pmod p.\] What makes \(A(x^p)^q \bmod x^N\) so fast? Every nonzero term in \(A(x^p)\) has degree divisible by \(p\)! Therefore, computing \(A(x^p)^q \bmod x^N\) is isomorphic to substituting \(y = x^p\) and computing \(A(y)^q \bmod y^{\lceil N/p \rceil}\) at one-\(p\)-th the degree!

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4, p = 3\)): Computing \((1 + 2x + x^2)^7 \bmod (3, x^5)\)

Let \(A(x) = 1 + 2x + x^2 \in \mathbb{F}_3[[x]]\) and let us compute \(A(x)^7 \bmod x^5\) in \(\mathbb{F}_3\). Write the exponent \(7\) in base \(p = 3\): \(7 = 2 \cdot 3 + 1\). By the Frobenius Endomorphism (Theorem 2.3): \[A(x)^7 = \big(A(x)^3\big)^2 \cdot A(x)^1 = A(x^3)^2 \cdot A(x) \quad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_3[[x]].\]

  1. Dilation step (\(A(x^3) \bmod x^5\)): Replace \(x\) by \(x^3\) in \(A(x) = 1 + 2x + x^2\) and truncate at \(x^4\): \[A(x^3) = 1 + 2x^3 + x^6 \equiv 1 + 2x^3 \pmod{x^5}.\]
  2. Square the dilated series modulo \(x^5\): \[A(x^3)^2 \equiv (1 + 2x^3)^2 = 1 + 4x^3 + 4x^6 \equiv 1 + x^3 \pmod{x^5} \quad (\text{since } 4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3).\]
  3. Multiply by \(A(x)^1 = 1 + 2x + x^2\) modulo \(x^5\): \[A(x)^7 \equiv (1 + x^3)(1 + 2x + x^2) = 1 + 2x + x^2 + x^3 + 2x^4 + x^5 \equiv 1 + 2x + x^2 + x^3 + 2x^4 \pmod{x^5}.\] Notice we never divided by \(3\), never called \(\ln\) or \(\exp\), and computed a 7th power by a trivial two-term shift!

The Lucas Obstruction to Exponential Generating Functions

Why does the Frobenius identity also govern binomial coefficients and Exponential Generating Functions (EGFs) modulo \(p\)? Consider \((1 + x)^n\) in \(\mathbb{F}_p[x]\). Writing \(n = \sum_{i \ge 0} n_i p^i\) in base \(p\) (\(0 \le n_i < p\)), Theorem 2.3 yields \[(1 + x)^n = \prod_{i \ge 0} \big((1 + x)^{p^i}\big)^{n_i} = \prod_{i \ge 0} \big(1 + x^{p^i}\big)^{n_i} \quad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_p[x].\] To extract the coefficient \([x^k](1 + x)^n = \binom{n}{k} \bmod p\), expand \(k = \sum_{i \ge 0} k_i p^i\) in base \(p\) (\(0 \le k_i < p\)). Since base-\(p\) representations are unique, the only way to form \(x^k\) by multiplying one term from each factor \((1 + x^{p^i})^{n_i}\) (whose degrees are \(0, p^i, 2p^i, \dots, n_i p^i < p^{i+1}\)) is to pick the \(x^{k_i p^i}\) term from the \(i\)-th factor! This proves Lucas’s Theorem: \[\binom{n}{k} \equiv \prod_{i \ge 0} \binom{n_i}{k_i} \pmod p.\]

What does Lucas’s Theorem mean for Exponential Generating Functions \(A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n!} x^n\) when \(p \le N\)?

  1. Algebraic Breakdown: For \(n \ge p\), the denominator \(n!\) is \(0 \pmod p\), so \(\frac{a_n}{n!}\) cannot even be represented in \(\mathbb{F}_p\).
  2. Combinatorial Breakdown (No Base-\(p\) Carries): Even if we keep \(a_n\) and \(b_m\) unscaled and look at the binomial convolution \(c_n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} a_k b_{n-k} \pmod p\), Lucas’s Theorem says \(\binom{n}{k} \equiv 0 \pmod p\) whenever adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p\) produces a carry in any digit position! Thus, binomial convolution modulo \(p\) disconnects into independent digit-wise convolutions across base-\(p\) digits, blocking the standard EGF mechanism.

2.3 1D Newton-Raphson Quadratic Convergence, sqrt, ln, exp, pow & Valuation Hazards

We now return to the standard competitive programming regime where \(1, 2, \dots, N \in K^\times\) (for instance, \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) with \(p > N\)). In Chapter 01, we computed \(F(x)^{-1} \bmod x^N\) by doubling the number of correct coefficients in each step: \(G_{k+1} = G_k(2 - F G_k) \bmod x^{2^{k+1}}\). That formula was not an isolated trick—it is a special case of 1D Newton-Raphson Iteration on the formal power series ring \(K[[x]]\).

The 3-Line Proof of 1D Newton-Raphson on \(K[[x]]\)

Suppose we have an unknown target series \(F(x) \in K[[x]]\) that satisfies an equation \(\Phi(F(x)) = 0 \pmod{x^N}\), where \(\Phi(G)\) is a smooth mapping on formal power series (such as \(\Phi(G) = G^2 - P(x)\) or \(\Phi(G) = \ln G - B(x)\)).

TipTheorem 2.4: 1D Newton-Raphson Quadratic Convergence in \(K[[x]]\)

Let \(\Phi(G)\) be a formal mapping with formal Fréchet derivative \(\Phi'(G) = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial G}(G)\). Suppose we have an approximation \(G_k(x) \in K[[x]]\) that agrees with the true solution \(F(x)\) in its first \(2^k\) coefficients: \[F(x) - G_k(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{x^{2^k}}.\] Provided the constant term of the derivative is a unit, \([x^0]\Phi'(G_0) \in K^\times\) (which guarantees \(\Phi'(G_k)\) is invertible in \(K[[x]]\) for all \(k\)), the next iterate defined by \[G_{k+1}(x) := G_k(x) - \frac{\Phi(G_k(x))}{\Phi'(G_k(x))} \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}\] satisfies \(F(x) - G_{k+1}(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}\). That is, every Newton step exactly doubles the number of correct coefficients.

The 3-Line Proof:

  1. Valuation Squaring: Since \(\operatorname{val}(F - G_k) \ge 2^k\), the square of their difference has valuation at least \(2 \cdot 2^k = 2^{k+1}\): \[\big(F(x) - G_k(x)\big)^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}.\]
  2. First-Order Taylor Expansion: Expand \(\Phi(F)\) around \(G_k\) in powers of the perturbation \(\Delta = F - G_k\). Because \(\Delta^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}\), all terms of order \(\ge 2\) vanish modulo \(x^{2^{k+1}}\): \[0 \equiv \Phi(F) \equiv \Phi(G_k) + \Phi'(G_k)\big(F - G_k\big) \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}.\]
  3. Solve for \(F\): Since \([x^0]\Phi'(G_k) = [x^0]\Phi'(G_0) \in K^\times\), the series \(\Phi'(G_k)\) is invertible modulo \(x^{2^{k+1}}\). Dividing by \(\Phi'(G_k)\) gives \[F(x) \equiv G_k(x) - \frac{\Phi(G_k(x))}{\Phi'(G_k(x))} \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}. \quad \blacksquare\]
NoteMaster Complexity Principle: Why Doubling Costs \(O(N \log N)\), Not \(O(N \log^2 N)\)

If each step of Newton iteration costs \(T(m) = c \cdot m \log m\) operations at length \(m = 2^k\), summing over \(m = 1, 2, 4, \dots, N\) yields a geometric series dominated by its final term: \[T_{\text{total}}(N) = \sum_{k=0}^{\lceil \log_2 N \rceil} T(2^k) \le c \left(N \log N + \frac{N}{2} \log N + \frac{N}{4} \log N + \dots\right) = 2c N \log N = O(N \log N).\] This single geometric-series property is why inv, sqrt, exp, and pow all run in \(O(N \log N)\) rather than \(O(N \log^2 N)\).

Primitive 1: Formal Square Root (sqrt)

Given \(P(x) \in K[[x]]\), we seek \(F(x) \in K[[x]]\) such that \(F(x)^2 \equiv P(x) \pmod{x^N}\).

  • The Diagnostic Signal: A quadratic formula \(\frac{-B \pm \sqrt{B^2 - 4AC}}{2A}\) from Catalan-like trees, lattice paths, or radical generating functions requires extracting the square root of a known series \(P(x)\).
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad (Unit Case \([x^0]P = p_0 \in K^\times\)): To find \(F\) with \(F^2 = P(x)\), treat \(P(x)\) as a fixed constant with respect to \(G\) and set \[\Phi(G) = G^2 - P(x) = 0 \implies \Phi'(G) = 2G.\] Crucial hypothesis: for \(\Phi'(G_0) = 2 g_0\) to be a unit in \(K\), we need \(2 \in K^\times\) (in \(\mathbb{F}_p\), this requires \(p > 2\), i.e., an odd prime) and \(g_0 \in K^\times\) where \(g_0^2 = p_0\). Substituting \(\Phi(G_k)\) and \(\Phi'(G_k)\) into Theorem 2.4 gives: \[G_{k+1} = G_k - \frac{G_k^2 - P}{2 G_k} = \frac{2 G_k^2 - G_k^2 + P}{2 G_k} = \frac{1}{2}\left(G_k + \frac{P(x)}{G_k}\right) \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}.\]
  • What if \([x^0]P(x) = 0\)? (Valuation Parity Shift): If \(P(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{x^N}\), then \(F(x) = 0\) is a valid square root. Otherwise, factor out the lowest nonzero power of \(x\): write \(P(x) = x^v \widetilde{P}(x)\) where \(v = \operatorname{val}(P) < N\) and \(\widetilde{p}_0 = [x^0]\widetilde{P}(x) \ne 0\). For \(F(x)^2 = x^v \widetilde{P}(x)\) to hold in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), the valuation \(v = 2 \operatorname{val}(F)\) must be even (\(v = 2m\)), and \(\widetilde{p}_0\) must be a quadratic residue modulo \(p\) (tested via Euler’s criterion \(\widetilde{p}_0^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod p\), with \(g_0 = \sqrt{\widetilde{p}_0} \bmod p\) found in \(O(\log p)\) time via Cipolla’s or Tonelli–Shanks’s algorithm). Then \[\sqrt{P(x)} \equiv x^m \sqrt{\widetilde{P}(x) \bmod x^{N - m}} \pmod{x^N}.\] Notice that because multiplying by \(x^m\) shifts degrees up by \(m\), we only need to compute \(\sqrt{\widetilde{P}(x)}\) to precision \(x^{N - m}\) (or \(x^{N - 2m}\) if we only trust the input \(P(x)\) modulo \(x^N\); in standard competitive programming library problems like Library Checker sqrt_of_formal_power_series, the input coefficients \(p_0, \dots, p_{N-1}\) are treated as exact up to \(x^N\) and \(\sqrt{\widetilde{P}}\) is computed modulo \(x^{N-m}\)).

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4\)): Computing \(\sqrt{1 - 4x} \bmod x^4\)

Let \(P(x) = 1 - 4x\) (the discriminant inside the Catalan generating function \(\frac{1 - \sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}\)).

  1. Base case (\(2^0 = 1\)): \(p_0 = 1\), so choose \(G_0(x) = 1 \pmod x\).
  2. Step \(k = 0 \to 1\) (\(\bmod x^2\)): \[G_1(x) \equiv \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{1 - 4x}{1}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(2 - 4x) = 1 - 2x \pmod{x^2}.\]
  3. Step \(k = 1 \to 2\) (\(\bmod x^4\)): First invert \(G_1(x) = 1 - 2x\) modulo \(x^4\): \[G_1(x)^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - 2x} \equiv 1 + 2x + 4x^2 + 8x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\] Multiply by \(P(x) = 1 - 4x\) modulo \(x^4\): \[P(x) G_1(x)^{-1} \equiv (1 - 4x)(1 + 2x + 4x^2 + 8x^3) \equiv 1 - 2x - 4x^2 - 8x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\] Average with \(G_1(x) = 1 - 2x\): \[G_2(x) \equiv \frac{1}{2}\big((1 - 2x) + (1 - 2x - 4x^2 - 8x^3)\big) = 1 - 2x - 2x^2 - 4x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\] Let us hand-check by squaring \(G_2(x)\): \((1 - 2x - 2x^2 - 4x^3)^2 = 1 - 4x + (4 - 4)x^2 + (-8 + 8)x^3 + O(x^4) = 1 - 4x \pmod{x^4}\). Exact match!
NoteAlgorithm Contract: sqrt(P, N)
  • Input: Polynomial \(P(x)\) truncated modulo \(x^N\) over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (\(p > 2\) odd prime).
  • Preconditions: If \(P \not\equiv 0 \pmod{x^N}\) with \(v = \operatorname{val}(P) < N\) and \(P(x) = x^v \widetilde{P}(x)\), then \(v\) must be even (\(v = 2m\)) and \([x^0]\widetilde{P}\) must be a quadratic residue in \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\) (i.e., \((\widetilde{p}_0)^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod p\)).   • Output: \(F(x) \bmod x^N\) satisfying \(F(x)^2 \equiv P(x) \pmod{x^N}\) (unique up to sign \(\pm F(x)\) once \([x^m]F = \sqrt{\widetilde{p}_0}\) is fixed).
  • Complexity: \(T_{\text{sqrt}}(N) \approx 2 T_{\text{inv}}(N) = O(N \log N)\) time, \(O(N)\) space (can be reduced to \(\frac{11}{6} E(N)\) by co-maintaining \(H_k = G_k^{-1} \bmod x^{2^{k-1}}\)).
  • Correctness Invariant: After iteration \(k\), \(G_k(x)^2 \equiv \widetilde{P}(x) \pmod{x^{2^k}}\), so \(\operatorname{val}(G_k^2 - \widetilde{P}) \ge 2^k\).

Primitive 2: Formal Logarithm (ln)

TipProposition 2.4 (ODE Characterization of Formal Exp and Log)

Let \(K\) be a commutative ring in which \(1, 2, \dots, N \in K^\times\) (in particular, \(\operatorname{char}(K) = 0\) or \(K = \mathbb{F}_p\) with prime \(p > N\)). For any series \(A(x) \in K[[x]] / (x^{N+1})\) with \([x^0]A(x) = 1\), and any series \(B(x) \in K[[x]] / (x^{N+1})\) with \([x^0]B(x) = 0\): \[ G(x) = \ln A(x) \iff G'(x) = \frac{A'(x)}{A(x)} \text{ with } [x^0]G(x) = 0, \qquad F(x) = \exp(B(x)) \iff F'(x) = B'(x) F(x) \text{ with } [x^0]F(x) = 1. \]

Given \(A(x) \in K[[x]]\) with constant term \([x^0]A(x) = 1\), we define its formal logarithm via the power series \(\ln(1 + u) = \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k} u^k\) substituted at \(u = A(x) - 1\) (which has valuation \(\ge 1\)). How do we compute \(\ln A(x) \bmod x^N\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time?

  • The Diagnostic Signal: We want to convert an infinite product or an exponentiation \(A(x)^\alpha\) into an additive sum, or extract connected components from labeled graphs (\(C(x) = \ln G(x)\)).
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Direct substitution into \(u - u^2/2 + u^3/3 - \dots\) would require \(N\) polynomial multiplications! Instead, apply the Formal Chain Rule (Theorem 2.2(5)) to differentiate \(G(x) = \ln(1 + u(x))\) where \(u(x) = A(x) - 1\): \[D_x G(x) = \left(\sum_{k \ge 1} (-1)^{k-1} u(x)^{k-1}\right) u'(x) = \frac{u'(x)}{1 + u(x)} = \frac{A'(x)}{A(x)}.\] Since \([x^0] G(x) = \ln(1 + 0) = 0\), applying the Fundamental Theorem of Formal Calculus (\(\int_0^x\)) recovers \(G(x)\) in one derivative, one inversion, one convolution, and one integral: \[\ln A(x) \equiv \int_0^x \frac{A'(t)}{A(t)}\,dt \pmod{x^N}.\]

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4\)): Computing \(\ln\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right) \bmod x^4\)

Let \(A(x) = \frac{1}{1-x} \equiv 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 \pmod{x^4}\).

  1. Differentiate \(A(x) \bmod x^4 \to A'(x) \bmod x^3\): \[A'(x) \equiv 1 + 2x + 3x^2 \pmod{x^3}.\]
  2. Invert \(A(x) \bmod x^3\): Since \(A(x) = (1-x)^{-1}\), its inverse is \(A(x)^{-1} \equiv 1 - x \pmod{x^3}\).
  3. Multiply \(A'(x) \cdot A(x)^{-1} \bmod x^3\): \[A'(x) A(x)^{-1} \equiv (1 + 2x + 3x^2)(1 - x) = 1 + (2-1)x + (3-2)x^2 - 3x^3 \equiv 1 + x + x^2 \pmod{x^3}.\]
  4. Integrate \(\int_0^x (1 + t + t^2)\,dt \bmod x^4\): \[\ln A(x) \equiv 0 + \frac{1}{1}x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 = x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\]
NoteAlgorithm Contract: ln(A, N)
  • Input: \(A(x) \bmod x^N\) over \(K\).
  • Preconditions: \([x^0] A(x) = 1\) and \(1, 2, \dots, N-1 \in K^\times\) (in \(\mathbb{F}_p\), requires \(p \ge N\)). If \([x^0]A(x) \ne 1\), \(\ln A(x)\) is not defined as a formal power series over \(\mathbb{F}_p\)!
  • Output: \(G(x) = \ln A(x) \bmod x^N\) with \([x^0]G(x) = 0\).
  • Complexity: 1 call to inv of length \(N-1\) plus 1 NTT multiplication of length \(N-1\), costing \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space.
  • Correctness Invariant: \([x^{n-1}](A'(x) A(x)^{-1}) = n g_n\) for all \(1 \le n < N\); dividing by \(n \in K^\times\) uniquely determines \(g_n\).

Primitive 3: Formal Exponential (exp)

Given \(B(x) \in K[[x]]\) with constant term \([x^0] B(x) = 0\), we define its formal exponential via \(\exp(B(x)) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{B(x)^k}{k!}\). Because \(\operatorname{val}(B) \ge 1\), this sum is formally convergent whenever \(1, 2, \dots, N-1 \in K^\times\).

  • The Diagnostic Signal: In labeled counting (Chapter 04), forming an unordered set of connected components translates to \(F(x) = \exp(B(x))\). In polynomial exponentiation or product-to-sum transforms, \(\prod_i F_i(x) = \exp\left(\sum_i \ln F_i(x)\right)\) requires recovering the product from its summed logarithm.
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: To solve \(F(x) = \exp(B(x))\), take the formal logarithm of both sides to linearize the unknown \(F\): \[\Phi(G) = \ln G(x) - B(x) = 0.\] What is the Fréchet derivative \(\Phi'(G) = \frac{\partial}{\partial G}(\ln G - B(x))\)? Here \(B(x)\) does not depend on \(G\), and \(\frac{d}{dG} \ln G = G^{-1}\). Plugging \(\Phi(G_k) = \ln G_k - B\) and \(\Phi'(G_k) = G_k^{-1}\) into 1D Newton-Raphson (Theorem 2.4) yields: \[G_{k+1}(x) = G_k(x) - \frac{\ln G_k(x) - B(x)}{G_k(x)^{-1}} = G_k(x)\Big(1 - \ln G_k(x) + B(x)\Big) \pmod{x^{2^{k+1}}}.\]

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4\)): Computing \(\exp\left(x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3\right) \bmod x^4\)

Let \(B(x) = x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3\). We expect \(\exp(B(x)) = \frac{1}{1-x} \equiv 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 \pmod{x^4}\).

  1. Base case (\(2^0 = 1\)): Since \([x^0]B(x) = 0\), start with \(G_0(x) = \exp(0) = 1 \pmod x\).
  2. Step \(k = 0 \to 1\) (\(\bmod x^2\)): Compute \(\ln G_0(x) \bmod x^2\): since \(G_0(x) = 1\), \(\ln(1) = 0 \pmod{x^2}\). Then \(G_1(x) \equiv 1 \cdot \big(1 - 0 + (x)\big) = 1 + x \pmod{x^2}.\)
  3. Step \(k = 1 \to 2\) (\(\bmod x^4\)): First compute \(\ln G_1(x) = \ln(1 + x) \bmod x^4\): \[\ln(1 + x) = \int_0^x \frac{1}{1+t}\,dt = \int_0^x (1 - t + t^2)\,dt = x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\] Now form \(1 - \ln G_1(x) + B(x) \bmod x^4\): \[1 - \left(x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3\right) + \left(x + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3\right) = 1 + x^2 \pmod{x^4}.\] Multiply by \(G_1(x) = 1 + x\) modulo \(x^4\): \[G_2(x) \equiv (1 + x)(1 + x^2) = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 \pmod{x^4}.\] In just two iterations, all four coefficients \(1, 1, 1, 1\) are recovered!

  • Preconditions: \([x^0] B(x) = 0\) (i.e., \(\operatorname{val}(B) \ge 1\)) and \(1, 2, \dots, N-1 \in K^\times\) (in \(\mathbb{F}_p\), requires \(p \ge N\)). If \([x^0] B(x) \ne 0\), the infinite sum \(\sum_{k \ge 0} b_0^k / k!\) does not converge in the formal topology!

Primitive 4: General Formal Power (pow) & Two Canonical Failure Modes

Given an arbitrary polynomial \(P(x) \in \mathbb{F}_p[[x]] / (x^N)\) and an exponent \(\alpha\) (which might be a huge non-negative integer \(K \le 10^{18}\), a negative integer, or a rational number \(a/b \in \mathbb{Q}\)), how do we compute \(P(x)^\alpha \bmod x^N\) when \(p > N\)?

TipThe Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: General Formal Power pow(P, M, N)
  • The Diagnostic Signal: You need \([x^n] P(x)^M\) for \(n < N\) where the exponent \(M\) is large (\(M \le 10^{18}\) or given as a string of \(10^6\) digits) and \(P(x)\) is a dense polynomial that may start with leading zeros (\([x^0]P(x) = 0\)) or a non-unit constant term (\([x^0]P(x) = c \neq 1\)).
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad:
    1. If \([x^0]P(x) = 1\), we can immediately write \(P(x)^\alpha = \exp(\alpha \ln P(x)) \bmod x^N\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time, eliminating the \(\log M\) factor of binary exponentiation!
    2. When \([x^0]P(x) \neq 1\), we cannot pass \(P(x)\) directly to ln. Instead, factor \(P(x)\) into three decoupled components:
      • Valuation \(v = \operatorname{val}(P)\): The index of the first nonzero coefficient \(c_v = [x^v] P(x) \ne 0\).
      • Leading scalar \(c_v \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\): Factor out \(c_v\) so the remaining series starts with \(1\).
      • Normalized monic series \(\widetilde{P}(x)\): Define \(\widetilde{P}(x) := c_v^{-1} x^{-v} P(x)\), which satisfies \([x^0]\widetilde{P}(x) = 1\).
    3. Raise each of the three factors \(P(x) = x^v \cdot c_v \cdot \widetilde{P}(x)\) to the power \(\alpha\) separately: \[P(x)^\alpha = x^{v \alpha} \cdot c_v^\alpha \cdot \exp\big(\alpha \ln \widetilde{P}(x)\big) \pmod{x^N}.\]

Here lies one of the most notorious bug sources in competitive programming (Library Checker — Pow of Formal Power Series, where \(M \le 10^{18}\) while \(p = 998244353\)). Notice that the exponent \(\alpha\) appears in three different places on the right-hand side—and each of the three places lives in a completely different algebraic structure!

WarningCanonical Failure Mode #2: The 3-Way Modular Reduction Trap in \(P(x)^\alpha \bmod x^N\) for \(\alpha \ge p\)

What Goes Wrong: A coder reads the exponent \(M\) (say \(M = 10^{18}\)) and immediately reduces M %= 998244353 at the top of poly_pow(P, M). This single line causes three distinct Wrong Answers:

  1. Bug A (\(0^0\) vs. \(0^p\)): If \(P(x) = 0\) and \(M = p = 998244353\), reducing \(M \bmod p\) turns \(M\) into \(0\), returning \(P(x)^0 = 1\) instead of \(0^p = 0\)!
  2. Bug B (Valuation Shift Wrapping): If \(P(x) = x\) (\(v = 1\)) and \(M = p = 998244353\) with \(N = 10\), reducing \(M \bmod p = 0\) shifts by \(x^{1 \cdot 0} = 1\) instead of \(x^p \equiv 0 \pmod{x^{10}}\)!
  3. Bug C (Fermat Exponent Mismatch): In the scalar factor \(c_v^M \bmod p\), the multiplicative group \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\) has order \(p - 1\), not \(p\)! Using \(c_v^{M \bmod p}\) instead of \(c_v^{M \bmod (p-1)}\) multiplies the entire output polynomial by the wrong constant whenever \(M \ge p\) and \(c_v \ne 1\).

The Exact 3-Way Reduction Rule: When computing \(P(x)^M \bmod x^N\) over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) (\(p > N\)) for a non-negative integer \(M \ge 0\) (even if \(M\) is given as a \(10^6\)-digit string!):

  1. Zero Exponent Guard (\(M = 0\) in \(\mathbb{Z}\)): Before anything else, if the unreduced integer \(M == 0\), immediately return the constant polynomial \(1 \bmod x^N\) (since \(P(x)^0 = 1\) even when \(P(x) = 0\)).
  2. Zero Polynomial or Valuation Overflow Guard (True Integer \(\mathbb{Z}\) / __int128): Let \(v = \operatorname{val}(P)\). If \(P(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{x^N}\), or if \(v > 0\) and the true integer product \(v \cdot M \ge N\) (checked via M >= (N + v - 1) / v to prevent 64-bit overflow!), immediately return the zero polynomial \(0 \bmod x^N\).
  3. Three Distinct Reductions of \(M\):
    • Degree Shift (\(x^{v M}\)): Uses the exact unreduced integer \(v \cdot M \in [0, N-1]\).
    • Leading Scalar (\(c_v^{M_{\text{exp}}} \bmod p\)): Uses \(M_{\text{exp}} = M \bmod (p - 1)\) by Fermat’s Little Theorem (\(c_v^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p\) for \(c_v \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\)).
    • Series Multiplier (\(M_{\text{mod}} \cdot \ln \widetilde{P}(x)\)): Uses \(M_{\text{mod}} = M \bmod p\) because the coefficients of \(\ln \widetilde{P}(x)\) live in the additive group of \(\mathbb{F}_p\).
NoteAlgorithm Contract: pow(P, M, N) — Dense Formal Power with 3-Way Modular Reduction
  • Input: Polynomial \(P(x) \bmod x^N\) over \(\mathbb{F}_p\); non-negative integer exponent \(M \ge 0\) (supplied either as a 64-bit integer or as a multi-precision decimal string holding \((M_{\text{raw}}, M \bmod p, M \bmod (p-1))\)).
  • Preconditions: \(p \ge N\) so that ln and exp are well-defined up to degree \(N-1\).
  • Output: \(F(x) = P(x)^M \bmod x^N\).
  • Complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space (1 call to ln and 1 call to exp of length \(N - vM\)).
  • Correctness Invariant: If \(M = 0\), returns \(1\); if \(P \equiv 0\) or \(vM \ge N\), returns \(0\); otherwise \(F(x) = x^{vM} \cdot c_v^{M \bmod (p-1)} \cdot \exp\big((M \bmod p) \ln \widetilde{P}(x)\big) \bmod x^N\) where \(\widetilde{P}(x) = c_v^{-1} x^{-v} P(x)\) has \([x^0]\widetilde{P} = 1\).

What if the exponent \(\alpha\) is not an integer, but a rational number \(\alpha = a / b \in \mathbb{Q}\) with \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\) and \(b > 0\) (such as \(\alpha = 1/2\) for sqrt, \(\alpha = -1/2\) for inverse square root, or \(\alpha = 1/k\) for \(k\)-th roots)?

WarningCanonical Failure Mode #3: Rational Exponent \(P(x)^{a/b}\) Existence Failure

What Goes Wrong: A coder computes \(\alpha = a \cdot b^{-1} \bmod p \in \mathbb{F}_p\) and passes this modular integer into poly_pow(P, alpha), assuming that \(P(x)^{a/b} = \exp\big((a b^{-1} \bmod p) \ln P(x)\big)\) works whenever \(b \not\equiv 0 \pmod p\).

Why It Fails: While \(\exp\big((a b^{-1} \bmod p) \ln \widetilde{P}(x)\big)\) is always well-defined for a normalized series \(\widetilde{P}(x)\) with \([x^0]\widetilde{P}(x) = 1\) (provided \(p \nmid b\) and \(p > N\)), the two outer factors \(x^{v \cdot (a/b)}\) and \(c_v^{a/b}\) are not automatically defined in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\):

  1. Valuation Divisibility Obstruction: In \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), every monomial \(x^k\) must have an integer exponent \(k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\). You cannot divide the valuation \(v\) by \(b\) modulo \(p\)! For \(x^{v a / b}\) to be a legitimate monomial in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\), \(b\) must divide \(v \cdot a\) as true integers in \(\mathbb{Z}\) (since \(\gcd(a,b)=1\), this is equivalent to \(b \mid v\) in \(\mathbb{Z}\)). For example, \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2} \notin \mathbb{F}_p[[x]]\) even though \(1/2 \equiv (p+1)/2 \pmod p\) exists in \(\mathbb{F}_p\)!
  2. Scalar \(b\)-th Residue Obstruction: In \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\), raising \(c_v\) to \(a/b\) means finding an element \(r \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\) such that \(r^b \equiv c_v^a \pmod p\). This is not \(c_v^{a b^{-1} \bmod (p-1)}\) when \(\gcd(b, p-1) > 1\)! For instance, for \(a/b = 1/2\), \(2\) is not even invertible modulo \(p - 1\) (since \(p - 1\) is even); a square root of \(c_v\) exists in \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\) if and only if \(c_v^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod p\).

Exact Necessary and Sufficient Existence Criterion for \(P(x)^{a/b} \in \mathbb{F}_p[[x]] / (x^N)\) (\(\gcd(a,b)=1, b>0, p > N\)): Assuming \(P(x) = x^v c_v \widetilde{P}(x) \not\equiv 0 \pmod{x^N}\):

  1. \(b \mid v\) in \(\mathbb{Z}\) (with \(a \ge 0\), or \(v = 0\) if \(a < 0\) so negative powers of \(x\) do not appear);
  2. \(c_v^a\) is a \(b\)-th power residue in \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\) (i.e., there exists \(r \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\) with \(r^b \equiv c_v^a \pmod p\), which holds iff \((c_v^a)^{(p-1)/\gcd(b, p-1)} \equiv 1 \pmod p\));
  3. For the normalized unit series \(\widetilde{P}(x)\) (\([x^0]\widetilde{P} = 1\)), \(\widetilde{P}(x)^{a/b} = \exp\big((a \cdot b^{-1} \bmod p) \ln \widetilde{P}(x)\big) \bmod x^{N - v a / b}\) is uniquely defined with constant term \(1\).

2.4 The Master \(O(N \cdot K)\) Sparse FPS Engine (sparse_div, sparse_ln, sparse_exp, sparse_pow, sparse_sqrt)

Suppose a problem asks for the first \(N = 2 \times 10^5\) coefficients of \((1 + x + x^3)^{10^9} \bmod x^N\), or \(\exp(x + x^5 - 2x^{12}) \bmod x^N\), or \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2x - 3x^2}} \bmod x^N\). Here, the input polynomial \(P(x)\) is \(K\)-sparse: it has at most \(K\) nonzero terms (with \(K \le 20\) or \(K \le 100\)).

Could we use the general \(O(N \log N)\) NTT engines (ln, exp, sqrt, pow) from Section 2.3? Yes, in \(O(N \log N)\) time with 6 to 15 NTT calls. Can we do dramatically better—computing the exact same answer in \(O(N \cdot K)\) time using a single 10-line for loop and zero NTTs?

The Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

TipThe Diagnostic Signal & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Why Differentiating Preserves Sparsity

The Diagnostic Signal: You need to apply a non-linear FPS operation (inv, ln, exp, pow, sqrt) up to \(x^N\) where the input polynomial \(P(x)\) (or its numerator and denominator after multiplying by \(1-x\)) has only \(K \ll N\) nonzero terms, and \(p > N\).

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Why did our \(O(N \log N)\) algorithm for \(P(x)^\alpha = \exp(\alpha \ln P(x))\) require NTTs even when \(P(x) = 1 + x + x^3\) has only \(K = 3\) terms? Because as soon as we take the intermediate logarithm \(L(x) = \ln(1 + x + x^3)\), the series \(L(x)\) has \(\Theta(N)\) nonzero terms! Taking \(\ln\) destroys the sparsity of \(P(x)\).

How can we bypass the dense intermediate series \(\ln P(x)\) and relate \(F(x) = P(x)^\alpha\) directly to the sparse polynomial \(P(x)\)? Differentiate first, then clear the denominator!

  1. Take the logarithmic derivative of \(F(x) = P(x)^\alpha\): \[\frac{F'(x)}{F(x)} = \alpha \frac{P'(x)}{P(x)}.\]
  2. Cross-multiply by \(P(x)\) and \(F(x)\) to eliminate all divisions of series: \[P(x) F'(x) = \alpha P'(x) F(x).\] Look closely at this equation:
  • \(P(x)\) is \(K\)-sparse (at most \(K\) nonzero coefficients).
  • Therefore, its derivative \(P'(x)\) is also \(K\)-sparse (in fact, at most \(K-1\) nonzero coefficients)!
  • Consequently, when we extract the coefficient of \(x^{n-1}\) on both sides of \(P(x) F'(x) = \alpha P'(x) F(x)\), both convolutions sum over at most \(K\) nonzero terms! Solving for \(n f_n\) gives a direct \(O(K)\)-time recurrence for \(f_n\) from \(f_0, \dots, f_{n-1}\).

Let us derive the exact coefficient-by-coefficient recurrences for all five sparse primitives. Throughout this section, we represent a \(K\)-sparse series \(P(x)\) as a list of nonzero pairs \(\{(d, p_d) : d \ge 0, p_d \ne 0\}\) of size at most \(K\). Notice a critical distinction in ring preconditions: sparse_div requires NO division by the loop index \(n\) (only \(b_0 \in K^\times\), so it works over any commutative ring \(K\) and in all characteristics \(p \le N\)), whereas the differential-equation primitives (sparse_ln, sparse_exp, sparse_pow, sparse_sqrt) divide by \(n\) and therefore require \(1, 2, \dots, N-1 \in K^\times\) (\(p \ge N\)).

1. Sparse Division (sparse_div): \(F(x) = \frac{A(x)}{B(x)} \bmod x^N\) (\(B\) is \(K\)-sparse, \(b_0 \in K^\times\), valid for ALL \(p\))

  1. Cross-multiply to clear the denominator: \[B(x) F(x) = A(x).\]
  2. Extract \([x^n]\) for \(n = 0, 1, \dots, N-1\): \[\sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(B) \\ 0 \le d \le n}} b_d f_{n-d} = a_n.\]
  3. Isolate the \(d = 0\) term (\(b_0 f_n\)) and solve for \(f_n\) (requires only \(b_0^{-1} \in K^\times\), never divides by \(n\)!): \[f_n = b_0^{-1} \left(a_n - \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(B) \\ 1 \le d \le n}} b_d f_{n-d}\right).\]

2. Sparse Logarithm (sparse_ln): \(F(x) = \ln P(x) \bmod x^N\) (\(P\) is \(K\)-sparse, \(p_0 = 1\))

  1. Differentiate and clear the denominator: \[F'(x) = \frac{P'(x)}{P(x)} \implies P(x) F'(x) = P'(x).\]
  2. Multiply by \(x\) to align indices via the pointing operator \(\vartheta = x D_x\): \[P(x) \big(x F'(x)\big) = x P'(x).\]
  3. Extract \([x^n]\) for \(n = 1, 2, \dots, N-1\), isolate \(d = 0\), drop \(d = n\), and divide by \(n\) (remembering \(f_0 = \ln(1) = 0\) and \(p_0 = 1\)): \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 0 \le d \le n}} p_d \cdot (n - d) f_{n-d} &= n p_n && \text{(extract } [x^n] \text{ of the Cauchy product } P(x)\big(x F'(x)\big) = x P'(x)\text{)} \\[4pt] p_0\, n\, f_n + \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n-1}} (n - d)\, p_d\, f_{n-d} + 0 &= n p_n && \text{(isolate } d = 0 \text{ and drop } d = n \text{ since } n - n = 0\text{)} \\[4pt] f_n &= p_n - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n-1}} (n - d)\, p_d\, f_{n-d}. && \text{(use } p_0 = 1 \text{ and divide by } n \in K^\times\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

3. Sparse Exponential (sparse_exp): \(F(x) = \exp P(x) \bmod x^N\) (\(P\) is \(K\)-sparse, \(p_0 = 0\))

  1. Differentiate using the Chain Rule: \[F'(x) = P'(x) F(x) \implies x F'(x) = \big(x P'(x)\big) F(x).\]
  2. Extract \([x^n]\) for \(n = 1, 2, \dots, N-1\) (starting from \(f_0 = \exp(0) = 1\)): \[n f_n = \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n}} d\, p_d\, f_{n-d} \implies f_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n}} d\, p_d\, f_{n-d}.\]

4. Sparse Power & Square Root (sparse_pow & sparse_sqrt): \(F(x) = P(x)^\alpha \bmod x^N\) (\(P\) is \(K\)-sparse, \(p_0 \in K^\times\))

  1. Differentiate \(F(x) = P(x)^\alpha\) and clear the denominator: \[\frac{F'(x)}{F(x)} = \alpha \frac{P'(x)}{P(x)} \implies P(x) \big(x F'(x)\big) = \alpha \big(x P'(x)\big) F(x).\]
  2. Extract \([x^n]\) for \(n = 1, 2, \dots, N-1\): On the left-hand side, \([x^n]\big(P(x) \cdot x F'(x)\big) = \sum_{d=0}^n p_d (n-d) f_{n-d}\). On the right-hand side, \([x^n]\big(\alpha x P'(x) \cdot F(x)\big) = \alpha \sum_{d=1}^n d p_d f_{n-d}\).
  3. Separate the \(d = 0\) term on the left (\(p_0 \cdot n f_n\)) from \(1 \le d \le n\) and collect \(f_{n-d}\): \[p_0\, n\, f_n + \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n}} (n - d)\, p_d\, f_{n-d} = \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n}} \alpha\, d\, p_d\, f_{n-d}.\] Moving the sum to the right-hand side gives the Master Sparse Power Recurrence: \[f_n = \frac{1}{n \cdot p_0} \sum_{\substack{d \in \operatorname{supp}(P) \\ 1 \le d \le n}} \big((\alpha + 1) d - n\big)\, p_d\, f_{n-d} \quad (n \ge 1, \text{ with } f_0 = p_0^\alpha).\]

Notice how setting \(\alpha = \frac{1}{2} \equiv \frac{p+1}{2} \pmod p\) immediately gives sparse_sqrt, and setting \(\alpha = -\frac{1}{2} \equiv \frac{p-1}{2} \pmod p\) immediately gives sparse inverse square root!

  • Preconditions:

Concrete Hand-Checkable Trace (\(N = 4\)): sparse_pow on \((1 + 2x + x^3)^\alpha \bmod x^4\)

Let \(P(x) = 1 + 2x + x^3\), so \(p_0 = 1\), and the nonzero sparse terms of degree \(d \ge 1\) are \((d=1, p_1=2)\) and \((d=3, p_3=1)\). Starting from \(f_0 = 1^\alpha = 1\):

  • \(n = 3\): Both \(d = 1\) and \(d = 3\) contribute: \[f_3 = \frac{1}{3 \cdot 1} \Big[\big((\alpha + 1)(1) - 3\big) \cdot 2 \cdot f_2 + \big((\alpha + 1)(3) - 3\big) \cdot 1 \cdot f_0\Big] = \frac{4\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}{3} + \alpha.\] Let us hand-check at \(\alpha = 2\): \((1 + 2x + x^3)^2 = 1 + 4x + 4x^2 + 2x^3 + 4x^4 + x^6\). Our trace formulas give \(f_1 = 4\), \(f_2 = 2(2)(1) = 4\), \(f_3 = 0 + 2 = 2\). Exact match!

Hidden Sparsity: Rational Polynomials and the \((1 - x)\) Trick

What if a polynomial \(A(x)\) looks dense, such as \(A(x) = 1 + x + x^2 + \dots + x^{d-1}\), and we need \(A(x)^K \bmod x^N\)? Applying sparse_pow directly to \(A(x)\) costs \(O(N d)\) time. However, multiplying and dividing by \((1 - x)\) turns \(A(x)\) into a ratio of two 2-sparse polynomials: \[A(x)^K = \left(\frac{1 - x^d}{1 - x}\right)^K = (1 - x^d)^K \cdot (1 - x)^{-K}.\] Here \((1 - x^d)^K\) has nonzero terms only at multiples of \(d\) (\(\binom{K}{j}(-1)^j x^{jd}\)), and \((1 - x)^{-K} = \sum_{m \ge 0} \binom{K + m - 1}{m} x^m\) is a 2-sparse negative power!

More generally, for any product of powers of sparse polynomials \(F(x) = \prod_{i=1}^m P_i(x)^{\alpha_i}\), taking the logarithmic derivative gives \[\frac{F'(x)}{F(x)} = \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i \frac{P_i'(x)}{P_i(x)}.\] Clearing the common denominator \(Q(x) = \prod_{i=1}^m P_i(x)\) yields a single sparse linear ODE \(Q(x) F'(x) = R(x) F(x)\) whose sparsity is bounded by the product of the sparsities of \(P_i(x)\). (We will see this technique shine again in Chapter 05 on AtCoder ABC 300 Ex — Fibonacci: Revisited and in Chapter 18 on D-Finite / Holonomic sequences.)

2.5 Logarithmic Derivative (\(F'/F\)), Newton–Girard Power Sums & Radical Rationalization

The logarithmic derivative operator \(\mathcal{L}(F) := \frac{F'(x)}{F(x)} = D_x \ln F(x)\) is one of the most versatile algebraic bridges in generating functions because it converts products into sums: \[\mathcal{L}\left(\prod_{i=1}^m F_i(x)^{\alpha_i}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i \frac{F_i'(x)}{F_i(x)}.\] Let us examine three high-impact applications of \(\mathcal{L}(F)\) and radical equations.

1. Harmonic Subset-Sum / Knapsack via Bucketed \(\ln / \exp\)

Suppose we have \(m\) items with positive integer weights \(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_m \in \{1, 2, \dots, N\}\), and we want to compute the subset-sum generating function \[P(x) = \prod_{i=1}^m (1 + x^{w_i})^{\alpha_i} \bmod x^{N+1}\] (for 0-1 knapsack, \(\alpha_i = 1\); for unbounded knapsack, \(\alpha_i = -1\) on \((1 - x^{w_i})\)).

  • The Diagnostic Signal: We have up to \(m \le 10^5\) factors of the binomial form \((1 \pm c x^{w_i})^{\alpha_i}\) modulo \(x^{N+1}\) (\(N \le 10^5\)), and many items may share the same weight \(w\).
  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Multiplying \(m\) polynomials one by one takes \(O(m N)\) time. Even a Divide-and-Conquer product tree takes \(O(N \log^2 N)\) time if we do not exploit the binomial structure \(1 \pm x^w\). Instead, take \(\ln P(x)\)! What is \(\ln(1 + x^w)\)? It is the standard alternating harmonic series evaluated at \(x^w\): \[\ln(1 + x^w) = \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k} x^{kw}, \qquad \ln\left(\frac{1}{1 - x^w}\right) = \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{k} x^{kw}.\] Notice that \(\ln(1 + x^w) \bmod x^{N+1}\) has only \(\lfloor N / w \rfloor\) nonzero terms! Even better: if we first bucket items by weight, letting \(\text{cnt}[w] = \sum_{w_i = w} \alpha_i\) be the total multiplicity of weight \(w\), then we only need to loop over each distinct weight \(w \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) once and add its multiples \(kw \le N\): \[\ln P(x) \equiv \sum_{w=1}^N \text{cnt}[w] \sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor N/w \rfloor} \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k} x^{kw} \pmod{x^{N+1}}.\] The total number of loop iterations to build \(\ln P(x)\) is the harmonic sum \(\sum_{w=1}^N \lfloor N/w \rfloor = O(N \log N)\)! A single call to exp at the end recovers \(P(x) \bmod x^{N+1}\) in \(O(N \log N)\) total time.

2. Newton–Girard Power Sums via Logarithmic Derivative

Given \(m\) numbers \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m \in K\), how can we simultaneously compute the \(k\)-th power sums \[S_k := \sum_{i=1}^m a_i^k \quad \text{for all } k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N\] in \(O(m \log^2 m + N \log N)\) time instead of the naive \(O(m N)\)?

  • The Diagnostic Signal: A problem asks for \(\sum_{i=1}^m a_i^k\) for all \(k \le N\), or asks for \(\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m (a_i + b_j)^k\) for all \(k \le N\) (which expands binomially into \(\sum_{r=0}^k \binom{k}{r} (\sum_i a_i^r)(\sum_j b_j^{k-r})\)).

  • The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad: Where does \(a_i^k\) naturally appear as the coefficient of \(x^k\)? In the geometric series \(\frac{1}{1 - a_i x} = \sum_{k \ge 0} a_i^k x^k\)! Summing over all \(i = 1, \dots, m\) gives the Ordinary Generating Function of the power sums \(S_k\): \[S(x) := \sum_{k \ge 0} S_k x^k = \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{1}{1 - a_i x} = m + \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{a_i x}{1 - a_i x}.\] How do we compute \(\sum_{i=1}^m \frac{a_i x}{1 - a_i x}\) without adding \(m\) fractions one by one? Recognise that \(\frac{-a_i}{1 - a_i x}\) is the logarithmic derivative of \((1 - a_i x)\)! Therefore: \[\sum_{i=1}^m \frac{a_i x}{1 - a_i x} = -x \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{-a_i}{1 - a_i x} = -x \frac{d}{dx} \ln \left(\prod_{i=1}^m (1 - a_i x)\right) = -\frac{x Q'(x)}{Q(x)},\] where \(Q(x) := \prod_{i=1}^m (1 - a_i x)\). This gives the complete two-stage algorithm:

    1. Compute \(Q(x) = \prod_{i=1}^m (1 - a_i x)\) in \(O(m \log^2 m)\) time using a Divide-and-Conquer Product Tree (recursively multiplying left and right halves via NTT).
    2. Truncate \(Q(x)\) modulo \(x^{N+1}\) and compute \(S(x) = m - x Q'(x) Q(x)^{-1} \bmod x^{N+1}\) in \(O(N \log N)\) time using one polynomial inversion (or -x * deriv(ln(Q))).

3. Quadratic Equations & Radical Rationalization (Avoiding Valuation Precision Loss)

In combinatorial enumeration (such as weighted binary trees in Codeforces 438E, Catalan paths, or Motzkin paths), the generating function \(F(x)\) often satisfies a quadratic equation with polynomial coefficients: \[A(x) F(x)^2 + B(x) F(x) + C(x) = 0,\] where typically \(\operatorname{val}(A) \ge 1\) (so \(A(0) = 0\), e.g., \(A(x) = x D(x)\)), \([x^0] B(x) = -1\), and \([x^0] C(x) = 1\).

Suppose we solve for \(F(x)\) using the standard high-school quadratic formula: \[F(x) = \frac{-B(x) \pm \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}}{2 A(x)}.\] First, which sign (\(\pm\)) is correct? Look at \(x = 0\): since \(A(0) = 0\) and \(B(0) = -1\), choosing the \(+\) sign gives numerator \(-(-1) + \sqrt{1 - 0} = 2 \ne 0\) divided by denominator \(2 A(0) = 0\), which blows up (\(2/0 \notin K[[x]]\))! Choosing the \(-\) sign makes the numerator vanish at \(x = 0\): \(-(-1) - \sqrt{1} = 0\), cancelling the zero in \(2 A(x)\).

However, computing \(F(x) = \frac{-B(x) - \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}}{2 A(x)} \bmod x^N\) directly in code has a severe precision and invertibility hazard:

  • Because \(\operatorname{val}(A) = v \ge 1\), \(2 A(x)\) is not a unit in \(K[[x]]\)! To divide by \(2 A(x) = 2 x^v \widetilde{A}(x)\), you must shift the numerator down by \(x^v\)—which means you must compute \(\sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}\) to precision \(x^{N + v}\) instead of \(x^N\)! Worse still, if \(A(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{x^N}\) (for instance, in an edge case where all node weights exceed \(N\)), dividing by \(2 A(x)\) divides by zero!
TipThe Radical Rationalization Trick (Conjugate Multiplication)

Whenever \(A(0) = 0\), \(B(0) \in K^\times\), and \(C(0) \in K^\times\), rationalize the numerator by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate \(-B(x) + \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}\): \[\begin{aligned} F(x) &= \frac{\big(-B(x) - \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}\big)\big(-B(x) + \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}\big)}{2 A(x) \big(-B(x) + \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}\big)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{B(x)^2 - \big(B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)\big)}{2 A(x) \big(-B(x) + \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}\big)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{2 C(x)}{-B(x) + \sqrt{B(x)^2 - 4 A(x) C(x)}} \pmod{x^N}. \end{aligned}\] Why is this rationalized form strictly superior?

  1. The non-unit factor \(2 A(x)\) cancels algebraically before any numerical division takes place!
  2. At \(x = 0\) (where \(B(0) = -1, A(0) = 0\)), the new denominator has constant term \(-(-1) + \sqrt{1} = 2 \in K^\times\). It is always an invertible unit modulo \(x^N\), requiring zero extra precision beyond \(x^N\) and working seamlessly even when \(A(x) = 0\)!

Hint 3 — Coefficient Extraction & Complexity Contract

Summing over all \(w\) and \(k\) takes \(\sum_{w=1}^M \lfloor M / w \rfloor = O(M \log M)\) operations. Finally, apply a single \(O(M \log M)\) Newton exp to recover \(F(x) \bmod x^{M+1}\).

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. Combinatorial Specification to Generating Function:
    • In the Unbounded Knapsack (Luogu P4389) model, choosing \(k_i \ge 0\) copies of item \(i\) contributes weight \(k_i w_i\), represented by the factor \(\sum_{k_i \ge 0} x^{k_i w_i} = (1 - x^{w_i})^{-1}\).     • In the 0-1 Subset Sum model, each item \(i\) is picked \(0\) or \(1\) time, giving \[F_{0\text{-}1}(x) = \prod_{i=1}^N (1 + x^{w_i}) = \prod_{w=1}^M (1 + x^w)^{c_w}.\]
  2. Logarithmic Transformation: Because \([x^0] F(x) = 1\) and \(M = 10^5 < p = 998244353\), we can write \(F(x) = \exp\big(L(x)\big) \bmod x^{M+1}\), where \(L(x) = \ln F(x) \bmod x^{M+1}\). Using the formal logarithm power series \(\ln(1 - u)^{-1} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{u^k}{k}\) and \(\ln(1 + u) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k-1} u^k}{k}\) at \(u = x^w\): \[L_{\text{unbounded}}(x) \equiv \sum_{w=1}^M c_w \sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor M/w \rfloor} \frac{1}{k} x^{kw} \pmod{x^{M+1}},\] \[L_{0\text{-}1}(x) \equiv \sum_{w=1}^M c_w \sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor M/w \rfloor} \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k} x^{kw} \pmod{x^{M+1}}.\]

Boundary & Characteristic Audit

  • Why frequency bucketing is mandatory: If all \(N = 10^5\) items had \(w_i = 1\) and we added \(\ln(1 \pm x^{w_i})\) for each item \(i\) without bucketing, we would execute \(N \cdot M = 10^{10}\) operations (TLE)! Bucketing collapses identical weights into a single scalar multiplier \(c_w\), bounding the total work by the harmonic series \(\sum_{w=1}^M \frac{M}{w} \approx M \ln M\).
  • Characteristic check: \(p = 998244353 > M = 10^5\), so \(k^{-1} \bmod p\) exists for all \(1 \le k \le M\) and exp(L) is valid modulo \(x^{M+1}\).

Final Extraction Algorithm & Complexity

  2. Precompute modular inverses inv[1..M] in \(O(M)\) time using inv[i] = (p - p / i) * inv[p % i] % p.
  3. Initialize L[0..M] = 0. For \(w = 1 \dots M\) (if \(c_w > 0\)), loop \(k = 1 \dots \lfloor M / w \rfloor\) and add \(c_w \cdot \text{inv}[k] \pmod p\) (or \((-1)^{k-1} c_w \cdot \text{inv}[k] \pmod p\)) to L[k * w].
  4. Compute \(F = \text{exp}(L, M + 1)\) via 1D Newton iteration in \(O(M \log M)\) time, and print \(F[1], \dots, F[M]\).

  2. The sum of the chosen elements \(\sum_{i \in S} A_i\) equals \(M\).

  • Constraints: \(1 \le N \le 10^5\), \(1 \le M \le 10^6\), \(1 \le A_i \le 10\).

Hint 2 — Algebraic Setup & Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

By telescoping across all \(k\) steps, the final value of res on any sample path is simply \[\text{res} = \prod_{i=1}^n a_i - \prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - c_i),\] where \(c_i \ge 0\) is the number of times index \(i\) was chosen across the \(k\) steps (\(\sum_{i=1}^n c_i = k\)). [x^k] \big(P(x) e^{nx}\big)\)!

Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation

  1. The Telescoping Invariant (Eliminating Step-by-Step Dependencies): Let \(a_i^{(t)}\) denote the value of the \(i\)-th element after \(t\) operations (\(0 \le t \le k\)). If the \(t\)-th operation selects index \(i_t\), then \(a_{i_t}^{(t)} = a_{i_t}^{(t-1)} - 1\) while \(a_j^{(t)} = a_j^{(t-1)}\) for all \(j \ne i_t\). Therefore, the amount added to res in step \(t\) is \[\Delta \text{res}_t = \prod_{j \ne i_t} a_j^{(t-1)} = a_{i_t}^{(t-1)} \prod_{j \ne i_t} a_j^{(t-1)} - \big(a_{i_t}^{(t-1)} - 1\big) \prod_{j \ne i_t} a_j^{(t-1)} = \prod_{j=1}^n a_j^{(t-1)} - \prod_{j=1}^n a_j^{(t)}.\] Summing \(\Delta \text{res}_t\) from \(t = 1\) to \(k\) telescopes all intermediate products, leaving only the initial product minus the final product: \[\text{res} = \sum_{t=1}^k \Delta \text{res}_t = \prod_{i=1}^n a_i^{(0)} - \prod_{i=1}^n a_i^{(k)} = \prod_{i=1}^n a_i - \prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - c_i),\] where \(c_i\) is the total number of times index \(i\) was chosen during the \(k\) steps.

  2. Multinomial Expectation via Exponential Generating Functions: Since each of the \(k\) operations chooses an index in \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) independently and uniformly at random, there are \(n^k\) equally likely outcome sequences, and a specific count vector \((c_1, \dots, c_n)\) with \(c_1 + \dots + c_n = k\) is produced by \(\frac{k!}{c_1! c_2! \cdots c_n!}\) sequences. Thus: \[\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - c_i)\right] = \frac{k!}{n^k} \sum_{\substack{c_1 + \dots + c_n = k \\ c_i \ge 0}} \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{a_i - c_i}{c_i!}.\] Notice that the inner sum is the exact coefficient of \(x^k\) in the Cauchy product of \(n\) Exponential Generating Functions \(G_i(x) := \sum_{c \ge 0} \frac{a_i - c}{c!} x^c\) (we will formalize Exponential Generating Functions and multinomial labeled products in Chapter 04, §4.1). Using the pointing identity \(c \frac{x^c}{c!} = x \frac{x^{c-1}}{(c-1)!}\) (or \(x D_x e^x = x e^x\)), each factor simplifies into a linear polynomial times \(e^x\): \[G_i(x) = a_i \sum_{c \ge 0} \frac{x^c}{c!} - x \sum_{c \ge 1} \frac{x^{c-1}}{(c-1)!} = (a_i - x) e^x.\] Multiplying \(G_1(x) \cdots G_n(x)\) gives \[G(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - x) e^x = e^{n x} \prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - x).\]

  3. Extracting \(k! [x^k] G(x)\) for Huge \(k \le 10^9\): Let \(P(x) := \prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - x) = \sum_{j=0}^n p_j x^j\), which is a polynomial of degree \(n \le 5000\). Since \(e^{nx} = \sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{n^m}{m!} x^m\), extracting \([x^k]\big(P(x) e^{nx}\big)\) only sums over \(0 \le j \le \min(n, k)\): \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - c_i)\right] &= \frac{k!}{n^k} [x^k]\left(\sum_{j=0}^n p_j x^j \sum_{m \ge 0} \frac{n^m}{m!} x^m\right) \\[4pt] &= \frac{k!}{n^k} \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} p_j \frac{n^{k - j}}{(k - j)!} = \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} p_j \cdot \frac{k^{\underline{j}}}{n^j}, \end{aligned}\] where \(k^{\underline{j}} = \frac{k!}{(k-j)!} = k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)\) is the \(j\)-th falling factorial of \(k\)!

Boundary & Characteristic Audit

  • Why no division by \(k!\) is needed: Although \(k \le 10^9\) can be close to \(p = 10^9 + 7\), the ratio \(\frac{k!}{(k-j)!} = k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)\) cancels \((k-j)!\) algebraically into a pure product of \(j \le n \le 5000\) integers! Thus we never divide by any factorial, and the formula works over any prime modulus \(p \nmid n\).
  • Notice also that \(p_0 = \prod_{i=1}^n a_i\), so the \(j = 0\) term in the sum is \(p_0 \cdot \frac{k^{\underline{0}}}{n^0} = \prod_{i=1}^n a_i\), which cancels \(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i\) in \(\text{Ans} = \prod_{i=1}^n a_i - \mathbb{E}[\prod(a_i - c_i)]\), leaving \[\text{Ans} = -\sum_{j=1}^{\min(n, k)} p_j \cdot k^{\underline{j}} \cdot n^{-j} \pmod{10^9 + 7}.\]

Final Extraction Algorithm & Complexity

  1. Initialize \(P^{(0)}(x) = p_0 = 1\) and \(p_1 = \dots = p_n = 0\). For \(i = 1, 2, \dots, n\), update the coefficients of \(P(x) = \prod_{r=1}^n (a_r - x)\) in-place in descending degree order \(j = i, i - 1, \dots, 0\) via \(p_j \gets a_i p_j - p_{j-1}\) (with \(p_{-1} = 0\)) in \(O(n^2)\) total time. Loop Invariant: After outer step \(i\), \(\sum_{j=0}^i p_j x^j = \prod_{r=1}^i (a_r - x)\), and iterating \(j\) from \(i\) down to \(0\) guarantees \(p_{j-1}\) still holds its step-\((i-1)\) value when computing the new \(p_j\).
  2. Compute \(n^{-1} \bmod (10^9 + 7)\) via Fermat’s Little Theorem, and accumulate \(k^{\underline{j}} \cdot n^{-j} \bmod (10^9 + 7)\) iteratively for \(j = 1, \dots, \min(n, k)\) in \(O(n)\) time.
  3. Output \(\left(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i - \sum_{j=0}^{\min(n, k)} p_j k^{\underline{j}} n^{-j}\right) \bmod (10^9 + 7)\).
