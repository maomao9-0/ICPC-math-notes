Advanced Mathematics for ICPC

A dependency-guided course for experienced competitive programmers

Turn mathematical structure into contest algorithms

Build the recognition, derivation, and modelling skills behind hard competitive-programming mathematics. The course assumes you already have a tested implementation library and focuses on deciding what to apply and why it works.

Start with the audit View the study plan

This is a dependency-guided course for an experienced competitive programmer who already solves around Codeforces 2200 level. It is deliberately biased toward techniques that turn a difficult mathematical observation into an implementable algorithm. Geometry is out of scope.

The notes assume that the reader already has a comprehensive, tested algorithm library. They focus on contracts, derivations, recognition clues, and failure modes rather than reproducing standard implementations. Code appears only when a non-standard snippet materially clarifies an idea. Shared symbols and preconditions are collected in notation reference.

How to use the course

  1. Take the prerequisite audit. Patch only the gaps it exposes.
  2. Read a chapter’s theory before opening its problem hints.
  3. Attempt each problem for 60–120 minutes. Open Hint 1 only when you cannot name the relevant mathematical object; open Hint 2 only when the reduction remains unclear; use Hint 3 when you know the mathematics but cannot obtain the target complexity.
  4. After solving, write a three-line postmortem: recognition clue, decisive identity, and the implementation hazard you would miss next time.
  5. Re-solve Mastery and Capstone problems after two and six weeks.

For a concrete multi-month cadence, use the optional 24-week study plan.

Problem levels used throughout the repository are local to this course:

  • Foundation isolates one prerequisite or modelling step.
  • Advanced combines a central theorem with a standard algorithm.
  • Mastery hides the model or requires a non-obvious transformation.
  • Capstone combines several chapters or is appropriate team-contest work.

Dependency map

The arrows are deliberately conservative. For example, probability can be studied before linear algebra if you postpone absorbing Markov chains, and Burnside can be studied before all of advanced combinatorics.

Recommended track

Phase Chapters Outcome
A. Algebraic base 01, 04 Move fluently between congruences, fields, identities, and counting models.
B. Core engines 02, 06, 10 Understand the contracts and structure of reusable algebraic machinery.
C. Inversion viewpoints 05, 09, 12 Recognize symmetry, poset inversion, and summation transformations.
D. Advanced coefficient work 07, 08 Derive and compute difficult coefficients, including Lagrange inversion.
E. Large-domain and stochastic work 03, 11 Handle summatory functions and expectation systems at contest scale.
F. Synthesis 13 Solve mixed, team-contest-strength mathematics.
G. Frontier algebra 14, 15, 16 Work beyond ordinary factorials, univariate FPS, and basic subset transforms.
H. Frontier synthesis 17, 18, 19 Apply randomized polynomial models and polynomial-coefficient recurrences.

Within a phase, follow the order in each chapter README. A chapter’s practice list is ordered; contest rating is only a secondary signal.

Source and spoiler policy

Statements are linked, not copied. The notes paraphrase the mathematical idea and give layered hints rather than complete solutions to listed exercises. Official statements and editorials are preferred; high-quality community tutorials are used where they explain a theorem better. A language label is shown when a useful source is not English. See the sources page for the source hierarchy and durable reference material.

No collection can guarantee that a future ICPC problem will not introduce new mathematics. The goal here is stronger and more realistic: build the common toolkit, the habit of deriving missing variants, and enough hard practice that a new statement can be reduced to familiar algebra under contest pressure.
