Advanced Mathematics for Competitive Programming

A textbook for expert programmers building mathematical foundations

You already know how to write a fast segment tree, how to squeeze a flow algorithm under the time limit, and how to debug a tricky DP at two in the morning. Then a problem says count the labelled forests modulo \(998244353\), or find \(x\) with \(x^2 \equiv a \pmod p\), and the difficulty is no longer engineering. The obstacle is a piece of mathematics that editorials name in one line and never explain.

This book is the missing explanation. It develops the algebra, number theory, counting, and probability behind hard ICPC, Codeforces, and AtCoder problems, starting from definitions and assuming no university mathematics — only that you are a confident programmer who can follow a careful argument.

Start with mathematical foundations, then follow the learning path. Keep the notation and algebra contracts open as a reference.

What a chapter looks like

Every chapter follows the same rhythm, because that is the order in which ideas actually become usable:

  1. A problem you cannot yet solve. Concrete, contest-shaped, and deliberately just out of reach.
  2. The object that solves it, introduced in plain words before any notation, with a small example you can check by hand.
  3. Why the central formula is true. Not a citation — a derivation you could reproduce on paper during a contest.
  4. The algorithm’s contract: what it assumes, what it returns, what it costs, and the inputs on which it silently returns garbage.
  5. Practice, from recognizing the model to combining several of them.

The failure modes are not an appendix. Knowing that Fermat inversion breaks the moment the modulus is composite, or that a formula needs \(n < p\) because it divides by a factorial, is exactly the knowledge that separates a correct submission from a wrong-answer verdict you cannot explain.

How to study

Read the derivations with a pen. When a chapter says why this is true, close the page and try to rebuild the argument; the gap you find is the part you did not actually understand. Work the small numerical examples by hand — they are chosen so that a misconception produces a visibly wrong number.

Attempt the original exercises before opening their solutions. Use the external contest problems to practice modelling under uncertainty. Difficulty labels are relative to this course; they are not predicted contest ratings. Some exercise pages deliberately point forward to applications that need a later chapter, and identify those extra prerequisites.

The book assumes you already have a tested competitive-programming library, so it contains almost no code. It teaches mathematical models, correctness invariants, input/output contracts, and resource bounds; the implementations stay in your library. No routine should be selected merely because its name resembles a phrase in the problem statement.

Curriculum at a glance

Read foundations first, then use these branches. Arrows show the main conceptual dependencies; the learning path gives chapter-level details.

Chapter numbers are the reading order: chapter \(n\) relies only on earlier chapters. Detours are still allowed — the learning path lists, for each chapter, the few earlier ones it genuinely uses.

Chapters

I · Arithmetic and counting

  • 00 · Mathematical foundations and proof: notation, logic, induction, invariants, extremal arguments, Bézout, double counting.
  • 01 · Modular algebra: units, fields, CRT, orders, valuations, logarithms, modular roots.
  • 02 · Multiplicative number theory: factorization, arithmetic functions, gcd filters, divisor inversion, Gaussian integers.
  • 03 · Combinatorics and practice: inclusion–exclusion, Stirling and Catalan models, partitions, binomial transforms, rook polynomials.
  • 04 · Binomial coefficients modulo anything: Lucas, Kummer carries, prime-power factorials, composite moduli by CRT.
  • 05 · Group actions and practice: orbits, stabilizers, Burnside, Pólya, induced graph symmetries.

II · Algebraic engines

  • 06 · Polynomials and practice: convolution, Fourier evaluation, division, product trees, interpolation, Taylor shift.
  • 07 · Linear algebra: vector spaces, rank, determinants, XOR bases, tree counts, recurrences and black-box methods.
  • 08 · Transforms: subset and divisor inversion, OR/AND/XOR, ranked disjoint convolution.
  • 09 · Formal power series and practice: precision, Newton lifting, inverse, logarithm, exponential, composition and reversion.
  • 10 · Generating functions and practice: labelled and unlabelled models, rational sequences, coefficient extraction, Lagrange inversion.
  • 11 · Set power series: disjoint multiplication, connected components, set exponential and logarithm.

III · Models and synthesis

  • 12 · Probability: sample spaces, indicators, conditioning, variance, stochastic DP and absorbing systems.
  • 13 · Discrete sums and Diophantine tools: interpolation, differences, floor sums, rational approximation, Pell equations.
  • 14 · Summatory sieves: quotient states, prefix recurrences, prime sums, multiplicative enumeration, powerful support.
  • 15 · Mixed capstones: modelling workshops with complete reduction guidance.

IV · Frontier topics

  • 16 · q-Analogues and subspace counting: Gaussian binomials, division-free recurrences, rank distributions.
  • 17 · Finite-field polynomials: quotient fields, Frobenius, factorization and irreducibility certification.
  • 18 · Algebraic graph methods: polynomial identity testing, Pfaffians, Tutte matrices, weighted matching.
  • 19 · Holonomic methods: differential equations, polynomial-coefficient recurrences, guessing and certification.
  • 20 · Frontier capstones: synthesis with explicit hypotheses, small models and validation plans.

What mastery means

For each chapter, record whether you can define its objects, reproduce its central proof, solve its local exercises, recognize its model in a contest statement, and explain a counterexample to its misuse. Revisit a difficult problem after two and six weeks; successful recall of the derivation is more informative than recognizing an editorial.

The course concentrates on algebra, number theory, counting, and probability. Geometry and continuous optimization are outside this curriculum. No finite toolkit covers every future contest problem; the transferable skill is deriving a new variant from well-understood objects and assumptions.
