16 — q-Analogues and Subspace Counting

Counting subspaces, not subsets

A problem gives you \(n\) binary vectors and asks how many sequences of them have XOR-span of a given dimension. Another asks how many \(\mathbb F_2\) matrices have rank exactly \(r\). Both look like they want a clever DP over bitmasks, and both are hopeless that way: the objects being counted are subspaces, and there are far too many subsets to sort into them.

Subspaces have their own counting theory, and it is startlingly close to the one you already know. Choosing an \(r\)-element subset of \(n\) has \(\binom nr\) answers; choosing an \(r\)-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb F_q^n\) has \(\binom nr_q\) answers — the Gaussian binomial coefficient — and the two satisfy almost the same recurrences and almost the same identities. Set \(q=1\) and the subspace formulas degenerate into the subset formulas.

That is what “q-analogue” means: a formula in a variable \(q\) that recovers a classical counting formula at \(q=1\) and counts a field-flavoured object at a prime power \(q\). The payoff is practical — once a contest quantity is recognized as a subspace or rank count, it collapses into a short product.

One warning governs the whole chapter, and it is the same one as in binomials modulo anything: the tidy product formula divides, the object being counted does not. When a denominator vanishes in your working modulus, the count still exists; only that route to it has failed.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Read units and valuations, binomial counting, and vector spaces, span, and rank, which supplies every geometric fact used below. The harder practice also uses convolution and Stirling transforms.

Learn to derive a subspace count by counting ordered bases, read a Gaussian binomial as an inversion or partition statistic, use the division-free recurrence when the product formula is singular, and turn a rank question into a product over independent row choices.

Throughout, \(q\) is a positive prime power (or a formal variable), \(0\le r\le n\), and the answer modulus — when there is one — is an unrelated number. Keep those two apart: \(q\) lives inside the objects, the modulus only compresses the final count.

1. Gaussian binomial coefficients

When \(q\) is a prime power, the Gaussian (or q-) binomial coefficient counts \(r\)-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_q^{n}\):

\[\binom nr_q=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{q^{n-i}-1}{q^{r-i}-1}.\]

Set the value to 1 for \(r=0\) and to zero for out-of-range \(r\). The product states an exact integer count; it is not a licence for arbitrary modular division.

To see the product, count ordered independent vectors in two ways. The first vector has \(q^n-1\) choices, the next must avoid the line already spanned and has \(q^n-q\) choices, and so on; divide by the number \((q^r-1)(q^r-q)\cdots(q^r-q^{r-1})\) of ordered bases of one fixed \(r\)-subspace, since each subspace is produced exactly that many times.

The same polynomial is the inversion generating polynomial for binary strings with \(r\) ones, and the area generating polynomial for partitions inside an \(r\times(n-r)\) rectangle. Thus \(\binom42_q=1+q+2q^2+q^3+q^4\) records how many such objects have each statistic value.

To connect binary strings, define an inversion as a pair of positions with a 1 before a 0. Splitting by the first bit: a leading 0 leaves a length-\(n-1\) string with \(r\) ones; a leading 1 leaves \(r-1\) ones and adds \(n-r\) inversions. This gives the recurrence below and the same boundary values, proving the polynomial identity by induction. Record, for each successive 1, how many zeros follow it; these counts form a nonincreasing list bounded by \(n-r\). Its Ferrers rows form a partition inside an \(r\times(n-r)\) rectangle, and their sum is the inversion count. For \(n=4,r=2\), the strings \(0011,0101,0110,1001,1010,1100\) have respectively \(0,1,2,2,3,4\) inversions. At \(q=2\), their weighted total is 35, the number of 2-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_2^4\); the six strings themselves are not the 35 subspaces.

2. The division-free recurrence

The recurrence

\[\binom nr_q=\binom{n-1}r_q+q^{n-r}\binom{n-1}{r-1}_q\]

remains valid even when the product formula appears to divide by zero. Fix a hyperplane \(H\cong\mathbb F_q^{n-1}\). An \(r\)-subspace is either contained in \(H\), giving the first term, or meets \(H\) in an \((r-1)\)-subspace; the latter case has \(q^{n-r}\) extensions, giving the second term. It is often the safest way to compute a whole table.

To justify the extension count, fix \(W=U\cap H\) of dimension \(r-1\). In the quotient vector space \(V/W\), an extension \(U/W\) is a line outside the hyperplane \(H/W\). There are \(q^{n-r+1}-q^{n-r}\) vectors outside that hyperplane, and each line contains \(q-1\) such nonzero vectors. Division gives \(q^{n-r}\) lines.

The recurrence with the boundary values computes a table through \(n,r\) in \(O(nr)\) ring operations with \(O(r)\) rolling memory, assuming powers of \(q\) are available; evaluate rows in an order preserving both prior-row entries. For a fixed numerical \(q\) modulo a prime \(P\), precompute q-integers and q-factorials only while their factors are invertible. If some \(q^i-1\) vanishes modulo \(P\), ordinary division is invalid even though the Gaussian binomial itself remains defined. Use the division-free recurrence, a proved q-Lucas theorem when its hypotheses match, or cancel cyclotomic-factor exponents before evaluation.

Define the q-integer \([i]_q=1+q+\cdots+q^{i-1}\) directly as a polynomial. At \(q=1\), it is \(i\), although the expression \((q^i-1)/(q-1)\) is \(0/0\). Thus at \(q=1\) the q-factorial method reduces to ordinary factorials when \(1,\ldots,n\) are units, and the Gaussian coefficient equals \(\binom nr\). When \(q\ne1\) and \(q-1\) is a unit, a q-integer vanishes exactly when \(q^i=1\). For example at \(q=2\) modulo 7, \([3]_q=7\) vanishes; the Gaussian polynomial \(\binom32_q=1+q+q^2\) is still defined and evaluates to zero. Do not “cancel zeros” after evaluation. The optional q-Lucas/cyclotomic methods require their own hypotheses; the division-free recurrence is the complete safe contract taught here.

NoteA vanishing denominator is not a vanishing count

Over \(\mathbb F_2\) with answer modulus 7, the factor \(q^3-1=7\) in the product formula is zero, so the quotient is undefined. The recurrence still returns \(\binom32_2=7\equiv0\): the count is genuinely divisible by the modulus here, but had the modulus been 5 the recurrence would have returned \(2\) while the product route still divided by a unit-free factor. Failure of the formula and vanishing of the answer are independent events.

3. Small characteristic and q-factorials

Factorial-scaled formulas require \(1,\ldots,N\) to be invertible, and q-factorials additionally require the q-integers to be invertible. When that fails, decide which of two situations you are in.

A Gaussian binomial has an integer polynomial definition and survives a failed product denominator: the object exists and only the route failed, so the division-free recurrence recovers it. By contrast, over \(\mathbb F_2\) the derivative of every polynomial has zero coefficient at \(x\) — differentiating \(a_2x^2\) gives \(2a_2x=0\) — so there is no formal antiderivative of \(x\) at all. There the failed division by 2 signifies nonexistence, not a missing trick. Determine which case you face before choosing a fallback; see small characteristic for the same distinction on ordinary factorials.

4. Rank distributions as a counting model

How many \(a\times b\) matrices over \(\mathbb F_q\) have rank \(r\)? Their column space is an \(r\)-subspace of \(\mathbb F_q^a\), with \(\binom ar_q\) choices. For a fixed basis of that space, the \(b\) columns form an \(r\times b\) coordinate matrix of full row rank. Choose its \(r\) independent rows successively, giving

\[N_{a,b}(r)=\binom ar_q\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(q^b-q^i), \qquad 0\le r\le\min(a,b).\]

Changing the chosen basis is not another matrix choice: coordinates transform with it, so fixing one basis for each subspace counts each matrix once. For binary \(2\times2\) matrices, ranks 0,1,2 occur \(1,9,6\) times, totaling \(16=2^4\) — a useful check, since the rank classes must partition all \(q^{ab}\) matrices. This is the bridge from subspaces to random linear maps and XOR sequence counting. A naive rank sum or table can still be too slow; the advanced problems require compressing the products or recognizing a convolution.

Decision checklist

  • Identify whether the q-object counts subspaces, inversions, or partitions before writing any formula; the three share a polynomial but not a model.
  • Separate the field size \(q\) from the answer modulus, and list every factor you intend to invert in the latter.
  • If any \(q^i-1\) is a non-unit modulo the answer modulus, switch to the division-free recurrence rather than patching the product.
  • For a rank question, ask what is fixed per subspace (a basis, a coordinate matrix) so that each object is counted exactly once.
  • Sanity-check totals: rank classes sum to \(q^{ab}\), subspace counts sum to the Galois number.

Exercises with hints and solutions

1. Concept check: a subspace count by hand

Count the 2-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb F_2^3\) two ways: by the product formula, and by the recurrence. Hint: a 2-subspace of \(\mathbb F_2^3\) is the kernel of a nonzero linear functional.

NoteSolution

The product gives \(\binom32_2=\frac{(2^3-1)(2^2-1)}{(2^2-1)(2-1)}=7\). The recurrence gives \(\binom32_2=\binom22_2+2^{1}\binom21_2=1+2\cdot3=7\). Directly: each 2-subspace is the kernel of a nonzero functional, there are \(2^3-1=7\) nonzero functionals, and proportional functionals share a kernel — over \(\mathbb F_2\) no two distinct nonzero functionals are proportional, so the count is 7. The three routes agree, and the last one explains why planes through the origin are as numerous as lines here.

2. Application: subspaces avoiding a vector

In \(\mathbb F_q^n\), fix a nonzero vector \(v\). How many \(r\)-subspaces do not contain \(v\)? Hint: first count those containing its one-dimensional span.

NoteSolution

Subspaces containing \(\langle v\rangle\) correspond bijectively to \((r-1)\)-subspaces of \(V/\langle v\rangle\), so the count avoiding \(v\) is \(\binom nr_q-\binom{n-1}{r-1}_q=q^r\binom{n-1}r_q\). For the latter identity, project an avoiding subspace to the quotient: its image is an \(r\)-subspace, and its lifts are graphs of the \(q^r\) linear maps from that subspace to \(\langle v\rangle\), after fixing a complementary hyperplane. For \(q=2,n=3,r=1\), there are \(7-1=6\) lines avoiding \(v\). For \(r=0\) the zero subspace always avoids a nonzero vector.

3. Synthesis: forbid an even zero-XOR subset

Among subsets of \(\mathbb F_2^N\), count those with no nonempty even-sized subset of XOR zero. Derive a count by cardinality. Hint: append one parity bit to every chosen vector.

NoteSolution

Map \(x\) to \((x,1)\in\mathbb F_2^{N+1}\). A dependence among these vectors is exactly an even zero-XOR subset. For \(r\) previously independent augmented vectors, their span has \(2^r\) elements. If \(r\ge1\), exactly half have last coordinate one: the last-coordinate map is a nonzero linear functional, so its two fibers both have size \(2^{r-1}\). The next allowed vector therefore has \(2^N-2^{r-1}\) choices. The first has \(2^N\) choices. For cardinality \(s\ge1\) the unordered count is

\[\frac{2^N\prod_{r=1}^{s-1}(2^N-2^{r-1})}{s!}, \qquad 1\le s\le N+1.\]

Add one for the empty set. Division by \(s!\) is exact over integers; the ARC146 C modulus and indexing bounds also make it a unit. Prefix products and inverse factorials evaluate all cardinalities in \(O(N)\) field operations. For \(N=2\), the counts are \(1,4,6,4\), totaling 15; the full four-element set is the only forbidden subset of the four-vector universe.

4. Synthesis: nonzero forbidden XOR versus zero

Count ordered length-\(m\) sequences in \(\mathbb F_2^k\) with no nonempty subsequence XOR equal to a specified \(x\). Derive a rank-sum expression for \(x\ne0\) and a separate formula for \(x=0\). Hint: use the preceding avoiding subspace count and full-row-rank coordinate matrices.

NoteSolution

For \(x\ne0\), the condition is \(x\) outside the span. The exact count is

\[\sum_{r=0}^{\min(m,k-1)}2^r\binom{k-1}r_2 \prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^m-2^i).\]

Each term chooses an avoiding \(r\)-subspace and a sequence spanning it. For \(x=0\), the empty subsequence is excluded, so the vectors must be independent: the count is \(\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}(2^k-2^i)\) when \(m\le k\), otherwise zero. Treating zero as an ordinary forbidden span vector would wrongly return zero for every instance. For \(k=1,x=1\), only the all-zero sequence survives; for \(k=1,x=0\), only the length-one sequence (1) survives among positive lengths. These formulas are the mathematical reduction, not a complete multi-query algorithm for CF1603 F’s largest bounds: its product/rank-sum evaluations need batch preprocessing and further algebraic simplification. Use the official contest tutorial linked on its statement for that optimization.

Summary and reference sheet

Setting Formula or method Hypotheses and resource
Subspace count Gaussian binomial product Field size a prime power; every displayed factor invertible
q-binomial at singular products Division-free recurrence Any commutative coefficient ring; \(O(nr)\)
q-integer \([i]_q=1+\cdots+q^{i-1}\) Polynomial definition; equals \(i\) at \(q=1\)
Inversions / partitions Same polynomial, different statistic Binary strings with \(r\) ones; boxes in \(r\times(n-r)\)
Rank-\(r\) matrices \(\binom ar_q\prod_{i<r}(q^b-q^i)\) \(0\le r\le\min(a,b)\); classes sum to \(q^{ab}\)

The final sanity check is semantic: are you counting ordered bases, subspaces, ordered sequences, or unordered sets? Each requires a different symmetry factor, and that factor’s modular inverse may not exist.
