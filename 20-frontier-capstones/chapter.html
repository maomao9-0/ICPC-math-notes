20 — Frontier Capstones

How to use this workshop

By now you have the machinery. What these problems test is the step no chapter can hand you: looking at a statement that mentions none of it and deciding which object you are actually counting. Nothing here announces “take a logarithm of a set power series” or “this is a Tutte matrix” — the statements are about cards, bitstrings, chessboards and graphs, and the algebra only appears once you have modelled them correctly.

So the failure mode to train against is not a slow implementation; it is a model that counts almost the right thing. Each guide below therefore spends most of its length on the reduction and its boundary cases, and ends with a hand-checkable instance you can compare against a brute-force oracle.

The skill tested here is choosing and certifying a mathematical model. Attempt each statement before reading its guide. Write down the objects, coefficient domain, invariant, target complexity, and smallest brute-force oracle. A successful solution explains why the algebra counts or detects exactly the requested objects.

Required chapters vary by problem: 16 for subspaces, 17 for binary polynomial orders, 11 for components, 18 for matching, and 19 for differential equations. Review labelled generating functions when a coefficient is factorial-normalized.

The objectives are to combine two or more chapters, carry a reduction through its boundary cases, and distinguish a mathematically valid model from an algorithm meeting the statement’s limits. The following guides contain spoilers and full reduction reasoning; they are not implementation templates.

First inclusion in a span: make 1

Official problem: ABC278 Ex — make 1.

View each card as a vector in \(\mathbb F_2^B\) and let \(e\) be the vector represented by integer one. A sequence becomes good precisely when its span first contains \(e\). Cards cannot repeat. These are two separate constraints: counting subspaces alone does not count ordered distinct draws.

Let \(F_s\) count length-\(s\) distinct-card sequences whose span avoids \(e\). Each such length-\(N-1\) prefix has \(2^B-(N-1)\) unused final cards. Subtract the extensions still avoiding \(e\):

\[\text{answer}=F_{N-1}(2^B-N+1)-F_N.\]

To compute \(F_s\), temporarily allow repetitions and call the count \(G_s\). Group equal positions into a partition of the \(s\) positions. Ordering its blocks by first occurrence assigns a unique distinct-card sequence to its blocks, giving

\[G_s=\sum_{t=0}^s {s\brace t}F_t,\qquad F_s=\sum_{t=0}^s [z^t](z(z-1)\cdots(z-s+1))\,G_t.\]

The inverse relation is the signed Stirling change of basis between ordinary powers and falling factorials. It is not inclusion–exclusion on independent equality events.

For rank \(r\), the number of \(r\)-subspaces avoiding \(e\) is \(2^r{B-1\brack r}_2\). To prove this, project onto the quotient by the line spanned by \(e\). Such a subspace projects injectively to an \(r\)-subspace of the quotient. After choosing that image, its lifts are graphs of \(2^r\) linear maps into the one-dimensional kernel. In a fixed \(r\)-space, the number of length-\(s\) spanning vector sequences is \(\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(2^s-2^i)\): count rank-\(r\) matrices by choosing \(r\) independent rows in \(\mathbb F_2^s\).

Put \(Q_0=1\) and \(Q_j=\prod_{i=1}^j(2^i-1)\). Simplifying the product gives

\[G_s=Q_s\sum_{r=0}^{\min(s,B-1)} \frac{2^{r(r+1)/2}\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}(2^{B-1-j}-1)}{Q_r}\, \frac1{Q_{s-r}}.\]

This is one ordinary convolution, after computing the two input arrays. Empty products are one. Over the statement’s modulus \(998244353\), verify that the \(Q_j\) used through \(j=N\) are nonzero before inversion; the bound \(N\le200000\) permits this directly. A product tree builds the falling-factorial polynomial of degree \(N\); divide by its last linear factor to obtain the degree-\(N-1\) row. Two dot products with \(G\) then give the answer. With multiplication cost \(M(N)\), this route costs \(O(M(N)\log N)\) plus \(O(N+\log B)\) preparation; no loop through all \(2^B\) vectors is needed.

Hand check. For \(B=2,N=2\), the avoiding length-one cards are \(0,2,3\), so \(F_1=3\). Among their six ordered distinct pairs, \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\) already span one, giving \(F_2=4\). The answer is \(3\cdot3-4=5\), agreeing with the official example.

Failure to catch: using replacement counts \(G_s\) directly ignores repeated cards. An oracle enumerates all distinct sequences for \(B\le3\) and checks XOR spans after each prefix. The official editorial describes the subspace/Stirling/convolution route.

Binary shifts and repeated factors

Official problem: CF1698G — Long Binary String.

Index bits from zero and encode a string by \(s(x)=\sum_i s_ix^i\) over \(\mathbb F_2\). XORing a copy shifted by \(j\) adds \(x^js(x)\). Thus reachable targets are polynomial multiples of \(s\), with the operation positions restricted to the finite string. If \(s=0\), no two-bit target exists.

Otherwise write \(s=x^ag\) with \(g(0)=1\). Every target’s first nonzero bit is at index at least \(a\). A target with bits at \(a,a+L\) is reachable exactly when \(g\mid1+x^L\). The earliest first bit maximizes lexicographic order; among targets with that first bit, the smallest positive \(L\) is best.

Factor \(g=\prod_i h_i^{e_i}\). Since \(g(0)=1\), the residue of \(x\) is a nonzero element of each field modulo \(h_i\). Let its odd order be \(o_i\mid2^{\deg h_i}-1\). For \(L=2^tu\) with \(u\) odd,

\[x^L+1=(x^u+1)^{2^t}.\]

The derivative of \(x^u+1\) is \(x^{u-1}\), coprime to \(x^u+1\), so the latter is square-free. Therefore divisibility is equivalent to \(o_i\mid u\) and \(2^t\ge e_i\) for all \(i\). The minimum gap is

\[L=\operatorname{lcm}_i(o_i)\cdot2^{\lceil\log_2(\max_i e_i)\rceil}.\]

For constant \(g=1\), the empty lcm is one and take the power-of-two factor as one. Find each \(o_i\) by factoring \(2^{\deg h_i}-1\) and repeatedly testing whether a prime factor can be removed from the candidate order using polynomial modular powering.

Worked instance. For input 1111, \(s=1+x+x^2+x^3=(x+1)^3\). The factor’s order is one but its multiplicity is three, so \(L=4\). The quotient \((1+x^4)/s=1+x\) means applying the string at shifts zero and one. The answer’s one-based positions are 1 and 5.

The original input length is at most 35. The lcm of distinct-factor orders is less than \(2^{\sum\deg h_i}\), while the repetition correction is at most 64. These bounds keep the constructed target and the last shifted operation far below \(10^{100}\), even allowing trailing zeros of the input pattern. This proves the finite-string boundary rather than silently replacing the task by an infinite ideal. Small string enumeration should test zero input, a monomial, a repeated factor, and a product of distinct factors.

Connected bipartite subgraphs

Official problem: ARC105 F — Lights Out on Connected Graph.

Assign each vertex its flip parity. A retained edge changes colour exactly when its endpoints have different parities, so the retained graph must be bipartite. The target is the number of connected bipartite spanning subgraphs of the given graph.

For each vertex subset \(S\), count graphs together with a valid two-colouring:

\[A[S]=\sum_{T\subseteq S}2^{e(T,S\setminus T)}.\]

Here \(T\) is the first colour class and \(e(T,S\setminus T)\) counts original crossing edges; each is independently retained or deleted. Every bipartite graph with \(k\) components appears \(2^k\) times. Its unique component partition makes the coloured count a partition exponential. Use the distinguished-component recurrence or the fast set-series logarithm of Chapter 11 to obtain \(C\), then return \(C[U]/2\) for the nonempty full set.

Complete four-vertex check. Among connected bipartite subgraphs of \(K_4\), a \(1+3\) bipartition contributes only its star, giving four. Each of the three unordered \(2+2\) bipartitions contributes the full four-cycle or one of its four spanning paths, giving \(3\cdot5=15\). A connected graph has a unique bipartition up to swapping, so these groups are disjoint. The total is 19, matching the official sample.

Precompute induced-edge counts so \(e(T,S\setminus T)=e(S)-e(T)-e(S\setminus T)\). Enumerating the cut terms costs \(O(3^n)\); connected extraction costs \(O(3^n)\) directly or \(O(n^22^n)\) with ranked multiplication. The official \(n\le17\) permits the subset model. The failed model is dividing every \(A[S]\) by two first: disconnected graphs have more than two colourings. An oracle enumerates all edge subsets on up to five vertices, then checks connectivity and bipartiteness independently.

Degree constraints become a minimum matching

Official problem: ABC412 G — Degree Harmony.

Vertex \(i\) must have degree at most \(A_i\), with the same parity as \(A_i\). Make \(A_i\) labelled copies of it. Pair copies internally at cost zero, or externally at cost one when their original vertices are adjacent. Every perfect matching consumes all copies. The copies used externally give the selected degree, and the rest occur in internal pairs, proving the bound and parity condition.

Conversely, any valid simple subgraph uses that many external copies and leaves an even number to pair internally. A matching could initially encode a multigraph, but any two external edges between the same original vertices can be replaced by internal pairs at both ends. This lowers the weight by two. Thus a minimum-weight matching never repeats an original edge and its weight equals the desired minimum simple-subgraph edge count.

If \(X=\sum_iA_i\) is odd, reject immediately. For even \(X\), a weighted Tutte matrix has weights zero and one. Its determinant has degree at most \(X\) in the weight variable. Interpolate from \(X+1\) distinct field values, keeping edge samples fixed; half its lowest nonzero degree is the answer. The baseline is \(O(X^4)\) field operations with cubic determinants, and each trial has false-zero/raised-minimum probability at most \(X/|S|\). The official bound is \(X\le150\).

Small instance. On a triangle with \(A=(1,2,3)\), retain just edge \(13\). The degrees \((1,0,1)\) satisfy all bounds and parities. A zero-edge answer cannot satisfy vertices 1 and 3, so the optimum is one. In the expanded matching, use one external \(13\) pair and pair the remaining copies internally. The determinant’s lowest degree is two.

Oracle and failure case. Enumerate original edge subsets and compare their optimum with explicit perfect matchings of the expanded graph for tiny \(X\). This catches both duplicate-edge handling and the forgotten division by two. The official editorial confirms the reduction.

Bridge: carry strings and run counts

Official problem: CF1761D — Carry Bit.

Let \(c_0=0\) and let \(c_i\) be the carry out of bit \(i-1\), for \(1\le i\le n\). Equal adjacent carry bits arise from three choices of the two input bits; a change arises from one. The requested total is the weighted count of length-\(n\) carry strings with exactly \(k\) ones.

For \(k=0\) the answer is \(3^n\). For \(k>0\), suppose the ones have \(t\) nonempty runs. Their lengths can be chosen in \(\binom{k-1}{t-1}\) ways. If the final carry is zero, the zero gaps require \(t\) positive gaps (between runs and at the end), with a possibly empty initial gap, giving \(\binom{n-k}{t}\) choices and \(2t\) carry changes. If the final carry is one, there are \(t-1\) positive internal zero gaps, giving \(\binom{n-k}{t-1}\) choices and \(2t-1\) changes. Hence

\[\sum_{t=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{t-1} \left(\binom{n-k}{t}3^{n-2t} +\binom{n-k}{t-1}3^{n-2t+1}\right).\]

Skip a term when its binomial coefficient vanishes; a feasible term always has nonnegative exponent. Each summand counts an explicitly specified run pattern and then the bit-pair choices at unchanged carries. Precomputed factorials and powers give \(O(n)\) time and memory; the modulus is prime and \(n\le10^6<p\).

For \(n=3,k=1\), only \(t=1\) contributes: \(2\cdot3+1\cdot9=15\). A brute-force oracle enumerates input pairs and computes \(\operatorname{popcount}(a)+\operatorname{popcount}(b)-\operatorname{popcount}(a+b)\). The tempting \(O(nk)\) carry DP is a useful oracle, but misses the full constraint target.

Rectangular components and a differential equation

Verified problem: QOJ60 — Chinese Elephant Chess. Count binary \(n\times m\) matrices with at most two ones per row and column. The associated bipartite graph has maximum degree two, so every connected component is an isolated vertex, a path, or an even cycle.

Use \(x\) for labelled rows and \(y\) for labelled columns, and put \(z=xy\). For \(k\) vertices of each colour, an alternating path has \(k!k!\) orders: start at its row endpoint, which removes reversal ambiguity. Its EGF contribution is \(z^k\), \(k\ge1\). A path with \(k+1\) row vertices and \(k\) column vertices has \((k+1)!k!/2\) orders for \(k\ge1\); reversing the path gives the same graph. The analogous column-heavy paths give the other term. Isolated vertices need separate treatment, since their reversal does not give two representations. An alternating cycle with \(k\) vertices of each colour, \(k\ge2\), has \(k!k!/(2k)\) representations after quotienting rotations and reversal.

Consequently the connected EGF is

\[C(x,y)=(x+y)\frac{2-z}{2(1-z)} +\frac{z}{1-z}-\frac z2-\frac12\log(1-z).\]

Each term came from a distinct component type. The all-graph EGF is \(A=\exp C\), and the required answer is \(n!m![x^ny^m]A\). Defining

\[J(z)=\frac{2-z}{2(1-z)},\qquad H(z)=\frac{\exp(z/(1-z)-z/2)}{\sqrt{1-z}},\]

we have \(A(x,y)=H(xy)\exp((x+y)J(xy))\).

Assume \(m=n+d\) with \(d\ge0\), swapping dimensions if necessary. Selecting \(r\) powers from the \(x\) exponential forces \(r+d\) from the \(y\) exponential. Therefore the diagonal-offset generating series is

\[D_d(z)=H(z)J(z)^d B_d(zJ(z)^2),\qquad B_d(t)=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{t^r}{r!(r+d)!},\]

and the answer is \(n!m![z^n]D_d(z)\). This is an actual coefficient extraction, not the substitution \(x=y\).

The successive coefficient ratio of \(B_d\) gives

\[tB_d''+(d+1)B_d'-B_d=0.\]

This produces a constant-order differential equation for \(D_d\) constructively. Let \(t(z)=zJ(z)^2\), \(h(z)=H(z)J(z)^d\), and let the prime denote differentiation in \(z\). All of the following are rational functions:

\[q=\frac{h'}h=\frac1{(1-z)^2}-\frac12+\frac1{2(1-z)}+d\frac{J'}J, \qquad a=\frac{t''}{t'}-(d+1)\frac{t'}t, \qquad b=\frac{(t')^2}{t}.\]

Applying the chain rule to \(D_d=hB_d(t)\) yields

\[D_d''-(2q+a)D_d'+(q^2-q'+aq-b)D_d=0.\]

Multiply by a common polynomial denominator and extract coefficients using Chapter 19. Its order and coefficient degrees are bounded constants independent of \(n,m,d\); thus each new coefficient uses a fixed number of previous coefficients. At zero, \(J=H=t'=1\), \(a=-(d+1)/z+O(1)\), and \(b=1/z+O(1)\). After clearing denominators without adding an unnecessary factor of \(z\), the coefficient solving for \([z^{k+1}]D_d\) is a fixed nonzero scalar times \((k+1)(k+d+1)\). Start with \(D_d(0)=1/d!\). For the official bounds \(n,m\le10^6\) modulo \(998244353\), these integers and factorials are units. Constructing the fixed-size rational operator, then scanning coefficients and preparing factorials, costs \(O(n+m)\) field operations.

Small checks. For \(n=1,m=3\), choose at most two of the three cells: \(1+3+3=7\). For \(n=m=2\), every binary matrix is valid, giving 16. These test both the isolated-path correction and the factorial normalization. Enumerating all binary matrices through \(3\times3\) checks the component model independently. A failed approach is an undifferentiated ordinary component GF: row and column labels are separate, and their factorials matter.

Grafy: a certified finite sum

Verified problem: QOJ11139 — Grafy, meaning “Graphs.” Its linked original statement asks for simple directed graphs with every in-degree and out-degree two, no loops, and permits opposite edges. The bound is \(n\le500\); a cubic counting method is appropriate. This problem is retained as an inclusion–exclusion synthesis exercise, not evidence that every graph count needs a holonomic recurrence.

Give each vertex two labelled outgoing stubs and two labelled incoming stubs. A bijection between the \(2n\) outgoing and incoming stubs is a permutation. Every valid simple graph corresponds to \(2^{2n}\) such bijections: independently order its two outgoing and two incoming edges at every vertex.

For one vertex’s outgoing pair, let \(L_1,L_2\) indicate that the first/second stub forms a loop; let \(E\) indicate that both stubs reach the same destination vertex. Let \(B\) indicate that both are loops. The local validity indicator is

\[1-L_1-L_2-E+2B.\]

To verify it, a single bad event gives zero; any intersection of two of \(L_1,L_2,E\) forces all three and equals \(B\), for which \(1-3+2=0\). Otherwise it is one. Multiply these local indicators over all source vertices and sum over stub permutations.

In the product expansion choose \(i\) vertices contributing \(2B\), \(j\) contributing one of \(-L_1,-L_2\), and \(k\) contributing \(-E\). The remaining vertices contribute one. The multinomial coefficient assigns these categories. A \(B\) vertex contributes coefficient two and two ways to match its own incoming stubs, hence \(4^i\). Each single-loop vertex has two choices of outgoing stub and two of its own incoming stub, hence \((-4)^j\). A double-destination event needs two still-free incoming stubs: its destination cannot be one of the \(i+j\) vertices whose incoming capacity has been partly used. Choose distinct destinations for the \(k\) selected sources in \(\binom{n-i-j}{k}k!\) ways, with two incoming orders and negative sign per source. The remaining unmatched stubs can be bijected arbitrarily.

This proves the exact formula

\[\frac1{4^n}\sum_{\substack{i,j,k\ge0\\i+j+k\le n}} \binom{n}{i,j,k,n-i-j-k} 4^i(-4)^j(-2)^k \binom{n-i-j}{k}k!\, (2n-2i-j-2k)!.\]

All factorial arguments are nonnegative on these bounds. Precompute factorials/inverses through \(2n\), powers, and use three nested loops; the cost is \(O(n^3)\) field operations and \(O(n)\) storage. The statement’s prime modulus exceeds \(2n\), and four is a unit. The accessible QOJ editorial gives the same cubic sum; the indicator derivation above supplies the missing interpretation of its factors.

Checks. At \(n=3\), every vertex must connect to both other vertices, giving one. At \(n=4\), deleting the selected edges from the complete loop-free digraph leaves one outgoing and one incoming edge at every vertex, hence a derangement permutation. There are nine derangements of four elements, matching the official sample. An independent oracle enumerates two outgoing neighbours per vertex and checks column sums. Counting ordered pairs of permutations without correcting their multiple decompositions would overcount graphs according to their component structure.

Exercises and postmortem

  1. Choose the invariant. Explain why the graph-colouring logarithm is valid while dividing every disconnected count by two is not.

    Solution: the unique component partition gives multiplicative colour choices, one factor two per component. The logarithm isolates a single component; only then is the multiplicity uniformly two.

  2. Boundary exercise. What does the binary-shift formula give for input 001?

    Solution: \(s=x^2\), so \(g=1\) and the least gap is one. Apply at shifts zero and one to obtain one-based positions 3 and 4. The initial power of \(x\) changes the first position but not the gap.

  3. Certification exercise. Why is verifying the first 100 coefficients of the QOJ60 recurrence insufficient by itself?

    Solution: any sequence can share a finite prefix and diverge. The component EGF, coefficient extraction, Bessel-type equation, and chain-rule operator form an identity proof; small coefficients test its algebra and indexing.

  4. Optimization exercise. Which of the Grafy and carry-bit models needs a guessed recurrence to meet its stated limits?

    Solution: neither. Grafy’s verified cubic sum fits its intended scale; Carry Bit’s run decomposition is linear. A more sophisticated technique is useful only when it preserves a proved contract and improves a relevant bottleneck.

For each attempted problem, record the recognition clue, exact counted object, coefficient domain, critical invariant, complexity, division/randomness conditions, and smallest independent oracle. Include the first tempting model that failed and the counterexample that exposed it.
