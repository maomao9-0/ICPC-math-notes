17 — Algebraic Graph Methods and Polynomial Randomization

Why this topic matters

A determinant is a signed sum over permutations. Each permutation can be drawn as directed cycles, so a matrix can encode graph objects. Carefully chosen signs cancel unwanted objects. Randomly substituting field elements then replaces a huge symbolic polynomial with one ordinary determinant.

Recognition signals include a graph-existence question with awkward combinatorial constraints, an answer determined by the first nonzero coefficient, and a DP that needs to know whether an enormous count is zero. The distinction to preserve is between an exact polynomial identity and a probabilistic evaluation of that identity.

Prerequisites: determinants, rank, and linear maps, conditional probability, and polynomial degree and interpolation.

After this chapter you should be able to prove a matching polynomial is nonzero, calculate a randomized error budget, recover a bounded-weight optimum by interpolation, and recognize when signs prevent an algebraic object from being a counting formula.

Polynomial identity testing: why random values help

Let \(P(x_1,\ldots,x_m)\) be a nonzero polynomial over a field \(F\), of total degree at most \(d\). Total degree means the maximum sum of exponents in one monomial, not the maximum exponent of a single variable. Choose each variable independently and uniformly from a finite nonempty set \(S\subseteq F\).

NoteSchwartz–Zippel bound

The probability that \(P\) evaluates to zero is at most \(\min(1,d/|S|)\). A nonzero evaluation proves the symbolic polynomial is nonzero. A zero evaluation may be unlucky.

For one variable this is the fact that a degree-\(d\) polynomial has at most \(d\) roots. For the induction, write

\[P=\sum_{j=0}^k Q_j(x_1,\ldots,x_{m-1})x_m^j,\qquad Q_k\ne0.\]

The leading coefficient \(Q_k\) has total degree at most \(d-k\), so it vanishes after the first substitutions with probability at most \((d-k)/|S|\). If it does not vanish, the remaining univariate polynomial has at most \(k\) roots. Adding these two possible causes of failure gives \(d/|S|\).

Repeating the whole test \(t\) times with independent samples, and declaring nonzero if any trial is nonzero, gives error at most \((d/|S|)^t\) when \(d<|S|\). Repetition is not useful if the chosen field makes a nonzero polynomial vanish everywhere: \(x^p-x\) on \(\mathbb F_p\) is an example. Its formal degree is \(p\), so the theorem correctly gives no useful guarantee.

Small exact example. For \(P(x,y)=x-y\) over \(\mathbb F_5\), exactly five of the 25 pairs vanish. The error is \(1/5\), matching the total-degree-one bound. If you sample one value and reuse it for both variables, the error becomes one: independence is a hypothesis, not an implementation detail.

Pairings, Pfaffians, and hafnians

A perfect matching partitions an even number \(n=2r\) of vertices into pairs that are edges. An alternating matrix has \(A_{ii}=0\) and \(A_{ji}=-A_{ij}\). In characteristic different from two this is a skew-symmetric matrix; in characteristic two the zero diagonal must still be imposed.

For each pairing, order its pairs \((i_1,j_1),\ldots,(i_r,j_r)\) so \(i_k<j_k\) and \(i_1<\cdots<i_r\). Its sign is the sign of the permutation \((i_1,j_1,\ldots,i_r,j_r)\). Define

\[\operatorname{Pf}(A)=\sum_{\text{pairings }M}\operatorname{sgn}(M)\prod_{(i,j)\in M}A_{ij}.\]

The hafnian replaces every pairing sign by \(+1\). It directly counts weighted pairings when the weights count choices; a general Pfaffian does not.

Four vertices. The three pairings give

\[\operatorname{Pf}(A)=A_{12}A_{34}-A_{13}A_{24}+A_{14}A_{23}.\]

If every upper-triangular entry is one, the Pfaffian is \(1-1+1=1\), although the complete four-vertex graph has three perfect matchings. Replacing distinct edge variables by one can therefore destroy counting information.

Why its square is a determinant

The identity is \(\det A=\operatorname{Pf}(A)^2\). One proof route parallels elimination. For an invertible leading alternating block

\[J=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}J&B\\-B^T&D\end{pmatrix},\]

simultaneous row and column elimination produces the remaining alternating matrix \(D+B^TJ^{-1}B\). The determinant becomes \(a^2\det(D+B^TJ^{-1}B)\), while the Pfaffian becomes \(a\operatorname{Pf}(D+B^TJ^{-1}B)\).

The Pfaffian rule follows from its pairing expansion: it is multilinear and alternating in the indexed vectors of an alternating bilinear form. Adding one vector to another changes no value (the extra term repeats a vector); swapping two indexed vectors changes the sign. These are the simultaneous row/column operations used in the elimination. Induction from the empty matrix, whose Pfaffian and determinant are both one, proves the identity for matrices with available pivots. Applying this to the matrix of independent symbolic entries proves a polynomial identity, so specialization also covers singular matrices and characteristic two.

This gives cubic field-operation Pfaffian elimination with pair pivots. If the remaining matrix has no nonzero entry, its positive-size Pfaffian is zero. Track permutation signs; each pair pivot contributes \(a\), not \(a^2\). General hafnian computation has no analogous cubic cancellation algorithm; direct pairing recursion is exponential. Special graph classes may admit special sign choices, but arbitrary matching counts cannot be replaced by a determinant.

The Tutte matrix

For a simple undirected graph on vertices \(1,\ldots,n\), assign an independent variable \(x_{ij}\) to each edge with \(i<j\). Put \(x_{ij}\) in entry \((i,j)\), \(-x_{ij}\) in entry \((j,i)\), and zero elsewhere. This is the Tutte matrix \(T\). For perfect matching, reject odd \(n\) immediately; the pairing argument below uses even \(n\). The rank result also applies to odd-size graphs.

Its Pfaffian has one monomial for every perfect matching, each with coefficient \(+1\) or \(-1\). Distinct matchings use different edge sets, so these monomials cannot cancel. Therefore the Pfaffian, and hence the determinant, is nonzero exactly when the graph has a perfect matching. This argument also works in characteristic two: signs coincide, but distinct monomials remain distinct.

There is a useful direct explanation from the determinant’s cycle covers. Pair every term containing an odd cycle of length at least three with the term obtained by reversing the odd cycle containing the smallest possible vertex. The permutation sign is unchanged while the product of alternating entries changes sign, so the pair cancels. The remaining even cycles admit alternating matching edges. Conversely, a perfect matching contributes its unique product of squared edge variables through two-cycles, with nonzero coefficient.

Sample every edge variable independently from \(S\) and evaluate the determinant. Its total degree is \(n\), so a graph with a perfect matching is incorrectly rejected with probability at most \(n/|S|\). A graph without one is always rejected. This certifies existence, not an explicit list of matching edges. Recovering the matching requires an additional algorithm.

Rank and maximum matching

If the maximum matching size is \(\nu\), the symbolic rank is \(2\nu\). A matching of size \(\nu\) supplies a nonsingular principal \(2\nu\)-vertex minor. For the other inequality, pair elimination of an alternating matrix shows its rank is even and equals the size of some nonsingular principal submatrix. Such a principal submatrix has a perfect matching by the preceding theorem, so its size is at most \(2\nu\).

Evaluation cannot increase symbolic rank: every larger minor is identically zero. A fixed nonzero \(2\nu\) minor remains nonzero with probability at least \(1-2\nu/|S|\). Thus the evaluated rank divided by two is a lower estimate of \(\nu\), equal to it with that probability. Do not equate the rank itself with the number of matched edges.

Weighted matching: the first nonzero coefficient

Suppose each edge has a nonnegative integer weight \(w_{ij}\le W\). Replace its variable in the Tutte matrix by \(x_{ij}y^{w_{ij}}\), retaining opposite signs below the diagonal. Let the resulting matrix be \(B(y)\). In its Pfaffian, each perfect matching contributes an edge-variable monomial times \(y\) raised to its total weight.

Let \(\mu\) be the minimum matching weight. The coefficient of \(y^\mu\) in the Pfaffian is a nonzero polynomial in edge variables because its matching monomials are distinct. Hence

\[\min\{k:[y^k]\det B(y)\ne0\}=2\mu.\]

The factor two comes from squaring the Pfaffian. For a graph of two vertices joined by an edge of weight three, \(\det B(y)=x_{12}^2y^6\): the answer is three, not six.

Now sample the edge variables once per trial, keeping them fixed across every evaluation of \(y\). With probability at least \(1-n/|S|\), the lowest determinant coefficient survives. The univariate determinant has degree at most \(nW\), so \(nW+1\) distinct field points recover it by interpolation. This requires enough field elements; for integer points modulo \(p\), require \(p>nW\). Cubic determinant evaluation at each point gives a baseline \(O((nW+1)n^3)\) field operations, plus interpolation; this is \(O(Wn^4)\) when \(W\ge1\). When all weights are zero, one determinant suffices. This is useful for small weights; huge binary-encoded weights make this approach infeasible even though the formula remains true.

Sampling new edge values at each interpolation point would evaluate different polynomials and invalidate interpolation. Random cancellation can only raise the reported minimum degree or erase the polynomial; it cannot create a lower-degree term. Independent repetitions may therefore take the smallest finite answer, with a one-sided error bound.

Worked weighted example. On four vertices give edges \(12,34\) weight one; edges \(13,24\) weight two; omit the other edges. Then

\[\operatorname{Pf}B(y)=x_{12}x_{34}y^2-x_{13}x_{24}y^4.\]

The determinant starts at degree four, so the minimum matching weight is two. Even though the two matchings can interact in higher coefficients after squaring, they cannot cancel the unique lowest edge monomial.

Determinant polynomials and rank-one changes

For \(n\times n\) constant matrices \(A,B\), \(\det(A+xB)\) has degree at most \(n\): each determinant term multiplies \(n\) entries of degree at most one. Thus \(n+1\) distinct evaluations and interpolation give a general \(O(n^4)\) baseline. Singular \(A\) is allowed; factoring out \(A\) would wrongly assume it invertible.

For column vectors \(u,v\) and invertible \(A\), column multilinearity gives the determinant lemma

\[\det(A+uv^T)=\det(A)\bigl(1+v^TA^{-1}u\bigr).\]

To see the scalar factor, factor out \(A\) and expand \(\det(I+ab^T)\) with \(a=A^{-1}u\). Terms selecting two updated columns vanish because those columns are parallel; choosing no updated column contributes one and choosing column \(i\) contributes \(a_ib_i\). If \(1+v^TA^{-1}u\ne0\), multiplication verifies

\[ (A+uv^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}- \frac{A^{-1}uv^TA^{-1}}{1+v^TA^{-1}u}.\]

This is Sherman–Morrison. The denominator condition is essential. The adjugate is the transpose of the matrix of signed minors; cofactor expansion gives \(A\operatorname{adj}(A)=\det(A)I\) even for singular \(A\). The division-free form

\[\det(A+uv^T)=\det A+v^T\operatorname{adj}(A)u\]

therefore remains valid without an inverse, by the same column expansion.

A contest workflow

  1. Define the exact polynomial and prove that the required object makes it nonzero. Distinct formal variables can prevent cancellations that numerical counts suffer.
  2. Bound its total degree in the variables you randomize. Record the field, sampling set, independence, and number of trials.
  3. Choose between one evaluation, rank computation, and univariate interpolation; state which quantities remain fixed during a trial.
  4. Interpret zero correctly and calculate a union bound over the actual outputs you need to certify.
  5. Compare tiny instances with explicit graph enumeration before trusting large random instances.

Exercises, solutions, and contest guidance

  1. Cancellation. Compute the Pfaffian and hafnian of the alternating four-by-four matrix whose upper entries are all one.

    Hint: there are three pairings. Solution: the Pfaffian is \(1-1+1=1\); the hafnian is \(1+1+1=3\). Its determinant is one, not the matching count.

  2. Probability budget. A nonzero target polynomial has degree at most 100 and samples use all of \(\mathbb F_{1000000007}\). Bound two independent false-zero trials.

    Solution: \((100/1000000007)^2<10^{-14}\). This is a worst-case mathematical bound, independent of any observed success rate.

  3. Singular update. Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)\) and \(u=v=(0,1)^T\). Find the new determinant without using \(A^{-1}\).

    Hint: use the adjugate. Solution: \(\operatorname{adj}(A)=\operatorname{diag}(0,1)\), so the new determinant is \(0+1=1\); the updated matrix is the identity.

  4. Application — Degree Harmony, ABC412 G. Replace vertex \(i\) by \(A_i\) copies. Join copies of the same vertex with weight-zero edges; join copies of adjacent original vertices with weight-one edges. A perfect matching assigns some stubs to original edges and pairs unused stubs internally. Internal pairs consume an even number, so the selected degree at \(i\) is at most \(A_i\) and has the same parity. Conversely, any graph satisfying those conditions matches its used stubs externally and the remaining even number internally. A general matching might create repeated original edges. However, two edges between the same original vertices can be replaced by one internal pair at each endpoint, decreasing weight by two. A minimum-weight matching therefore creates no repeated edge and corresponds to a simple subgraph as required. The official editorial gives the reduction and determinant method. The degree/interpolation contract above is the needed algebraic implementation guidance; see Chapter 19 for a worked instance.

  5. Alternate method — No Need, ABC056 D. Card \(i\) matters exactly when the other cards contain a subset of sum in \([K-a_i,K)\). Work modulo \(x^K\) and use

    \[F(x)=\prod_j(1+r_jx^{a_j}).\]

    Hint: divide out card \(i\); its factor has constant term one. Solution guidance: the coefficient for any subset sum is a sum of distinct square-free monomials in the formal variables \(r_j\), so existence is equivalent to a nonzero polynomial even modulo a fixed prime. Assign independent uniform values in \(\mathbb F_p\). For each necessary card, choose one existing witness sum before sampling. Its coefficient has degree at most \(N-1\), and missing that card implies this witness coefficient vanished. A union bound over at most \(N\) cards gives \(N(N-1)/p\), without an extra factor \(K\). The products and quotients can be maintained in \(O(NK)\) field operations by coefficient recurrences. Ordinary coefficients modulo \(p\) can instead hide every witness through cancellation. This modelling method is documented in the editorial on AtCoder.
