13 — Discrete Sums and Diophantine Tools

Sums you cannot loop over

Three tasks, each with a bound around \(10^{18}\):

  • \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} i^{100} \bmod p\);
  • \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lfloor (ai+b)/m \rfloor\);
  • the smallest solution of \(x^2 - 61y^2 = 1\).

A loop is out of the question in all three, and yet none of them needs one. The first is a polynomial in \(n\) of degree \(101\), so \(102\) samples pin it down completely. The second is a lattice-point count whose geometry lets you swap the two axes, which is the Euclidean algorithm in disguise — \(O(\log m)\) steps. The third is generated by a single fundamental unit found from a continued fraction.

That is the theme: what object stays small even though the input range is huge? A polynomial is determined by finitely many values. A Euclidean descent shrinks its parameters geometrically. A Pell orbit is one multiplication repeated. Contest statements almost never name any of these, so the skill being trained here is recognition, plus the hypotheses that make each reduction legal — most of these methods divide by something, and in a modular problem that something can be zero.

Prerequisites and learning objectives

Read Bézout and units and polynomials. Bernoulli preprocessing also uses formal power series; interpolation does not.

You should finish able to prove degree bounds, evaluate huge polynomial sums, count lattice points without enumeration, and convert rational and quadratic constraints into integer recurrences. Symbols \(n, d\) are nonnegative integers unless stated otherwise, and inequalities concern ordered integers, never residues.

1. Polynomial interpolation

Over a field \(K\), a polynomial of degree at most \(d\) is determined by its values at \(d+1\) distinct points: if two such polynomials agreed there, their difference would have more roots than its degree, so it is zero. That is the entire reason huge-index polynomial sums are cheap — the infinite function is really \(d+1\) numbers.

Lagrange interpolation writes the polynomial down:

\[P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d} y_i\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.\]

The factor for index \(i\) is \(1\) at \(x_i\) and \(0\) at all other samples, so the sum assigns the requested value at each point. At arbitrary points this costs \(O(d^2)\) for one query. When \(x_i=i\), prefix/suffix products and factorials reduce one query to \(O(d)\) after \(O(d)\) preprocessing:

\[\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-j}{i-j} =\frac{\prod_{j<i}(x-j)\prod_{j>i}(x-j)}{i!(-1)^{d-i}(d-i)!}.\]

Preconditions are not cosmetic: sample points must be distinct and their differences invertible. In \(\mathbb F_p\), the points \(0,\ldots,d\) therefore require \(d<p\). For many arbitrary queries use a product tree and multipoint evaluation.

The unit-difference condition also licenses interpolation over a commutative ring, but the field root-bound proof does not: \(x^2-1\) has four roots \(1,3,5,7\) modulo 8. Over a field, the root bound follows by dividing out one factor \((x-a)\) for each distinct root.

NoteExample: reconstructing a quadratic

Suppose \(P(0)=1,P(1)=2,P(2)=5\) and \(\deg P\le2\) over \(\mathbb Q\). At \(x=4\), the Lagrange basis values are \(3,-8,6\), so \(P(4)=3-16+30=17\). The polynomial is \(x^2+1\). Without the degree bound, adding \(x(x-1)(x-2)\) preserves all three samples but changes the answer.

For consecutive evaluation, prefix product \(L_i=\prod_{0\le j<i}(x-j)\) and suffix product \(R_i=\prod_{i<j\le d}(x-j)\) omit exactly the forbidden factor. Multiply \(y_iL_iR_i\) by inverse factorials and \((-1)^{d-i}\). If \(x\) equals a sample point, return that sample directly. For \(d<p\), reduce a huge integer query modulo \(p\) only after proving the desired function is represented by this polynomial.

2. Finite differences and polynomial sums

Define \(\Delta P(x)=P(x+1)-P(x)\) over any coefficient ring. It is the discrete analogue of a derivative: the leading \(x^d\) terms cancel, so the degree drops by at least one. It drops by exactly one when the leading coefficient times \(d\) is nonzero; this can fail in small characteristic, as \(\Delta(x^p)=1\) over \(\mathbb F_p\). When \(1,\ldots,d\) are units, repeated differences give the Newton expansion

\[P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d} \Delta^k P(0){x\choose k}.\]

For example, \(\Delta(x^2)=2x+1\) and \(\Delta^2(x^2)=2\). This Newton basis is ideal when values are consecutive or arguments shift repeatedly. Over a characteristic-zero field (more generally a \(\mathbb Q\)-algebra), or when \(1,\ldots,d+1\) are units in the coefficient ring, it also proves that if \(P\) has degree \(d\), then

\[S(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}P(i)\]

has degree \(d+1\), because \(\Delta S=P\) and its leading coefficient is the leading coefficient of \(P\) divided by \(d+1\). Therefore evaluate \(S(0),\ldots,S(d+1)\) directly and interpolate at huge \(n\). More generally, the needed condition is that all denominators in this Newton/interpolation basis are units. In small characteristic, \(S\) may not be representable by a polynomial of the expected degree, so the ordinary consecutive-point method is not licensed.

Here is the basis derivation. When \(1,\ldots,d\) are units, define \(\binom{x}{k}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)/k!\). Pascal’s identity gives \(\Delta\binom{x}{k}=\binom{x}{k-1}\) for \(k\ge1\). These polynomials have distinct degrees and unit leading coefficients and therefore form a basis through degree \(d\). In \(P=\sum_k a_k\binom{x}{k}\), apply \(\Delta^j\) and set \(x=0\): only \(a_j\) survives. Thus \(a_j=\Delta^jP(0)\). Summation is then telescoping:

\[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{i}{k} =\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\binom{i+1}{k+1}-\binom{i}{k+1}\right) =\binom{n}{k+1}.\]

For \(P(x)=x^2\), the difference coefficients are \(0,1,2\); hence \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i^2=\binom n2+2\binom n3=n(n-1)(2n-1)/6\). At \(n=4\), this is \(1+4+9=14\). The identity is exact over integers, but evaluating it modulo 2 or 3 needs integer cancellation, not division by 6.

Recognition signal: a fixed exponent or degree, an enormous upper bound, and a modular answer. Do not interpolate when the degree depends on \(n\), or when the sample denominators vanish modulo the chosen prime.

3. Floor sum via Euclidean descent

Define

\[F(n,m,a,b)=\sum_{0\le i<n}\left\lfloor\frac{ai+b}{m}\right\rfloor,\]

with nonnegative inputs and \(m>0\). Geometrically this counts the lattice points under a line of rational slope, and the whole algorithm is one idea: strip the part of the line you can sum in closed form, then swap the axes. Swapping turns the slope into its reciprocal, which is exactly the step of the Euclidean algorithm — hence \(O(\log m)\) iterations.

First strip quotient parts. Writing \(a=q_am+a'\) and \(b=q_bm+b'\) separates full multiples of \(m\), whose floors are ordinary arithmetic progressions:

\[a=q_am+a',\quad b=q_bm+b'.\]

Exactly,

\[ F(n,m,a,b)=q_a\frac{n(n-1)}2+q_bn+F(n,m,a',b'). \]

Now consider the normalized remainder with \(0\le a,b<m\). The sum counts lattice points \((i,j)\) with \(0\le i<n\) and \(1\le j\le(ai+b)/m\). Let \(y=an+b\), the numerator one step beyond the final \(i\); this is the boundary used by the half-open rectangle. If \(y<m\), no point reaches the first horizontal level and the remainder is zero. Otherwise \(a>0\); put \(q=\lfloor y/m\rfloor\) and \(r=y\bmod m\). On horizontal row \(1\le j\le q\), substitute \(t=n-i\) in \(jm\le ai+b=y-at\); the number of points on that row is \(\lfloor((q-j)m+r)/a\rfloor\). Summing and reindexing \(q-j=0,\ldots,q-1\) swaps the axes and yields

\[F(n,m,a,b)=F(\lfloor y/m\rfloor,a,m,y\bmod m)\]

after the parameter update \((n,m,a,b)\leftarrow(\lfloor y/m\rfloor,a,m,y\bmod m)\). Normalize again at the start of the next iteration: the new numerator coefficient is the old modulus and may be at least the new denominator. For example, \(F(4,7,3,2)=0+0+1+1=2\), and the update gives \(F(2,3,7,0)\); stripping \(7=2\cdot3+1\) gives \(2+F(2,3,1,0)=2\). This is the Euclidean algorithm geometrically, so there are \(O(\log m)\) iterations.

The horizontal count is between zero and \(n\): for \(j\ge1\), \(y-jm<an\) because \(b<m\). A top row may be empty; for \(F(1,2,1,1)\), \(y=2\) but the only actual floor is zero. The empty row makes the half-open update uniform. Maintain original answer = accumulated stripped terms + current F. Both normalization and axis swap preserve this invariant. Stop on \(n=0\), or after normalization when \(an+b<m\); if \(a=0\), the latter holds automatically. The logarithmic bound counts arithmetic operations.

Use __int128 when its range covers the proven product bounds. For negative \(a\) or \(b\), use mathematical floor division to normalize them; C++ truncation is not floor division. A library routine can have overflow semantics different from exact mathematics; the AtCoder contract returns modulo \(2^{64}\) on overflow.

Applications include counting lattice points, sums of residues, Beatty sequences, and recursively counting points constrained by rational lines.

The residue sum follows from \(u=m\lfloor u/m\rfloor+(u\bmod m)\):

\[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(ai+b)\bmod m =a\frac{n(n-1)}2+bn-mF(n,m,a,b).\]

For \((4,7,3,2)\) the residues \(2,5,1,4\) sum to \(12=18+8-14\). For \((n,m,a,b)=(3,5,-1,0)\) the floors are \(0,-1,-1\) and residues \(0,4,3\). Truncating division toward zero breaks this normalization.

4. Continued fractions

The same Euclidean descent, read as approximation rather than as counting: each quotient tells you how far you can go along one direction before the best rational approximation of a real number changes.

Euclid’s quotients form the finite simple continued fraction of a positive rational number, and the same process gives an infinite expansion for an irrational number \(\alpha\):

\[\alpha=[a_0;a_1,\ldots].\]

This means \(a_0+1/(a_1+1/(a_2+\cdots))\), with \(a_0=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\) and subsequent \(a_i\) positive integers. Invert the fractional part repeatedly to find the quotients. For rationals, stop at an integer; choose the final coefficient greater than one unless there is only one coefficient. A finite truncation is a convergent. For example, \(13/5=[2;1,1,2]\).

Convergents satisfy

\[P_i=a_iP_{i-1}+P_{i-2},\qquad Q_i=a_iQ_{i-1}+Q_{i-2},\]

starting with \((P[-2],P[-1])=(0,1)\) and \((Q[-2],Q[-1])=(1,0)\). Substitution in the nested fractions proves these recurrences. They give \(2/1,3/1,5/2,13/5\) for the example. The determinant \(D_i=P_iQ_{i-1}-P_{i-1}Q_i\) obeys \(D_i=-D_{i-1}\), so \(D_i=\pm1\); in particular every convergent is reduced. For the tail \(t=[a_{i+1};a_{i+2},\ldots]>1\),

\[\alpha=\frac{tP_i+P_{i-1}}{tQ_i+Q_{i-1}},\qquad \left|\alpha-\frac{P_i}{Q_i}\right| =\frac1{Q_i(tQ_i+Q_{i-1})}.\]

This explains alternating sides and rapidly decreasing errors. The adjacent determinant also makes these integer vectors a basis of \(\mathbb Z^2\); expressing a competing \((p,q)\) in this basis is the mechanism behind the best-approximation theorem below. Its specified error measure is essential. For an irrational \(\alpha\), its convergents are best approximations of the second kind: no fraction \(p/q\) with \(0<q<Q_i\) has a smaller value of \(|q\alpha-p|\) than \(|Q_i\alpha-P_i|\). (For a rational input, the final convergent is the number itself.) The recurrence is just the record of successive Euclidean quotients, so it also supplies the Bezout-like identity below. For other bounded-denominator objectives, the optimum may be a convergent or an intermediate (semiconvergent), so derive the relevant comparison rather than citing “best approximation” without a definition. Adjacent convergents obey \(P_iQ_{i-1}-P_{i-1}Q_i=\pm1\), a built-in error check.

Use continued fractions for rational approximation, modular inequalities, and Pell equations. Intermediate fractions replace the next quotient by \(1\le t\le a_i\): \((tP_{i-1}+P_{i-2})/(tQ_{i-1}+Q_{i-2})\). For approximating \(13/5\) with denominator at most 3 in absolute error, \(8/3\) beats the convergent \(5/2\), since \(1/15<1/10\). An optimum need not be a convergent. Rational Euclid takes logarithmically many arithmetic steps. Compare by exact cross products with a proved width bound or big integers; 128 bits do not cover arbitrary input lengths.

5. Pell equations

Pell equations are how contests hide exponentially growing answers behind tiny input. Solutions are not found by search; they are generated, by repeatedly multiplying one fundamental solution.

For nonsquare \(D>0\), Pell’s equation is

\[x^{2}-Dy^{2}=1.\]

The continued fraction of \(\sqrt D\) is periodic. The first convergent satisfying the equation gives the fundamental solution \((x_1,y_1)\) with \(x_1,y_1>0\). Multiplication of numbers \(x+y\sqrt D\) preserves \(x^2-Dy^2\), proving powers remain solutions. The stronger theorem is that every solution with \(x>0,y>0\) follows from

\[(x_k+y_k\sqrt D)=(x_1+y_1\sqrt D)^{k},\]

or the recurrence

\[x'=x_1x+Dy_1y,\qquad y'=x_1y+y_1x.\]

The converse needs descent. Write \(\varepsilon=x_1+y_1\sqrt D>1\) for the least positive norm-one unit. Given another \(u=x+y\sqrt D>1\), choose \(k\) with \(1\le v=u\varepsilon^{-k}<\varepsilon\). Since \(\varepsilon^{-1}=x_1-y_1\sqrt D\), \(v\) still has integer coefficients and norm one. They are \((v+v^{-1})/2>0\) and \((v-v^{-1})/(2\sqrt D)\ge0\). Unless \(v=1\), this contradicts minimality of \(\varepsilon\). Thus \(u=\varepsilon^k\); norm preservation alone would not establish completeness.

For \(D=2\), \([1;2,2,\ldots]\) gives fundamental pair \((3,2)\), then \((17,12)\) and \((99,70)\). Searching convergents suffices because a Pell solution satisfies \(|x/y-\sqrt D|=1/(y^2(x/y+\sqrt D))<1/(2y^2)\), and the continued-fraction approximation criterion says a reduced fraction this close must be a convergent. Periodicity and existence of a solution are classical quadratic-surd theorems, separate from the power-generation argument. The integer expansion maintains \((\sqrt D+m)/d\) with \(d\mid D-m^2\):

\[m'=da-m,\quad d'=(D-m'^2)/d,\quad a'=\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor\sqrt D\rfloor+m'}{d'}\right\rfloor.\]

Start with \(m=0,d=1,a=\lfloor\sqrt D\rfloor\). Bounded reduced surd states yield periodicity. This avoids cumulative floating-point error; the number of convergents is not uniformly \(O(\log D)\), and the fundamental solution may have many more bits than \(D\).

For \(x^{2}-Dy^{2}=N\), existence and seed generation are subtler; do not assume the \(N=1\) theorem transfers unchanged. Contest tasks often bound the search enough to find seeds and then advance each orbit by the fundamental unit.

Values grow exponentially. Use big integers or stop with division-based overflow checks before multiplication.

6. Linear Diophantine equations and CRT viewpoint

For \((a,b)\ne(0,0)\), the equation \(ax+by=c\) has an integer solution iff \(g=\gcd(a,b)\) divides \(c\): the left side is always a multiple of \(g\), and Bézout constructs one whenever that necessary condition holds. Extended Euclid finds \(u,v\) with \(au+bv=g\); scale by \(c/g\). All solutions are

\[x=x_0+k\frac bg,\qquad y=y_0-k\frac ag.\]

To prove completeness, subtract solutions: \(a\Delta x=-b\Delta y\). For nonzero coefficients, coprimality of \(a/g,b/g\) implies \(b/g\) divides \(\Delta x\), and substitution determines \(\Delta y\). If one coefficient is zero, its nonzero partner fixes one variable and the other is free, consistently with the formula. Here \(g>0\) and \(k\) ranges over all integers.

If \(a=b=0\), all integer pairs solve the equation when \(c=0\), and none solve when \(c\ne0\); keep that degenerate case outside an extended-gcd routine.

Intersect this parameterization with sign or interval constraints using correct floor/ceiling division. A congruence \(ax\equiv c\pmod m\) is the same equation \(ax+my=c\); after dividing by \(\gcd(a,m)\), invert in the reduced modulus.

For \(6x+9y=30\), take \((x_0,y_0)=(2,2)\) to obtain \(x=2+3k,y=2-2k\). Nonnegativity requires \(\lceil-2/3\rceil\le k\le1\), yielding \((2,2),(5,0)\). A linear objective becomes linear in \(k\), so its minimum on a bounded feasible interval is at an endpoint; zero slope makes all points tie. Check separately whether the feasible interval is empty or unbounded.

For nonlinear-looking determinant constraints such as \(ad-bc=k\), fixing two variables often reduces the rest to Bézout. For quadratic forms, factorization, modular obstructions, descent, or Pell reduction may apply.

7. Stern–Brocot and modular extrema

Start with boundaries \(a/b=0/1\) and \(c/d=1/0\), the latter denoting infinity. Their mediant is \((a+c)/(b+d)\). Boundaries maintain \(bc-ad=1\), so the mediant is reduced and lies strictly between them. For a target in this interval, replace the lower or upper boundary by the mediant according to its side. The determinant invariant is preserved. To see that every positive reduced \(p/q\) is reached, write it uniquely as \((ua+vc)/(ub+vd)\) with positive integers \(u,v\) while it is strictly between the boundaries. Comparing with the mediant subtracts the smaller of \(u,v\) from the larger. Euclid eventually gives \(u=v=1\). The strict interval choices also prove uniqueness: this is the Stern–Brocot tree.

Repeated same-side updates can be batched: replacing the lower bound \(t\) times gives \((a+tc)/(b+td)\). Solve the exact denominator and target inequalities for the largest legal integer \(t\), observing whether the target bound is strict. The run lengths relate to continued-fraction quotients, with initial/final adjustments depending on the sentinel and endpoint conventions. Use the inequalities as the contract, not a memorized unqualified run sequence.

Functions such as \(\min_{0\le i<n} (ai+b) \bmod m\) use the same Euclidean geometry as floor_sum: quotient away full multiples, then swap axes after the first wrap. Keep half-open interval conventions consistent.

An alternative derived contract avoids a new recursive template. For a proposed threshold \(0\le t<m\), write \(ai+b=qm+r\) with \(0\le r<m\). Then

\[[r<t]=1+\left\lfloor\frac{ai+b}{m}\right\rfloor -\left\lfloor\frac{ai+b+m-t}{m}\right\rfloor.\]

Thus the count below \(t\) is \(n+F(n,m,a,b)-F(n,m,a,b+m-t)\). For \(n>0\), binary-search the first \(t\in[1,m]\) whose count is positive and return \(t-1\). This costs \(O(\log^2m)\) arithmetic operations. Direct Euclidean minimum algorithms can remove a logarithm, but need their own interval invariant. For \(n=0\) there is no minimum.

8. Bernoulli numbers and Faulhaber sums

Section 2 computed one power sum at one degree. If a problem needs the closed form for many degrees, there is a single generating function that produces them all at once.

Bernoulli numbers are defined by

\[\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}=\sum_{k\ge0}B_k\frac{x^{k}}{k!}.\]

With the convention \(i^0=1\) even at \(i=0\), they give for \(d\ge0\)

\[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i^{d}=\frac1{d+1}\sum_{j=0}^{d}{d+1\choose j}B_j n^{d+1-j}.\]

To derive the formula, count the exponential weight of each summand:

\[\sum_{d\ge0}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i^d\right)\frac{x^d}{d!} =\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}e^{ix} =\frac{e^{nx}-1}{e^x-1} =\frac{e^{nx}-1}{x}\frac{x}{e^x-1}.\]

The coefficient of \(x^d\), multiplied by \(d!\), chooses degree \(j\) from the Bernoulli factor and degree \(d-j\) from the other factor; this yields the displayed binomial coefficient. The proof is over \(\mathbb Q[[x]]\), or truncated over a ring where the denominators through \(d+1\) are units. The first terms are \(B_0=1,B_1=-1/2,B_2=1/6\), giving sums \(n\), \(n(n-1)/2\), and \(n(n-1)(2n-1)/6\). The formal quotient \(x/(e^x-1)\) means the inverse of \((e^x-1)/x\), whose constant term is 1; it is not inversion of a zero-constant series. To obtain degree \(d\), form the exponential through degree \(d+1\).

This computes all power-sum formulas through one FPS inversion and factorial scaling. Check the \(B_1\) sign convention and require all denominators through \(d+1\) to be invertible. For one degree and one query, consecutive-point interpolation is often simpler; Bernoulli preprocessing wins for many degrees.

9. Pitfalls

  • C++ % and / on negatives do not implement Euclidean remainder/floor division.
  • Lagrange denominators vanish if the interpolation degree reaches the modulus.
  • Inferring “it is polynomial” from samples is not a proof; establish a degree bound.
  • \(a*n+b\) can overflow before the floor sum shrinks it.
  • Pell recurrences need all seed orbits for generalized equations.
  • Extended-GCD coefficients may overflow even if the final normalized answer fits.

Exercises with solutions

1. Concept check: polynomiality before interpolation

Compute \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(i^2+1)\) as a polynomial and evaluate at \(n=4\). Why do four sampled values suffice? Hint: summation raises the degree by one over \(\mathbb Q\).

NoteSolution

The answer is \(n+n(n-1)(2n-1)/6\), degree three, and its value at 4 is 18. The degree proof licenses four samples; four samples alone do not prove the function is cubic. Modulo a prime \(p>3\), these rational coefficients and the four consecutive sample differences are valid units. For smaller moduli, evaluate the integer formula by exact cancellation or use a different basis.

2. Application: a residue threshold

Among \(i=0,1,2,3\), how many residues \((3i+2)\bmod7\) are below 3? Derive the general threshold count rather than enumerate a large \(n\). Hint: compare floors before and after adding \(m-t\).

NoteSolution

The residues \(2,5,1,4\) give two. The general formula is \(n+F(n,m,a,b)-F(n,m,a,b+m-t)\); here it is \(4+2-4=2\). For \(t=0\) it gives zero, and for \(t=m\) it gives \(n\), checking both boundaries. Two floor sums cost \(O(\log m)\) arithmetic operations, even for negative \(a,b\) after mathematical floor normalization.

3. Advanced: an interval Diophantine optimum

Find nonnegative integers satisfying \(6x+9y=30\) that minimize \(2x+5y\). Hint: optimize the single integer parameter, not both original variables.

NoteSolution

\(x=2+3k,y=2-2k\), with \(k=0,1\). The objective is \(14-4k\), so choose \(k=1\) and obtain \((5,0)\) with cost 10. Extended Euclid and interval intersection cost \(O(\log\max(|a|,|b|))\) arithmetic steps in the general case. Verify the original equation and bounds after mapping coordinates.

4. Synthesis: square triangular numbers

Find the first three positive integers that are both a square and a triangular number, where the \(n\)th triangular number is \(n(n+1)/2\). Hint: complete the square in \(n(n+1)=2m^2\).

NoteSolution

Multiply by 4 and add 1: \((2n+1)^2-8m^2=1\). The fundamental solution for \(D=8\) is \((x,m)=(3,1)\), and multiplying by \(3+\sqrt8\) produces \((17,6),(99,35)\). Thus \(n=(x-1)/2\) is \(1,8,49\), and the numbers are \(1,36,1225\). All generated \(x\) stay odd. Conversely every positive solution of the original problem gives a positive Pell solution, so the fundamental-unit descent proves completeness. Enumerating \(n\) up to the largest answer ignores the exponentially spaced structure; generate units with big integers instead.

Contest checklist and reference

  • Prove the degree bound before sampling a purported polynomial.
  • State a coefficient field and list every denominator required.
  • Keep half-open integer bounds consistent through floor and axis swaps.
  • Distinguish absolute approximation error from \(|q\alpha-p|\).
  • Prove that a generated family includes all solutions, not just valid ones.
  • Derive overflow bounds before choosing a fixed-width type.
