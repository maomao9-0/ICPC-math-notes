Capstone Synthesis Reserve & Multi-Perspective Problem Compendium

Cross-Chapter Architectural Bridges (Syn-01–Syn-14) and the 12 Revisited Dual-Perspective Flagship Problems

Look back across the twenty-four chapters of this textbook and a single unifying theme emerges: a competitive programming counting problem rarely belongs to a single rigid silo. A constraint on adjacent colors can be attacked through exponential generating functions for parity-constrained cycles, or through the Smirnov inclusion–exclusion sieve followed by rational coefficient extraction. A tournament survival count can be unrolled through poset Möbius inversion on binary subtrees, or lifted into a truncated Set Power Series over the subset lattice. Mastery of Formal Power Series and Generating Functions lies not merely in executing individual polynomial engines (inv, ln, exp, sqrt, pow, Bostan–Mori, CDQ-FFT, Lagrange inversion, or FWT), but in recognizing structural isomorphisms between seemingly distant paradigms and choosing the representation with the sharpest asymptotic and constant-factor profile for the given constraints.

This capstone module completes the 254-problem architecture of the textbook:

\[254 \text{ Canonical Pool Problems} = 192 \text{ Chapter Ladder Problems } (24 \times 8) + 48 \text{ Part Capstone Problems } (6 \times 8) + 14 \text{ Synthesis Reserve Problems}.\]

It serves two primary pedagogical functions:

  1. The 14 Capstone Synthesis Reserve Problems (Syn-01 through Syn-14): Every problem in this reserve is solved from first principles along two distinct mathematical perspectives bridging multiple chapters and parts of the textbook. Each writeup is completely self-contained with an inline problem statement, exact constraints, a hand-checkable sample trace (\(N \le 4\)), three progressive hints, full step-by-step derivations of both perspectives, an explicit asymptotic and constant-factor trade-off analysis, and a formal 5-part Algorithm Contract.
  2. The Master Index & Architectural Synthesis of the 12 Revisited Dual-Perspective Flagship Problems: Twelve canonical problems appear twice in the main 24-chapter ladders—first solved with an foundational engine in an earlier chapter, and later revisited with a higher-powered algebraic, operator, or bivariate framework. Section 3 synthesizes all twelve flagship pairs with direct cross-links and comparative diagnostic rules.

Syn-01 (#006) — Permutation (Pairs with No Common Ascent)

  • Source: AtCoder fps_24_k — Permutation
  • Bridged Chapters: Ch 08 (Cluster Inclusion–Exclusion) \(\times\) Ch 01 (Newton Polynomial Inversion) \(\times\) Ch 06 (Bivariate Eulerian Polynomials) \(\times\) Ch 04 (Block / Derangement EGFs)

Problem Statement

Given an integer \(N\), count the number of ordered pairs of permutations \((P, Q)\) of \((1, 2, \dots, N)\) such that there is no index \(1 \le i \le N - 1\) where both \(P_i < P_{i+1}\) and \(Q_i < Q_{i+1}\) hold simultaneously (i.e., \(P\) and \(Q\) share no common ascent position). Output the count modulo \(998244353\).

(Note: By symmetry of reversal \(Q_i \mapsto N + 1 - Q_i\), this is equivalent to counting pairs of permutations \((P, Q)\) such that at every position \(1 \le i \le N-1\), their relative order transitions satisfy \(\neg(P_i < P_{i+1} \land Q_i < Q_{i+1})\).)

Constraints

  • \(1 \le N \le 2.5 \times 10^5\)
  • Modulus \(p = 998244353\) (prime, \(p > N\))
  • Required complexity: \(O(N \log N)\) time and \(O(N)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

  • \(N = 1\): There is \(1! \times 1! = 1\) pair \(((1), (1))\), and the condition over \(1 \le i \le 0\) is vacuously true. Output: \(1\).
  • \(N = 2\): Out of \((2!)^2 = 4\) pairs of permutations of \(\{1, 2\}\), only \(P = (1, 2), Q = (1, 2)\) has a shared ascent at \(i = 1\). Removing this \(1\) forbidden pair leaves \(4 - 1 = 3\).
  • \(N = 3\): Total pairs \((3!)^2 = 36\). Let \(E_1\) be the event \(P_1 < P_2 \land Q_1 < Q_2\), and \(E_2\) be \(P_2 < P_3 \land Q_2 < Q_3\).
    • \(\lvert E_1 \rvert\): A contiguous increasing block of size \(2\) at \(\{1, 2\}\) in both \(P\) and \(Q\) leaves \(\binom{3}{2, 1}^2 = 9\) pairs. By symmetry \(\lvert E_2 \rvert = 9\).
    • \(\lvert E_1 \cap E_2 \rvert\): Both \(P\) and \(Q\) are strictly increasing on \(\{1, 2, 3\}\), so \(P = Q = (1, 2, 3)\), giving \(1\) pair.
    • By Inclusion–Exclusion, the number of valid pairs is \(36 - (9 + 9) + 1 = 19\).
  • \(N = 4\): Valid pairs \(= 211\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

The condition forbids a local adjacent event \(E_i = [P_i < P_{i+1} \land Q_i < Q_{i+1}]\) at each boundary \(i \in \{1, \dots, N-1\}\). Whenever a set of chosen boundary events \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}\) is forced to hold simultaneously, it partitions the index set \(\{1, \dots, N\}\) into contiguous blocks of lengths \(s_1 + s_2 + \dots + s_k = N\) on which both \(P\) and \(Q\) must be strictly increasing. Notice that once we choose which \(s_j\) values go into the \(j\)-th block of \(P\) and which \(s_j\) values go into the \(j\)-th block of \(Q\), their internal orderings are uniquely forced (\(1\) way each).

Hint 2 — Perspective 1 vs. Perspective 2 Setup
  • Perspective 1 (Cluster Inclusion–Exclusion + FPS Inversion): A contiguous block of length \(s \ge 1\) contains \(s - 1\) forced boundaries, contributing an inclusion–exclusion sign of \((-1)^{s-1}\). Since choosing an ordered sequence of blocks of lengths \(s_1, \dots, s_k\) for \(P\) and independently for \(Q\) contributes \(\frac{N!}{s_1! \cdots s_k!} \times \frac{N!}{s_1! \cdots s_k!}\), factor the double multinomial coefficient into per-block weights \(\frac{(-1)^{s-1}}{(s!)^2} x^s\) and apply the \(\operatorname{SEQ}\) construction.
  • Perspective 2 (Bivariate Eulerian Diagonal / Hadamard Product): Let \(A_N(u) = \sum_{\pi \in S_N} u^{\operatorname{asc}(\pi)}\) be the Eulerian polynomial, or track the exact subset of ascent positions \(A(P) \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}\). Requiring \(A(P) \cap A(Q) = \emptyset\) is equivalent to taking the diagonal over independent permutations via the Hadamard product of their local step matrices, which also connects directly to the Carlitz–Scoville–Vaughan alternating double-factorial EGF.
Hint 3 — Key Equations & Extraction Contract

Define the doubly-exponential block generator

\[B(x) = \sum_{s=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{s-1} \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2}.\]

Then the valid pair count for length \(N\) is given by

\[a_N = (N!)^2 [x^N] \frac{1}{1 - B(x)} = (N!)^2 [x^N] \left( \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} (-1)^s \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2} \right)^{-1}.\]

Since \([x^0] \sum_{s \ge 0} (-1)^s \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2} = 1 \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times\), a single polynomial inversion modulo \(x^{N+1}\) solves the problem in \(O(N \log N)\) time.

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Contiguous Cluster Inclusion–Exclusion & Newton Polynomial Inversion (Ch 08 \(\times\) Ch 01)

The Diagnostic Signal

We want to count pairs \((P, Q) \in S_N \times S_N\) avoiding the bad boundary set \(B(P, Q) = \{i \in \{1, \dots, N-1\} : P_i < P_{i+1} \text{ and } Q_i < Q_{i+1}\}\). Whenever bad events occur on the linear adjacency graph of indices \(1 - 2 - \cdots - N\), any subset of forced bad edges \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}\) decomposes the index line \(\{1, \dots, N\}\) into \(k = N - \lvert S \rvert\) connected components (contiguous intervals) of lengths \(s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k \ge 1\) with \(s_1 + \dots + s_k = N\) and \(\lvert S \rvert = \sum_{j=1}^k (s_j - 1)\).

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

For a fixed subset \(S\) with interval lengths \((s_1, \dots, s_k)\): 1. For permutation \(P\) to be strictly increasing on each interval of length \(s_j\), we only need to partition the label set \(\{1, \dots, N\}\) into ordered subsets of sizes \(s_1, \dots, s_k\); inside each interval, the elements must appear in increasing order. The number of such permutations \(P\) is the multinomial coefficient \(\binom{N}{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k} = \frac{N!}{s_1! s_2! \cdots s_k!}\). 2. Independently, the number of permutations \(Q\) strictly increasing on the exact same intervals is also \(\frac{N!}{s_1! s_2! \cdots s_k!}\). 3. Therefore, the number of pairs \((P, Q)\) satisfying all bad events in \(S\) is \(\left(\frac{N!}{s_1! \cdots s_k!}\right)^2\).

Applying Inclusion–Exclusion over all subsets \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}\):

\[\begin{aligned} a_N &= \sum_{S \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}} (-1)^{\lvert S \rvert} \lvert \{(P, Q) : S \subseteq B(P, Q)\} \rvert \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{N} \sum_{\substack{s_1 + \dots + s_k = N \\ s_j \ge 1}} (-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^k (s_j - 1)} \left( \frac{N!}{s_1! \cdots s_k!} \right)^2 \\ &= (N!)^2 \sum_{k=0}^{N} \sum_{\substack{s_1 + \dots + s_k = N \\ s_j \ge 1}} \prod_{j=1}^k \frac{(-1)^{s_j - 1}}{(s_j!)^2}. \end{aligned}\]

Recognizing the inner sum as the \(k\)-fold Cauchy convolution \([x^N] B(x)^k\) where \(B(x) = \sum_{s=1}^\infty (-1)^{s-1} \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2}\) has valuation \(\operatorname{val}(B) = 1 \ge 1\), the outer sum over \(k \ge 0\) is the formal geometric series \(\operatorname{SEQ}(B(x))\):

\[a_N = (N!)^2 [x^N] \frac{1}{1 - \sum_{s=1}^\infty (-1)^{s-1} \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2}} = (N!)^2 [x^N] \left( \sum_{s=0}^\infty (-1)^s \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2} \right)^{-1}.\]

Let \(D(x) = \sum_{s=0}^N \frac{(-1)^s}{(s!)^2} x^s \bmod x^{N+1}\). Since \([x^0] D(x) = 1 \neq 0\), \(D(x)\) is invertible in \(\mathbb{F}_p[[x]] / (x^{N+1})\). Evaluating at \(N = 3\): * \(D(x) = 1 - x + \frac{1}{4} x^2 - \frac{1}{36} x^3 \bmod x^4\). * \(D(x)^{-1} = 1 + (x - \frac{1}{4}x^2 + \frac{1}{36}x^3) + (x^2 - \frac{1}{2}x^3) + x^3 = 1 + x + \frac{3}{4} x^2 + \frac{19}{36} x^3 \bmod x^4\). * Multiplying \([x^N] D(x)^{-1}\) by \((N!)^2\) gives: \(a_1 = 1^2 \cdot 1 = 1\), \(a_2 = 4 \cdot \frac{3}{4} = 3\), \(a_3 = 36 \cdot \frac{19}{36} = 19\), matching our hand-checked trace!

Perspective 2: Bivariate Ascent Filtration & Hadamard Diagonal of Step Operators (Ch 06 \(\times\) Ch 04)

Instead of collapsing intervals first, we can view a pair \((P, Q)\) as a single permutation \(\sigma = P \circ Q^{-1}\) or track the step-by-step insertion of \((N, \dots, 1)\) via the Hadamard product of two ordinary generating functions on the Boolean hypercube \(\{0, 1\}^{N-1}\).

Specifically, for any single permutation \(P \in S_N\) and any prescribed subset of ascents \(U \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}\), define the indicator function \(f_P(U) = [U \subseteq \operatorname{Asc}(P)]\). Then the condition \(\operatorname{Asc}(P) \cap \operatorname{Asc}(Q) = \emptyset\) is expressed via the subset Möbius inversion identity \(\sum_{U \subseteq \operatorname{Asc}(P) \cap \operatorname{Asc}(Q)} (-1)^{\lvert U \rvert} = [\operatorname{Asc}(P) \cap \operatorname{Asc}(Q) = \emptyset]\):

\[a_N = \sum_{U \subseteq \{1, \dots, N-1\}} (-1)^{\lvert U \rvert} \left( \sum_{P \in S_N} [U \subseteq \operatorname{Asc}(P)] \right)^2.\]

This reveals why the squared factorials \((s!)^2\) appear in the denominator of \(D(x)\): for a single permutation \(P\), the EGF for avoiding a set of ascents has block weight \(\frac{(-1)^{s-1}}{s!} x^s\), whose sum is \(1 - e^{-x}\), giving \([x^N] \frac{1}{1 - (1 - e^{-x})} = [x^N] e^x = 1\) (since only the strictly decreasing permutation \((N, N-1, \dots, 1)\) has zero ascents!). When we require two independent permutations \((P, Q)\) to have disjoint ascent sets, we replace the single-permutation block weight \(\frac{x^s}{s!}\) by its termwise Hadamard product \(\frac{x^s}{s!} \odot \frac{x^s}{s!} = \frac{x^s}{(s!)^2}\), replacing the exponential series \(e^{-x} = \sum_{s \ge 0} \frac{(-1)^s}{s!} x^s\) by the Bessel-Clifford series \(J_0(2\sqrt{x}) = \sum_{s \ge 0} \frac{(-1)^s}{(s!)^2} x^s\).

(Bonus Structural Bridge): If the problem asks for \(m\) permutations \((P^{(1)}, \dots, P^{(m)})\) sharing no common \(m\)-way ascent (\(P^{(1)}_i < P^{(1)}_{i+1} \land \dots \land P^{(m)}_i < P^{(m)}_{i+1}\)), the exact same Hadamard replacement immediately yields

\[a_N^{(m)} = (N!)^m [x^N] \left( \sum_{s=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^s}{(s!)^m} x^s \right)^{-1}.\]

WarningFailure Mode & Remedy: Forgetting the Outer \((N!)^2\) Normalization in Double EGFs

A common mistake when seeing two independent permutations is to multiply by a single \(N!\) (treating \(x^s / (s!)^2\) as an ordinary EGF) or to forget that \(P\) and \(Q\) partition their label sets \(\{1, \dots, N\}\) independently. Always write out the explicit multinomial factor \(\binom{N}{s_1, \dots, s_k}^2 = \frac{(N!)^2}{\prod_{j=1}^k (s_j!)^2}\) before factoring into per-block weights; this guarantees both the internal \((s!)^{-2}\) denominator and the final \((N!)^2\) multiplier are matched.

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Smirnov Run-Length Inclusion–Exclusion, Excursion OGF & Bostan–Mori (Ch 08 \(\times\) Ch 05)

The Diagnostic Signal

In Formulation A, we must count words \(c_1 c_2 \dots c_T\) of length \(T\) such that \(f(c_1) = 1\), \(f(c_t) \neq f(c_{t+1})\) for \(1 \le t \le T-1\), and \(f(c_T) \neq 1\). Notice that \(f(c_1) = 1\) fixes the boundary color! Consequently, the circular word decomposes uniquely at every occurrence of color family \(1\):

\[(c_1 \in \text{Fam}_1) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Block}_1 \;\longrightarrow\; (c \in \text{Fam}_1) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Block}_2 \;\longrightarrow\; \cdots \;\longrightarrow\; (c \in \text{Fam}_1) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Block}_k,\]

where \(k \ge 1\) and each \(\text{Block}_j\) is a non-empty Smirnov word (no two adjacent elements from the same family) drawn strictly from the remaining \(N-1\) families \(\{2, 3, \dots, N\}\). Because \(\text{Block}_k\) is non-empty and contains only families in \(\{2, \dots, N\}\), its last element automatically satisfies \(f(c_T) \in \{2, \dots, N\} \implies f(c_T) \neq 1\)!

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad
  1. Smirnov OGF on \(\{2, \dots, N\}\): By the Smirnov inclusion–exclusion sieve (Chapter 08), a compressed run of \(r \ge 1\) elements of family \(i \in \{2, \dots, N\}\) has weight \((-1)^{r-1} (A_i x)^r\). Summing over \(r \ge 1\) gives the per-family cluster series

\[g_i(x) = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{r-1} (A_i x)^r = \frac{A_i x}{1 + A_i x}.\]

Let \(S(x) = \sum_{i=2}^N g_i(x) = \sum_{i=2}^N \frac{A_i x}{1 + A_i x}\). Since \(\operatorname{val}(S) = 1\), a non-empty valid word on \(\{2, \dots, N\}\) is generated by the geometric series of non-empty runs:

\[W(x) = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} S(x)^m = \frac{S(x)}{1 - S(x)}.\]

  1. Excursions from Family \(1\): The initial element \(c_1 \in \text{Fam}_1\) has weight \(A_1 x\). It is followed by \(\text{Block}_1\) (generated by \(W(x)\)), and then \(k - 1 \ge 0\) additional pairs of \((\text{single element from Fam}_1, \text{Block}_j)\), each generated by \(A_1 x \cdot W(x)\). Thus the total OGF for valid circular words of length \(T\) is:

\[\begin{aligned} F(x) &= A_1 x \cdot W(x) \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \bigl(A_1 x W(x)\bigr)^j = \frac{A_1 x W(x)}{1 - A_1 x W(x)} \\ &= \frac{A_1 x \frac{S(x)}{1 - S(x)}}{1 - A_1 x \frac{S(x)}{1 - S(x)}} = \frac{A_1 x S(x)}{1 - (1 + A_1 x) S(x)}. \end{aligned}\]

  1. Divide-and-Conquer Rational Aggregation + Bostan–Mori: Write \(S(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) where \(Q(x) = \prod_{i=2}^N (1 + A_i x)\) has degree \(N-1\) and \(P(x)\) has degree \(N-1\), computed via a binary merge tree of NTT multiplications in \(O(N \log^2 N)\) time. Substituting \(S(x) = P(x) / Q(x)\) gives the rational generating function of degree \(\le N\):

\[ans_T = [x^T] F(x) = A_1 [x^{T-1}] \frac{P(x)}{Q(x) - (1 + A_1 x) P(x)}.\]

Verifying on our sample trace \(N = 3, A = (1, 2, 1), T = 3\): * \(S(x) = \frac{2x}{1 + 2x} + \frac{x}{1 + x} = \frac{2x(1+x) + x(1+2x)}{(1+2x)(1+x)} = \frac{3x + 4x^2}{1 + 3x + 2x^2}\). * \(Q(x) - (1 + x) P(x) = (1 + 3x + 2x^2) - (1 + x)(3x + 4x^2) = 1 - 5x^2 - 4x^3\). * \(ans_3 = 1 \cdot [x^2] \frac{3x + 4x^2}{1 - 5x^2 - 4x^3} = [x^2] (3x + 4x^2)(1 + 5x^2 + \cdots) = 4\). Exact match! * When \(T \le 10^{18}\), Bostan–Mori extracts \([x^{T-1}]\) in \(O(N \log N \log T)\) time.

Perspective 2: Cycle-Parity Hyperbolic / Logarithmic EGF (Ch 04 \(\times\) Ch 02)

When the colors are homogeneous across cycles of a permutation (Formulation B, or when decomposing a permutation into properly colored directed cycles), we can bypass rational merge trees altogether using the exponential formula \(\mathcal{F}(x) = \exp(\mathcal{C}(x))\).

For a directed cycle of length \(L \ge 1\) with \(C\) colors, the number of proper colorings where no adjacent vertices share a color is: * For \(L = 1\): a 1-cycle (fixed point) has no adjacency constraint (or if self-loops are allowed, \(C\) choices; if self-loops are forbidden, \(0\) choices). Under the standard vertex-coloring model where 1-cycles have \(C\) choices and \(L\)-cycles (\(L \ge 2\)) have chromatic polynomial \(w_L = (C-1)^L + (-1)^L (C-1)\): * Notice that for \(L = 1\), the formula \((C-1)^1 + (-1)^1 (C-1) = 0\), whereas \(w_1 = C\). Thus the connected cycle EGF is:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathcal{C}(x) &= C x + \sum_{L=2}^{\infty} \frac{(C-1)^L + (-1)^L (C-1)}{L} x^L \\ &= C x + \sum_{L=1}^{\infty} \frac{((C-1)x)^L}{L} + (C-1) \sum_{L=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-x)^L}{L} \\ &= C x + \ln \frac{1}{1 - (C-1)x} + (C-1) \ln \frac{1}{1 + x}. \end{aligned}\]

Exponentiating \(\mathcal{C}(x)\) via \(\operatorname{SET}(\mathcal{C}) = \exp(\mathcal{C}(x))\) cancels all logarithms into a pure rational-exponential closed form:

\[\mathcal{F}(x) = \exp(\mathcal{C}(x)) = \frac{e^{C x}}{(1 - (C-1)x)(1 + x)^{C-1}}.\]

(Note: For \(N = 2, C = 3\), \(\mathcal{F}(x) = \frac{e^{3x}}{(1 - 2x)(1 + x)^2} = (1 + 3x + \frac{9}{2}x^2)(1 + 2x + 4x^2)(1 - 2x + 3x^2) \equiv 1 + 3x + \frac{15}{2} x^2 \pmod{x^3}\), so \(2! [x^2] \mathcal{F}(x) = 15\), matching our sample trace!)

Syn-03 (#051) — Dark Horse (Tournament Tree Poset IE vs. Set Power Series)

  • Source: AtCoder ARC 093 F — Dark Horse
  • Bridged Chapters: Ch 08 (Poset Inclusion–Exclusion) \(\times\) Ch 06 (Falling-Factorial Block Allocation) \(\times\) Ch 24 (Set Power Series & Submask SOS Convolution)

Problem Statement

There are \(2^N\) players labeled \(1, 2, \dots, 2^N\) competing in a standard single-elimination binary tournament tree of height \(N\). In any match between player \(x\) and player \(y\): * Usually, the player with the smaller label wins (\(\min(x, y)\) beats \(\max(x, y)\)). * However, player \(1\) is the “Dark Horse”: there is a given set of \(M\) specific players \(A = \{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_M\}\) (\(2 \le A_1 < A_2 < \dots < A_M \le 2^N\)) who beat player \(1\) in a head-to-head match, while player \(1\) beats all other players in \(\{2, \dots, 2^N\} \setminus A\). (Matches not involving player \(1\) are still won by the player with the smaller label.)

All \((2^N)!\) initial bracket assignments (permutations of the \(2^N\) leaves) are equally likely. Count the number of bracket permutations in which player \(1\) wins the entire tournament, modulo \(10^9 + 7\).

Constraints

  • \(1 \le N, M \le 16\)
  • \(2 \le A_1 < A_2 < \dots < A_M \le 2^N\)
  • Modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\)
  • Required complexity: \(O(M N 2^N)\) time and \(O(2^N)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

  • \(N = 2\) (\(2^2 = 4\) players), \(M = 1\), \(A = \{3\}\):
    • Player \(1\) beats \(2\) and \(4\), but loses to \(3\). However, player \(2\) beats \(3\) and \(4\)!
    • Once player \(1\)’s leaf position is fixed (4 choices), the remaining leaves are partitioned relative to player \(1\) into a round-1 opponent subtree of size \(2^0 = 1\) and a round-2 opponent subtree of size \(2^1 = 2\).
    • Player \(1\) wins iff the winner (minimum label) of the size-\(1\) subtree is not \(3\), and the winner (minimum label) of the size-\(2\) subtree is not \(3\).
    • Bad event \(E_0\): the size-\(1\) subtree has minimum \(3\) (so its single leaf is \(3\); \(1\) way to fill size-\(1\) subtree, \(2! = 2\) ways to fill remaining \(2\) leaves \(\implies 2\) ways).
    • Bad event \(E_1\): the size-\(2\) subtree has minimum \(3\) (so its 2 leaves must be \(\{3, 4\}\) in \(2! = 2\) orders, and the remaining 1 leaf is \(2\) \(\implies 2\) ways).
    • Since \(\{3\}\) cannot be in both subtrees simultaneously (\(E_0 \cap E_1 = \emptyset\)), out of \(3! = 6\) ways to place \(\{2, 3, 4\}\), valid assignments per position of player \(1\) are \(6 - 2 - 2 = 2\).
    • Multiplying by \(2^N = 4\) leaf positions for player \(1\) gives \(4 \times 2 = 8\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

By symmetry of the binary tournament tree, fix player \(1\) at leaf \(0\) (and multiply the final answer by \(2^N\) at the end). Then the remaining \(2^N - 1\) leaves decompose into \(N\) disjoint opponent subtrees \(T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{N-1}\) of sizes \(2^0, 2^1, \dots, 2^{N-1}\), corresponding to the \(N\) rounds player \(1\) must survive. Because matches not involving player \(1\) are always won by the minimum label, the champion of opponent subtree \(T_k\) is simply \(\min(T_k)\)! Thus player \(1\) wins the tournament iff \(\min(T_k) \notin A\) for all \(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\).

Hint 2 — Perspective 1 vs. Perspective 2 Setup
  • Perspective 1 (Subtree Poset Inclusion–Exclusion + Descending Allocation DP): Apply Inclusion–Exclusion over the subset of subtrees \(S \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\) whose minimum falls in \(A\). If \(k \in S\) has \(\min(T_k) = A_i\), then all other \(2^k - 1\) players in \(T_k\) must be strictly larger than \(A_i\). If we process the bad players in descending order \(A_M > A_{M-1} > \dots > A_1\), then at step \(i\), we know exactly how many players larger than \(A_i\) are available and how many have already been committed to subtrees in \(S\)!
  • Perspective 2 (Set Power Series / Submask SOS Exp): A collection of bad subtrees \(S\) with total size \(\operatorname{mask}(S) = \sum_{k \in S} 2^k\) is an exponential set assembly over the Boolean lattice \(2^{\{0,\dots,N-1\}}\), where the order of choosing \(A_{i_k} = \min(T_k)\) corresponds to a chain in the subset lattice.
Hint 3 — Key Equations & Extraction Contract

Sort \(A\) in descending order: \(B_1 > B_2 > \dots > B_M\). Let \(dp[i][\text{mask}]\) be the number of ways to assign the minimums and internal leaves of the subtrees in \(\text{mask} \subseteq \{0, \dots, N-1\}\) using a subset of \(\{B_1, \dots, B_i\}\) as their subtree minimums. Because \(\sum_{k \in \text{mask}} 2^k = \text{mask}\) as an integer in binary: * Skip \(B_i\): \(dp[i][\text{mask}] \gets dp[i-1][\text{mask}]\). * Assign \(B_i = \min(T_k)\) for some \(k \notin \text{mask}\): There are \((2^N - B_i) - \text{mask}\) players strictly larger than \(B_i\) that have not yet been placed into \(\text{mask}\). We choose \(2^k - 1\) of them and permute all \(2^k\) leaves of \(T_k\) in \(\binom{2^N - B_i - \text{mask}}{2^k - 1} (2^k)!\) ways.

Finally, sum \((-1)^{\operatorname{popcount}(\text{mask})} dp[M][\text{mask}] (2^N - 1 - \text{mask})!\) and multiply by \(2^N\).

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Tournament Subtree Inclusion–Exclusion & Descending Poset Allocation (Ch 08 \(\times\) Ch 06)

The Diagnostic Signal

Fixing player \(1\)’s leaf position (\(2^N\) choices by tree isomorphism), the remaining \(2^N - 1\) leaves partition into \(N\) disjoint subtrees \(T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{N-1}\) with \(\lvert T_k \rvert = 2^k\). Inside \(T_k\), player \(1\) does not participate, so the winner of \(T_k\) is \(\min(T_k)\). Player \(1\) wins the tournament if and only if none of the \(N\) events \(E_k = [\min(T_k) \in A]\) occur.

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

When we force a subset of bad events \(S \subseteq \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}\) to hold, each \(k \in S\) must have its minimum \(\min(T_k)\) equal to some distinct element \(a_k \in A\), and the remaining \(2^k - 1\) elements of \(T_k\) must be chosen from \(\{a_k + 1, \dots, 2^N\}\).

Why do the choices for different \(k \in S\) interact? Because if \(a_{k_1} < a_{k_2}\), the pool \(\{a_{k_1} + 1, \dots, 2^N\}\) contains the pool \(\{a_{k_2} + 1, \dots, 2^N\}\)! This nested-interval structure is the classic diagnostic signal to process the minimums in descending order \(a_{k_{\text{max}}} > \dots > a_{k_{\text{min}}}\): * When we process a candidate minimum \(B \in A\) after all chosen minimums \(> B\) have already fully populated their subtrees (using a total of \(\sum_{r \in S_{> B}} 2^r = \text{mask}_{> B}\) elements from \(\{B + 1, \dots, 2^N\}\)), every single one of those \(\text{mask}_{> B}\) elements is guaranteed to be \(> B\)! * Therefore, out of the \(2^N - B\) elements in \(\{B + 1, \dots, 2^N\}\), exactly \((2^N - B) - \text{mask}_{> B}\) elements remain available to populate the \(2^k - 1\) non-minimum slots of \(T_k\).

Notice the remarkable binary miracle of tournament trees: because \(\lvert T_k \rvert = 2^k\), the total number of elements already allocated to a subset of subtrees \(\text{mask} \subseteq \{0, \dots, N-1\}\) is literally the integer value \(\text{mask} = \sum_{k \in \text{mask}} 2^k\)!

Let \(B_1 > B_2 > \dots > B_M\) be the elements of \(A\) sorted in descending order. Define \(dp[i][\text{mask}]\) for \(0 \le i \le M\) and \(0 \le \text{mask} < 2^N\) as the number of ways to select and permute all leaves inside the subtrees of \(\text{mask}\) such that their subtree minimums are drawn from \(\{B_1, \dots, B_i\}\). * Base case: \(dp[0][0] = 1\), and \(dp[0][\text{mask}] = 0\) for \(\text{mask} > 0\). * Transition for \(i = 1, \dots, M\) and each \(\text{mask} \in [0, 2^N)\): 1. \(B_i\) is not used as the minimum of any subtree in \(S\): add \(dp[i-1][\text{mask}]\) to \(dp[i][\text{mask}]\). 2. \(B_i\) is used as the minimum of subtree \(T_k\) (\(k \notin \text{mask}\)): the number of ways to choose \(2^k - 1\) elements from the \((2^N - B_i - \text{mask})\) available elements \(> B_i\) and arrange all \(2^k\) leaves of \(T_k\) (since any of the \(2^k\) leaves can hold \(B_i\) and the remaining \(2^k - 1\) leaves can be permuted in \((2^k - 1)!\) ways, giving \(2^k \cdot (2^k - 1)! = (2^k)!\)) is:

\[W(B_i, \text{mask}, k) = \binom{2^N - B_i - \text{mask}}{2^k - 1} (2^k)!.\]

 So we add $dp[i-1][\text{mask}] \cdot W(B_i, \text{mask}, k)$ to $dp[i][\text{mask} \mid 2^k]$.

After processing all \(M\) elements of \(A\), for each \(\text{mask} \in [0, 2^N)\), the remaining \((2^N - 1 - \text{mask})\) leaves outside \(S\) can be assigned the remaining \((2^N - 1 - \text{mask})\) players in \((2^N - 1 - \text{mask})!\) ways. By Inclusion–Exclusion:

\[\text{Ans} = 2^N \sum_{\text{mask}=0}^{2^N - 1} (-1)^{\operatorname{popcount}(\text{mask})} dp[M][\text{mask}] \cdot (2^N - 1 - \text{mask})!.\]

Perspective 2: Set Power Series & Truncated Subset-Chain Convolution over \(\mathcal{R}_N\) (Ch 24)

Problem Statement

\[a_1 < (a_1 \mathbin{\mathrm{OR}} a_2) < (a_1 \mathbin{\mathrm{OR}} a_2 \mathbin{\mathrm{OR}} a_3) < \dots < (a_1 \mathbin{\mathrm{OR}} a_2 \mathbin{\mathrm{OR}} \dots \mathbin{\mathrm{OR}} a_n).\]

  • \(1 \le k \le 3 \times 10^4\)
  • Modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\) (non-NTT prime)

  • \(n = 4, k = 3\): Each step must turn on at least one of the \(k = 3\) bits that was previously \(0\). By the Pigeonhole Principle, since there are only \(3\) bits and \(4\) steps, it is impossible! Output: \(0\).

    • Step 1 turns on \(c_1 \ge 1\) bits out of \(2\); Step 2 turns on \(c_2 \ge 1\) new bits out of the remaining \(2 - c_1\), while any subset of the \(c_1\) already-active bits can be freely set to \(0\) or \(1\) in \(a_2\) (\(2^{c_1}\) choices).
    • Here \(c_1 = 1, c_2 = 1\) is the only valid composition: \(\binom{2}{1} \binom{1}{1} \cdot 2^{c_1} = 2 \times 1 \times 2^1 = 4\).
    • Explicitly, the 4 valid pairs \((a_1, a_2)\) are \((1, 2), (1, 3), (2, 1), (2, 3)\).
  • \(n = 2, k = 3\):
    • \((c_1, c_2) = (1, 1)\): \(\binom{3}{1}\binom{2}{1} 2^1 = 12\).
    • \((c_1, c_2) = (1, 2)\): \(\binom{3}{1}\binom{2}{2} 2^1 = 6\).
    • \((c_1, c_2) = (2, 1)\): \(\binom{3}{2}\binom{1}{1} 2^2 = 12\).
    • Total: \(12 + 6 + 12 = 30\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

At each step \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), let \(c_i \ge 1\) be the number of new bit positions turned on for the first time by \(a_i\). Since there are only \(k\) bits in total and each \(c_i \ge 1\), if \(n > k\), the answer is immediately \(0\)! Any bit activated in steps \(1, \dots, i-1\) can be freely set to \(0\) or \(1\) in steps \(i, i+1, \dots, n\)!

Hint 2 — Perspective 1 vs. Perspective 2 Setup

where \(F_n(x) \bmod x^{k+1}\) is computed in \(\le \lfloor \log_2 k \rfloor + 1\) doubling steps via

\[[x^m] F_{a+b}(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{m} \left( 2^{b i} [x^i] F_a(x) \right) \left( [x^{m-i}] F_b(x) \right).\]

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Scale-Twisted Binomial EGF Doubling (Ch 04)

The Diagnostic Signal

For \(n > k\), the Pigeonhole Principle forces at least one step to activate \(0\) new bits, so \(\text{Ans} = 0\). Henceforth assume \(1 \le n \le k \le 3 \times 10^4\).

Each bit position \(b \in \{0, 1, \dots, k-1\}\) is either: * Never activated across all \(n\) steps, or * Activated for the first time at some step \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\), and then freely chosen in \(\{0, 1\}\) during the remaining \(n - i\) steps (\(2^{n-i}\) ways).

However, we also have the global constraint that every step \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) must activate at least one new bit (\(c_i \ge 1\)). In the Labeled EGF dictionary (Chapter 04), choosing a non-empty labeled subset of bits for step \(i\), where each bit carries weight \(2^{n-i}\), has the exponential generating function

\[G_i(x) = \operatorname{SET}_{\ge 1}(2^{n-i} x) = \sum_{c_i = 1}^{\infty} \frac{(2^{n-i} x)^{c_i}}{c_i!} = e^{2^{n-i} x} - 1.\]

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Multiplying the EGFs of all \(n\) active steps \(i = 1, \dots, n\) (and letting \(j = n - i \in \{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}\)) gives the EGF for the union of all \(m = c_1 + \dots + c_n\) activated bits:

\[F_n(x) = \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} \left(e^{2^j x} - 1\right) = \sum_{m=n}^{\infty} f_{n, m} \frac{x^m}{m!}.\]

Choosing which \(m\) of the \(k\) bits are activated (or multiplying \(F_n(x)\) by \(e^x\) for the \(k - m\) inactive bits) immediately yields:

\[\text{Ans} = k! [x^k] \left( e^x F_n(x) \right) = \sum_{m=n}^k \binom{k}{m} f_{n, m}.\]

How do we compute \(F_n(x) \bmod x^{k+1}\) quickly? Splitting the product of length \(a + b\) into its first \(b\) terms (\(0 \le j < b\)) and remaining \(a\) terms (\(b \le j < a + b\)) shifts the scale of \(x\) by \(2^b\):

\[F_{a+b}(x) = \left( \prod_{j=0}^{b-1} (e^{2^j x} - 1) \right) \left( \prod_{r=0}^{a-1} (e^{2^r (2^b x)} - 1) \right) = F_b(x) \cdot F_a(2^b x).\]

On coefficients, scaling \(x \mapsto 2^b x\) is a simple \(O(k)\) pointwise multiplication \([x^m] F_a(2^b x) = 2^{b m} [x^m] F_a(x)\), followed by one polynomial multiplication modulo \(x^{k+1}\). Because \(n \le k\), binary exponentiation requires \(\le \lfloor \log_2 k \rfloor + 1 \le 15\) doublings, running in \(O(k \log k \log n)\) time.

Problem Statement

You have an \(n \times n\) grid and an integer \(K\). Each cell \((r, c)\) (\(1 \le r, c \le n\)) must be filled with an integer from \(\{1, 2, \dots, K\}\) such that: * The minimum value in every row is \(1\), and * The minimum value in every column is \(1\).

Count the number of valid \(n \times n\) grids modulo \(10^9 + 7\). (We analyze both the contest bound \(n \le 250\) and the strengthened FPS bound \(n \le 2 \times 10^5\).)

Constraints

  • \(1 \le n \le 250\) (strengthened version: \(1 \le n \le 2 \times 10^5\))
  • \(1 \le K \le 10^9\)
  • Modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\)
  • Required complexity: \(O(n \log n)\) time and \(O(n)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

  • \(n = 1, K = 5\): The single cell \((1, 1)\) must be \(1\). Output: \(1\).
  • \(n = 2, K = 2\): Each cell is in \(\{1, 2\}\). A row or column has minimum \(1\) iff it is not all \(2\)s.
    • Total \(2 \times 2\) grids: \(2^4 = 16\).
    • Using \(i\) bad rows (all \(2\)s) and \(j\) bad columns (all \(2\)s): the \(i + j\) rows/columns cover \(4 - (2-i)(2-j)\) cells forced to be \(2\), leaving \((2-i)(2-j)\) free cells with \(2\) choices each:
      • \((i, j) = (0, 0)\): \(\binom{2}{0}\binom{2}{0} 2^4 = 16\).
      • \((i, j) \in \{(1, 0), (0, 1)\}\): \(-2 \times \binom{2}{1}\binom{2}{0} 2^2 = -16\).
      • \((i, j) \in \{(2, 0), (0, 2)\}\): \(+2 \times \binom{2}{2}\binom{2}{0} 2^0 = +2\).
      • \((i, j) = (1, 1)\): \(+\binom{2}{1}\binom{2}{1} 2^1 = +8\).
      • \((i, j) \in \{(2, 1), (1, 2)\}\): \(-2 \times \binom{2}{2}\binom{2}{1} 2^0 = -4\).
      • \((i, j) = (2, 2)\): \(+\binom{2}{2}\binom{2}{2} 2^0 = +1\).
    • Total: \(16 - 16 + 2 + 8 - 4 + 1 = 7\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

A row (or column) has minimum \(1\) iff it does not have all entries in \(\{2, 3, \dots, K\}\). Thus a “bad” row (or column) is one whose entries all belong to the \((K-1)\)-element set \(\{2, \dots, K\}\). If we fix \(i\) bad rows and \(j\) bad columns, how many cells are forced into \(\{2, \dots, K\}\), and how many cells remain completely unconstrained in \(\{1, \dots, K\}\)?

Hint 2 — Perspective 1 vs. Perspective 2 Setup
  • Perspective 1 (Row-Only IE / Binomial Collapse of 2D IE): In the 2D Inclusion–Exclusion sum over \(0 \le i, j \le n\), the exponent \((n-i)(n-j)\) is linear in \(j\) for each fixed \(i\)! Summing over \(j\) first using the Binomial Theorem \((a + b)^n = \sum_{j=0}^n \binom{n}{j} a^{n-j} b^j\) collapses the entire \(O(n^2)\) double sum into a single \(O(n \log n)\) 1D sum (which also corresponds directly to doing IE on rows only while requiring every column to contain at least one \(1\) outside the \(i\) bad rows!).
  • Perspective 2 (Chirp-\(Z\) / Bluestein Identity on the Bilinear Exponent \((n-i)(n-j)\)): What if a grid problem has row/column weights \(A_i, B_j\) that are not binomial coefficients, so the inner sum over \(j\) cannot be collapsed by \((a+b)^n\)? The bilinear exponent \(q^{u v}\) with \(u = n - i\), \(v = n - j\) and \(q = K / (K-1)\) is rewritten via Bluestein’s identity \(u v = \binom{u+v}{2} - \binom{u}{2} - \binom{v}{2}\), turning the 2D sum into a single 1D convolution!

Starting from the 2D Inclusion–Exclusion formula:

\[\text{Ans} = \sum_{i=0}^n \sum_{j=0}^n (-1)^{i+j} \binom{n}{i} \binom{n}{j} K^{(n-i)(n-j)} (K-1)^{n^2 - (n-i)(n-j)}.\]

  • Perspective 1 Collapse: Factor \((K-1)^{n^2 - (n-i)(n-j)} = (K-1)^{n i} \cdot (K-1)^{(n-i) j}\) and sum over \(j\):

\[\text{Ans} = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \binom{n}{i} \Bigl( (K-1)^i \bigl( K^{n-i} - (K-1)^{n-i} \bigr) \Bigr)^n.\]

  • Perspective 2 Bluestein Convolution: For \(K > 1\), let \(q = K (K-1)^{-1} \bmod p\) and substitute \(u = n - i, v = n - j\) with \(q^{uv} = q^{\binom{u+v}{2}} q^{-\binom{u}{2}} q^{-\binom{v}{2}}\).
Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Bipartite Row/Column IE & Binomial Theorem Sum Collapse (Ch 08 \(\times\) Ch 06)

The Diagnostic Signal

Let \(R_r\) be the bad event that row \(r\) contains no \(1\) (all its entries are in \(\{2, \dots, K\}\)), and \(C_c\) be the bad event that column \(c\) contains no \(1\). If we choose \(i\) bad rows and \(j\) bad columns: * The \((n-i)(n-j)\) cells in the intersection of the \((n-i)\) unchosen rows and \((n-j)\) unchosen columns are completely free (\(K\) choices per cell). * The remaining \(n^2 - (n-i)(n-j)\) cells lie in at least one chosen bad row or bad column, so each has \((K-1)\) choices.

By 2D Inclusion–Exclusion over all subsets of bad rows and bad columns:

\[\text{Ans} = \sum_{i=0}^n \sum_{j=0}^n (-1)^{i+j} \binom{n}{i} \binom{n}{j} K^{(n-i)(n-j)} (K-1)^{n^2 - (n-i)(n-j)}.\]

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Why evaluate an \(O(n^2)\) double sum when \(j\) appears in the exponent only linearly as \((n-i)(n-j)\)? Rewrite the exponent of \((K-1)\) as

\[n^2 - (n-i)(n-j) = n i + (n - i) j.\]

Now isolate everything depending on \(j\) inside the inner sum:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Ans} &= \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \binom{n}{i} (K-1)^{n i} \sum_{j=0}^n \binom{n}{j} \left( K^{n-i} \right)^{n-j} \left( -(K-1)^{n-i} \right)^j \\ &= \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \binom{n}{i} (K-1)^{n i} \left( K^{n-i} - (K-1)^{n-i} \right)^n \\ &= \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \binom{n}{i} \Bigl( (K-1)^i \bigl( K^{n-i} - (K-1)^{n-i} \bigr) \Bigr)^n. \end{aligned}\]

Notice the direct combinatorial meaning of this collapsed formula (1D Row-Only Inclusion–Exclusion): * Fix \(i\) rows to be bad (containing no \(1\)s). * Then for each of the \(n\) columns independently: the \(i\) entries in the bad rows each have \((K-1)\) choices, contributing \((K-1)^i\); the remaining \(n - i\) entries in the non-bad rows must contain at least one \(1\) so that the column has minimum \(1\), which happens in \(K^{n-i} - (K-1)^{n-i}\) ways! * Since the \(n\) columns are now completely independent given the \(i\) bad rows, raising the single-column count \((K-1)^i (K^{n-i} - (K-1)^{n-i})\) to the \(n\)-th power immediately gives the formula!

Evaluating the \(n+1\) terms takes \(O(n \log n)\) time (or \(O(n)\) using a linear sieve for \(m^n\)).

Perspective 2: Bluestein / Chirp-\(Z\) Transform on Bilinear Grid Sums (Ch 16 \(\times\) Ch 07)

What if the row and column weights are arbitrary arrays \(\alpha_i, \beta_j\) (for instance, when rows and columns have distinct target minimums or capacity constraints), so the Binomial Theorem no longer collapses the inner sum?

Assume \(K > 1\) (if \(K = 1\), every entry is \(1\) and the answer is \(1\)). Let \(u = n - i\), \(v = n - j\), and \(q = K (K - 1)^{-1} \bmod p\). Then the 2D sum has the canonical Chirp-\(Z\) bilinear form:

\[\text{Ans} = (K - 1)^{n^2} \sum_{u=0}^n \sum_{v=0}^n \underbrace{\left((-1)^{n-u} \binom{n}{u}\right)}_{A_u} \underbrace{\left((-1)^{n-v} \binom{n}{v}\right)}_{B_v} q^{u v}.\]

Applying Bluestein’s identity (Chapter 07)

\[u v = \binom{u + v}{2} - \binom{u}{2} - \binom{v}{2}\]

factors the coupled exponent \(q^{u v}\) into three univariate factors:

\[\text{Ans} = (K - 1)^{n^2} \sum_{s=0}^{2n} q^{\binom{s}{2}} \sum_{u + v = s} \left( A_u q^{-\binom{u}{2}} \right) \left( B_v q^{-\binom{v}{2}} \right),\]

which is a single polynomial convolution of length \(2n + 1\), solvable in \(O(n \log n)\) time for arbitrary sequences \(A_u, B_v\)!

Problem Statement

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

For every cell of the \(n \times n\) board to be attacked, either every row contains at least one rook or every column contains at least one rook (otherwise an empty row and an empty column intersect at an unattacked cell!). The Diagnostic Signal

For \(k \ge 1\), the two cases (“one rook per row” vs. “one rook per column”) are disjoint and symmetric of equal size, contributing a factor of \(2\).

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Assume each row \(1, \dots, n\) has exactly \(1\) rook, placed in column \(f(i) \in \{1, \dots, n\}\). * No two rooks share a row, so all attacking pairs are vertical. * In a column \(j\) that receives \(r_j \ge 1\) rooks, only adjacent rooks in that column attack each other (since rooks block lines of sight), creating exactly \(r_j - 1\) attacking pairs. * Summing over all \(c\) non-empty columns gives

\[k = \sum_{j=1}^c (r_j - 1) = \sum_{j=1}^c r_j - c = n - c \implies c = n - k.\]

\[\text{Ans} = 2^{[k > 0]} \binom{n}{n-k} (n-k)! \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ n - k \end{Bmatrix}.\]

\[\lvert \operatorname{Surj}(n, c) \rvert = \sum_{j=0}^{c} (-1)^{c - j} \binom{c}{j} j^n.\]

\[\text{Ans} = 2^{[k > 0]} \binom{n}{n-k} \sum_{j=0}^{n-k} (-1)^{n-k-j} \binom{n-k}{j} j^n,\]

Perspective 1: Aho–Corasick Automaton Transfer Matrix & Digit DP (Ch 05)

The Diagnostic Signal

When \(\sum_{i=1}^m \lvert P_i \rvert \le 1500\) and \(\lvert N \rvert \le 1200\), the total number of prefixes across all patterns \(P_1, \dots, P_m\) is at most \(S \le 1501\). The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad
  1. Automaton Construction & Bad-State Propagation:
    • Insert each \(P_i\) into a trie rooted at \(0\) and mark its terminal node bad[u] = true.
    • Run BFS to compute failure links fail[u] and deterministic transitions tr[u][d].
    • Crucially, a state \(u\) is forbidden if any of its suffixes is a forbidden pattern, so during BFS we propagate:

\[\text{bad}[u] \gets \text{bad}[u] \lor \text{bad}[\text{fail}[u]].\]

  1. Precomputing Unconstrained Suffix Counts (Removing Redundant State Flags): Instead of carrying four booleans in the DP, precompute \(dp[\ell][u]\) = number of valid digit strings of length \(\ell\) (\(0 \le \ell \le \lvert N \rvert\)) starting from safe state \(u\):

\[dp[\ell][u] = \sum_{\substack{d=0 \\ \neg\text{bad}[\text{tr}[u][d]]}}^{9} dp[\ell - 1][\text{tr}[u][d]].\]

  1. Counting Valid Integers in \([1, N]\):
    • Shorter lengths \(1 \le \ell < \lvert N \rvert\): choose the first digit \(d \in \{1, \dots, 9\}\) (so no leading zeros!) and complete with \(\ell - 1\) digits:

\[\sum_{\ell = 1}^{\lvert N \rvert - 1} \sum_{\substack{d = 1 \\ \neg\text{bad}[\text{tr}[0][d]]}}^{9} dp[\ell - 1][\text{tr}[0][d]].\]

  • Same length \(\lvert N \rvert\): walk the digits \(N_1 N_2 \dots N_{\lvert N \rvert}\) along the automaton state \(u\) (starting at \(u = 0\)). At position \(i \in \{1, \dots, \lvert N \rvert\}\), for each digit \(d \in \{[i=1], \dots, N_i - 1\}\) with \(\neg\text{bad}[\text{tr}[u][d]]\), add \(dp[\lvert N \rvert - i][\text{tr}[u][d]]\). Perspective 2: Goulden–Jackson Multi-Pattern Cluster Method (Ch 08)

What if individual pattern lengths \(\lvert P_i \rvert\) are huge (say \(\sum \lvert P_i \rvert = 10^5\)), while the number of patterns \(m\) is small (\(m \le 20\))?   2. Cluster Matrix \(C(x)\): For each ordered pair \((P_i, P_j)\), let \(O(P_i, P_j)\) be the set of overlap lengths \(1 \le k < \min(\lvert P_i \rvert, \lvert P_j \rvert)\) where the suffix of \(P_i\) of length \(k\) equals the prefix of \(P_j\) of length \(k\). Define the overlap polynomial

\[C_{i, j}(x) = \sum_{k \in O(P_i, P_j)} x^{\lvert P_j \rvert - k}.\]

By the Goulden–Jackson Cluster Theorem (Chapter 08), the cluster weight generating functions \(\mathbf{w}(x) = (w_1(x), \dots, w_m(x))^\top\) satisfy the \(m \times m\) linear system

\[(I + C(x)) \mathbf{w}(x) = \begin{pmatrix} x^{\lvert P_1 \rvert} \\ \vdots \\ x^{\lvert P_m \rvert} \end{pmatrix}, \quad F(x) = \frac{1}{1 - 10x + \sum_{i=1}^m w_i(x)}.\]

Perspective 1: 3D Poset Hook Product & At-Least-\(k\) Binomial Inversion (Ch 08 \(\times\) Ch 06 \(\times\) Ch 04)

The Diagnostic Signal

Let \(g_r\) be the probability that there are exactly \(r\) great cells, and let \(f_j = \sum_{r=j}^D \binom{r}{j} g_r\) be the expected number of \(j\)-subsets of great cells. By Binomial Inversion (Chapter 06), the tail probability \(\Pr[\text{great cells} \ge k] = \sum_{r=k}^D g_r\) is given by the kernel \((-1)^{j-k} \binom{j-1}{k-1}\):

\[\sum_{r=k}^{D} g_r = \sum_{j=k}^{D} (-1)^{j - k} \binom{j - 1}{k - 1} f_j.\]

How do we compute \(f_j\)? First, if two cells share any coordinate, they lie in a common slice, so at most one of them can be the maximum of that slice. Thus any \(j\) great cells must have pairwise distinct \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-coordinates, and there are

\[\frac{n^{\underline{j}} m^{\underline{j}} l^{\underline{j}}}{j!}\]

such unordered \(j\)-subsets of cells \(C = \{c_1, \dots, c_j\}\).

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Why isn’t the probability that all \(j\) cells in \(C\) are great simply a product of independent slice sizes? Because for \(r \neq s\), the slice neighborhood of \(c_r\) and the slice neighborhood of \(c_s\) intersect (for instance, at \((x_r, y_s, z_s)\))!

Whenever poset maximum conditions overlap symmetrically across \(j\) roots \(c_1, \dots, c_j\), break the overlap by conditioning on the relative order of the \(j\) roots: * There are \(j!\) linear orderings of the values of \(c_1, \dots, c_j\), all equally likely by symmetry. Fix the ordering \(\text{val}(c_j) > \text{val}(c_{j-1}) > \dots > \text{val}(c_1)\). * Let \(\mathcal{N}_i\) be the union of the slice neighborhoods of \(\{c_1, \dots, c_i\}\). A cell \((x, y, z)\) lies outside \(\mathcal{N}_i\) iff \(x \notin \{x_1, \dots, x_i\}\), \(y \notin \{y_1, \dots, y_i\}\), and \(z \notin \{z_1, \dots, z_i\}\), so \(\lvert \overline{\mathcal{N}_i} \rvert = (n - i)(m - i)(l - i)\) and

\[U_i = \lvert \mathcal{N}_i \rvert = n m l - (n - i)(m - i)(l - i).\]

  • Under the ordering \(\text{val}(c_j) > \text{val}(c_{j-1}) > \dots > \text{val}(c_1)\), the condition that every \(c_i\) beats its own slice neighborhood (given that \(c_{i+1}, \dots, c_j\) already beat theirs!) is equivalent to requiring that for each \(i = j, j-1, \dots, 1\), \(c_i\) has the single largest value among all \(U_i\) cells in \(\mathcal{N}_i\)!
  • Because \(\mathcal{N}_1 \subset \mathcal{N}_2 \subset \dots \subset \mathcal{N}_j\) is a strictly nested chain of subsets, the event that \(c_j = \max(\mathcal{N}_j)\), \(c_{j-1} = \max(\mathcal{N}_{j-1})\), \(\dots\), \(c_1 = \max(\mathcal{N}_1)\) has probability equal to the telescoping hook product:

\[\Pr[c_1, \dots, c_j \text{ all great}] = j! \prod_{i=1}^{j} \frac{1}{U_i}.\]

Multiplying by \(\frac{n^{\underline{j}} m^{\underline{j}} l^{\underline{j}}}{j!}\) cancels \(j!\) and yields:

\[f_j = n^{\underline{j}} m^{\underline{j}} l^{\underline{j}} \prod_{i=1}^{j} \frac{1}{n m l - (n - i)(m - i)(l - i)}.\]

Perspective 2: Continuous-Time \(\operatorname{Exp}(1)\) Arrival Integral (Ch 14)

Instead of discrete permutations of \(\{1, \dots, n m l\}\), assign each cell \(c\) an independent exponential random variable \(T_c \sim \operatorname{Exp}(1)\), and declare \(c\) to have a larger value than \(c'\) iff \(T_c < T_{c'}\) (earliest arrival wins).

Fix the arrival times \(0 < t_j < t_{j-1} < \dots < t_1 < \infty\) of the \(j\) cells \(c_j, \dots, c_1\). For each cell \(c \in \mathcal{N}_i \setminus \mathcal{N}_{i-1}\) (there are \(U_i - U_{i-1}\) such cells, whose earliest required dominator among \(\{c_1, \dots, c_j\}\) is \(c_i\) at time \(t_i\)), we must have \(T_c > t_i\), which happens with probability \(e^{-t_i}\). Integrating over the simplex \(0 < t_j < \dots < t_1 < \infty\):

\[\begin{aligned} \Pr[c_1, \dots, c_j \text{ all great}] &= j! \int_{0 < t_j < \dots < t_1 < \infty} \prod_{i=1}^{j} e^{-(U_i - U_{i-1} - 1) t_i} e^{-t_i} \, dt_1 \cdots dt_j \\ &= j! \int_{0 < t_j < \dots < t_1 < \infty} \prod_{i=1}^{j} e^{-(U_i - U_{i-1}) t_i} \, dt_1 \cdots dt_j = j! \prod_{i=1}^{j} \frac{1}{U_i}, \end{aligned}\]

via the change of variables \(s_i = t_i - t_{i+1} > 0\), where \(\sum_{i=1}^j (U_i - U_{i-1}) t_i = \sum_{i=1}^j U_i s_i\) decouples the simplex integral into \(j\) independent integrals \(\int_0^\infty e^{-U_i s_i} ds_i = \frac{1}{U_i}\)!

  • Perspective 2 (Picard–Queyranne Poset Ideal / Minimum-Cut Closure + Binary Search): For any fixed time threshold \(T\), choosing a subset of shops \(Q\) and their required plants \(P = \bigcup_{j \in Q} S_j\) is a Maximum Weight Closure problem on the bipartite implication poset:
    • Source \(s \to \text{shop } j\) with capacity \(pro_j\);
    • \(\text{shop } j \to \text{plant } i \in S_j\) with capacity \(+\infty\);
    • \(\text{plant } i \to \text{sink } t\) with capacity \(pay_i\) if \(t_i \le T\), or \(+\infty\) if \(t_i > T\) (or simply exclude any shop \(j\) that requires a plant with \(t_i > T\)).
Hint 3 — Key Equations & Extraction Contract

For a fixed time threshold \(T\), let \(Q(T) = \{j \in \{1, \dots, M\} : \max_{i \in S_j} t_i \le T\}\) be the shops whose prerequisite plants can all be completed within time \(T\), and \(P(T) = \{i \in \{1, \dots, N\} : t_i \le T\}\). In the closure flow network \(\mathcal{G}(T)\) on \(Q(T) \cup P(T)\):

\[f(T) = \sum_{j \in Q(T)} pro_j - \operatorname{MinCut}(\mathcal{G}(T)) = \sum_{j \in Q(T)} pro_j - \operatorname{MaxFlow}(\mathcal{G}(T)).\]

Since \(f(T)\) is monotonically non-decreasing in \(T\), binary search over the sorted array of distinct build times \(T_{(1)} < \dots < T_{(K)}\) (\(K \le N\)) in \(\le \lceil \log_2 N \rceil + 1 \le 8\) Dinic max-flow calls!

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Parametric Time-Filtered Poset Ideals (Ch 05 \(\times\) Ch 06)

The Diagnostic Signal

Why is this problem placed in the Synthesis Reserve alongside generating function tools? Because on a bipartite implication poset \((Q \cup P, \preceq)\), every valid configuration is an order ideal (down-set) \(I \subseteq Q \cup P\): if \(j \in I \cap Q\) and \(i \in S_j\), then \(i \in I \cap P\).

In small-width posets, one enumerates order ideals via a transfer matrix or bivariate generating function \(\sum_{I} x^{\max_{i \in I} t_i} y^{\text{profit}(I)}\). However, when \(N, M \le 200\) and the implication graph is an arbitrary bipartite DAG without bounded treewidth, counting all ideals is #P-hard, whereas optimizing the maximum weight ideal at each threshold \(T\) is polynomial-time solvable via submodular minimization / network flow!

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Filter the poset by the time parameter \(T\): * Define the completion requirement of shop \(j\) as \(\tau_j = \max_{i \in S_j} t_i\). * Under time budget \(T\), any plant \(i\) with \(t_i > T\) cannot be built, and consequently any shop \(j\) with \(\tau_j > T\) cannot be opened. * Conversely, any subset of shops \(Q' \subseteq Q(T) = \{j : \tau_j \le T\}\) uses only plants in \(P' = \bigcup_{j \in Q'} S_j \subseteq P(T) = \{i : t_i \le T\}\), so its completion time is guaranteed to be \(\le T\)! * Therefore, the maximum profit achievable within time \(T\) is

\[f(T) = \max_{Q' \subseteq Q(T)} \left( \sum_{j \in Q'} pro_j - \sum_{i \in \bigcup_{j \in Q'} S_j} pay_i \right).\]

Because \(Q(T_1) \subseteq Q(T_2)\) whenever \(T_1 \le T_2\), the feasible family of shop subsets grows monotonically with \(T\), proving \(f(T_1) \le f(T_2)\).

Perspective 2: Picard–Queyranne Project Closure Minimum Cut & Binary Search (Ch 08)

To evaluate \(f(T)\) for a fixed threshold \(T\), construct a directed flow network \(\mathcal{G}(T)\) with source \(s\) and sink \(t\): 1. For each feasible shop \(j \in Q(T)\) (\(\tau_j \le T\)), add a directed edge \(s \to j\) with capacity \(pro_j\). 2. For each feasible plant \(i \in P(T)\) (\(t_i \le T\)), add a directed edge \(i \to t\) with capacity \(pay_i\). 3. For each \(j \in Q(T)\) and each prerequisite plant \(i \in S_j\), add a directed edge \(j \to i\) with capacity \(+\infty\).

Consider any \(s\)-\(t\) cut \((S, \bar{S})\) with finite capacity (\(s \in S, t \in \bar{S}\)): * Because edges \(j \to i\) have capacity \(+\infty\), no finite-capacity cut can have \(j \in S\) and \(i \in \bar{S}\). Thus if a shop \(j \in S \cap Q(T)\) is selected, all of its prerequisite plants \(i \in S_j\) must also lie in \(S \cap P(T)\)! * The capacity of the cut \((S, \bar{S})\) is

\[\operatorname{cap}(S, \bar{S}) = \sum_{j \in Q(T) \setminus S} pro_j + \sum_{i \in P(T) \cap S} pay_i = \sum_{j \in Q(T)} pro_j - \left( \sum_{j \in Q(T) \cap S} pro_j - \sum_{i \in P(T) \cap S} pay_i \right).\]

Minimizing \(\operatorname{cap}(S, \bar{S})\) over all finite cuts simultaneously maximizes the net profit \(\sum_{j \in Q(T) \cap S} pro_j - \sum_{i \in P(T) \cap S} pay_i\):

\[f(T) = \sum_{j \in Q(T)} pro_j - \operatorname{MaxFlow}(\mathcal{G}(T)).\]

Binary searching over the sorted unique values of \(\{t_1, \dots, t_N\}\) finds the smallest \(T_{\min}\) with \(f(T_{\min}) \ge L\), and \(P_{\max} = f(T_{\min})\).

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Complementary Goulden–Jackson Pattern Avoidance (Ch 08 \(\times\) Ch 05)

The Diagnostic Signal

Counting strings that contain \(P\) at least once is the exact complement of counting strings of length \(n\) that avoid \(P\) altogether:

\[\text{Ans}_n = \sigma^n - [x^n] A(x).\]

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

By the single-pattern Goulden–Jackson Cluster Method (Chapter 08), if we mark overlapping bad occurrences of \(P\) with weight \(-1\), any cluster of overlapping occurrences of \(P\) is generated by \(w(x)\) satisfying

\[(1 + C_{P, P}(x)) w(x) = x^m \implies w(x) = \frac{x^m}{C_P(x)},\]

where \(C_P(x) = 1 + C_{P, P}(x) = \sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}(P)} x^{m - b}\) sums \(x^{m - b}\) over all border lengths \(1 \le b \le m\) with \(P[1 \dots b] = P[m - b + 1 \dots m]\) (easily extracted in \(O(m)\) time by walking the KMP prefix function \(b = m, \pi[m], \pi[\pi[m]], \dots > 0\)).

Substituting \(w(x)\) into the cluster series \(A(x) = \frac{1}{1 - \sigma x + w(x)}\) gives the rational generating function for \(P\)-avoiding strings:

\[A(x) = \frac{1}{1 - \sigma x + \frac{x^m}{C_P(x)}} = \frac{C_P(x)}{(1 - \sigma x) C_P(x) + x^m}.\]

Subtracting \(A(x)\) from the all-strings OGF \(\frac{1}{1 - \sigma x}\) cancels \((1 - \sigma x) C_P(x)\) in the numerator:

\[G(x) = \frac{1}{1 - \sigma x} - \frac{C_P(x)}{(1 - \sigma x) C_P(x) + x^m} = \frac{x^m}{(1 - \sigma x)\bigl((1 - \sigma x) C_P(x) + x^m\bigr)}.\]

Let us check our sample traces (\(n = 3, m = 2, \sigma = 26\)): * For \(P = \text{"AB"}\), \(C_P(x) = 1\): \(G(x) = \frac{x^2}{(1 - 26x)(1 - 26x + x^2)} = x^2 (1 + 26x + \cdots)(1 + 26x + \cdots) \implies [x^3] G(x) = 26 + 26 = 52\). * For \(P = \text{"AA"}\), \(C_P(x) = 1 + x\): \(G(x) = \frac{x^2}{(1 - 26x)((1 - 26x)(1 + x) + x^2)} = \frac{x^2}{(1 - 26x)(1 - 25x - 25x^2)} = x^2 (1 + 26x + \cdots)(1 + 25x + \cdots) \implies [x^3] G(x) = 26 + 25 = 51\). Both match our hand trace!

Perspective 2: First-Passage Hitting-Time PGF & KMP Automaton Transfer (Ch 14)

Can we derive the exact factor \(\frac{x^m}{(1 - \sigma x)((1 - \sigma x)C_P(x) + x^m)}\) directly from first-passage decomposition without Inclusion–Exclusion?

Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be the language of strings avoiding \(P\) (with OGF \(A(x)\)), and let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the language of strings that contain \(P\) only once, as a suffix (the first-passage language, with OGF \(F(x)\)). 1. Every avoiding string extended by \(1\) character either remains in \(\mathcal{A}\) or enters \(\mathcal{F}\) for the first time (or is empty):

\[1 + \sigma x A(x) = A(x) + F(x) \implies (1 - \sigma x) A(x) + F(x) = 1.\]

  1. Every string in \(\mathcal{A} \cdot P\) (obtained by appending \(P\) to an avoiding string, OGF \(A(x) x^m\)) contains its first occurrence of \(P\) ending at distance \(m - b\) from the end, where \(b \in \mathcal{B}(P)\) is the overlap border between that first occurrence and the suffix \(P\):

\[A(x) x^m = F(x) C_P(x).\]

Solving these two linear equations for \(F(x)\) immediately yields

\[F(x) = \frac{x^m}{(1 - \sigma x) C_P(x) + x^m}.\]

Finally, every string containing \(P\) at least once decomposes uniquely as a first-passage prefix \(u \in \mathcal{F}\) followed by an arbitrary suffix \(v \in \Sigma^*\) (generated by \(\frac{1}{1 - \sigma x}\)), so

\[G(x) = F(x) \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \sigma x} = \frac{x^m}{(1 - \sigma x)\bigl((1 - \sigma x) C_P(x) + x^m\bigr)}.\]

(Algorithmic Note): For \(n \le 1000, m \le 100\), either extract \([x^n] G(x)\) via the degree-\((m+1)\) linear recurrence defined by its denominator \(Q(x) = (1 - \sigma x)((1 - \sigma x)C_P(x) + x^m)\) in \(O(n m)\) time (with zero alphabet factor \(\sigma\)!), or build the \(m\)-state KMP automaton in \(O(m \sigma)\) and run the \(O(n m \sigma)\) DP; for \(n \le 10^{18}\), Bostan–Mori extracts \([x^n] G(x)\) in \(O(m \log m \log n)\) time!

Syn-14 (#253) — Codeforces 961G Partitions (Linearity of Expectation vs. Pointed Bivariate Partition EGF)

Problem Statement

You are given \(n\) items with weights \(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n\). The weight of a non-empty subset \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\) is defined as

\[W(S) = \lvert S \rvert \sum_{i \in S} w_i.\]

The weight of a partition \(\mathcal{P} = \{S_1, S_2, \dots, S_k\}\) of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(k\) non-empty disjoint subsets is \(\sum_{r=1}^k W(S_r)\). Sum the weights of all partitions of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(k\) non-empty blocks, modulo \(10^9 + 7\).

Constraints

  • \(1 \le n, k \le 2 \times 10^5\)
  • \(1 \le w_i \le 10^9\)
  • Modulus \(p = 10^9 + 7\)
  • Required complexity: \(O(k \log p)\) or \(O(k)\) time and \(O(k)\) space.

Sample Trace (\(N \le 4\))

  • \(n = 4, k = 2\), weights \(w = (1, 2, 3, 4)\) (\(\sum w_i = 10\)):
    • There are \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right\} = 7\) partitions of \(\{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) into \(2\) blocks:
      • Four \(1 + 3\) splits (e.g. \(\{1\} \cup \{2, 3, 4\}\)): in each, the singleton has size \(1\) and the triple has size \(3\). Across the 4 splits, each element \(i\) is a singleton \(1\) time (contributing \(1 \cdot w_i\)) and in a triple \(3\) times (contributing \(3 \cdot w_i\)), total \(10 w_i\).
      • Three \(2 + 2\) splits (e.g. \(\{1, 2\} \cup \{3, 4\}\)): in each, every block has size \(2\), so each element \(i\) contributes \(2 \cdot w_i\) in each of the 3 splits, total \(6 w_i\).
    • Total coefficient of each \(w_i\) is \(10 + 6 = 16 = \left\{\begin{smallmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right\} + 3 \left\{\begin{smallmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{smallmatrix}\right\} = 7 + 3 \times 3 = 16\).
    • Total weight: \(16 \times (1 + 2 + 3 + 4) = 160\).
Hint 1 — Diagnostic Signal & Combinatorial Model

By symmetry and Linearity of Summation, every item \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) belongs to some unique block \(S \ni i\) of size \(\lvert S \rvert\), so its weight \(w_i\) is multiplied by \(\lvert S \rvert\) in that partition. Because the partition structure is completely symmetric with respect to the \(n\) item labels, the total sum must have the form

\[\text{Ans} = C(n, k) \sum_{i=1}^n w_i,\]

where \(C(n, k)\) is the sum of \(\lvert S_i \rvert\) (the size of the block containing a fixed item \(i\), say item \(1\)) over all \(k\)-block partitions of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\).

Hint 2 — Perspective 1 vs. Perspective 2 Setup
  • Perspective 1 (Pair-Indicator Split): Write the size of the block \(S\) containing item \(i\) as \(\lvert S \rvert = 1 + \sum_{j \neq i} [j \in S]\).
    • The constant \(1\) (for \(i\) itself) is summed over all \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) partitions.
    • For each of the \(n - 1\) other items \(j \neq i\), gluing \(i\) and \(j\) into a single super-element leaves \(n - 1\) elements to be partitioned into \(k\) blocks in \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) ways!
  • Perspective 2 (Pointed Bivariate Partition EGF): Sum \(\binom{n-1}{s-1} s \left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n - s \\ k - 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) over the size \(s \ge 1\) of item \(1\)’s block by extracting \((n-1)! [x^{n-1}] \frac{\partial}{\partial u} \left( e^{u x} \frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \right)\Big\vert_{u=1}\).
Hint 3 — Key Equations & Extraction Contract

Both perspectives collapse to the exact same two-Stirling-number identity:

\[C(n, k) = \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} + (n - 1) \begin{Bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{Bmatrix},\]

where each Stirling number \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} m \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\} = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{k-j} \binom{k}{j} j^m\) is evaluated in \(O(k \log p)\) (or \(O(k)\) with a linear sieve) without even needing an FFT!

Complete Dual-Perspective Derivation & Comparative Analysis

Perspective 1: Linearity of Expectation & Pair-Coincidence Decomposition (Ch 14 \(\times\) Ch 06)

The Diagnostic Signal

Expand the weight of a partition \(\mathcal{P} = \{S_1, \dots, S_k\}\) by decomposing the block size \(\lvert S_r \rvert = \sum_{j \in S_r} 1\):

\[\sum_{r=1}^k \lvert S_r \rvert \sum_{i \in S_r} w_i = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \left( 1 + \sum_{j \neq i} [j \text{ is in the same block as } i \text{ in } \mathcal{P}] \right).\]

The Reverse-Engineering Scratchpad

Summing over all \(\mathcal{P} \in \Pi(n, k)\) (partitions of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(k\) non-empty blocks): 1. Diagonal contribution (\(j = i\)): In every partition \(\mathcal{P} \in \Pi(n, k)\), item \(i\) is in the same block as itself. There are \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) such partitions. 2. Off-diagonal contribution (\(j \neq i\)): Fix any ordered pair \((i, j)\) with \(j \neq i\) (for a fixed \(i\), there are \(n - 1\) choices for \(j\)). How many partitions \(\mathcal{P} \in \Pi(n, k)\) place \(i\) and \(j\) into the same block? Fuse \(i\) and \(j\) into a single composite element \(\{i, j\}\). Any partition of the resulting \(n - 1\) elements into \(k\) non-empty blocks corresponds bijectively to a partition of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) into \(k\) non-empty blocks where \(i\) and \(j\) share a block! Thus there are \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) such partitions for each \(j \neq i\).

Combining both contributions immediately gives:

\[C(n, k) = \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} + (n - 1) \begin{Bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{Bmatrix}.\]

Perspective 2: Pointed Bivariate EGF & Operator Differentiation (Ch 04)

Alternatively, single out item \(1\) (the “pointed” element). If item \(1\)’s block has size \(s \ge 1\), we choose the other \(s - 1\) elements of its block in \(\binom{n-1}{s-1}\) ways, multiply by the block size \(s\), and partition the remaining \(n - s\) elements into \(k - 1\) non-empty blocks in \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n - s \\ k - 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) ways:

\[C(n, k) = \sum_{s=1}^{n} \binom{n - 1}{s - 1} s \begin{Bmatrix} n - s \\ k - 1 \end{Bmatrix}.\]

In the EGF ring, the pointed block of size \(s\) has generator \(\sum_{s \ge 1} s \frac{x^{s-1}}{(s-1)!} = \frac{d}{dx}(x e^x) = (1 + x) e^x\), while the remaining \(k - 1\) blocks have generator \(\frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\). Multiplying them gives the EGF for \(C(n, k)\) with respect to \(n - 1\):

\[\begin{aligned} C(n, k) &= (n - 1)! [x^{n - 1}] \left( (1 + x) e^x \frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \right) \\ &= (n - 1)! [x^{n - 1}] \left( e^x \frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \right) + (n - 1)! [x^{n - 2}] \left( e^x \frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \right). \end{aligned}\]

Since \(\frac{d}{dx} \left( \frac{(e^x - 1)^k}{k!} \right) = e^x \frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\), we have \(m! [x^m] \left( e^x \frac{(e^x - 1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \right) = (m + 1)! [x^{m+1}] \frac{(e^x - 1)^k}{k!} = \left\{\begin{smallmatrix} m + 1 \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\). Applying this at \(m = n - 1\) and \(m = n - 2\) gives

\[C(n, k) = \begin{Bmatrix} n \\ k \end{Bmatrix} + (n - 1) \begin{Bmatrix} n - 1 \\ k \end{Bmatrix}.\]

Notice how the differential identity \((1 + x) e^x = e^x + x e^x\) is the exact EGF dual of Perspective 1’s indicator split \(\lvert S \rvert = 1 + (\lvert S \rvert - 1)\)!

Using \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} m \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\} = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{k-j} \binom{k}{j} j^m\), we can combine both Stirling evaluations into a single loop over \(0 \le j \le k\):

\[C(n, k) = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k - j} \binom{k}{j} j^{n - 1} (j + n - 1) \quad (\text{for } n \ge 1).\]

In the combined single-loop formula \(\sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{k-j} \binom{k}{j} j^{n-1} (j + n - 1)\), when \(n = 1\) and \(k = 1\), the \(j = 0\) term has \(0^{1-1} \cdot (0 + 0) = 1 \cdot 0 = 0\), whereas \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right\} + 0 \cdot \left\{\begin{smallmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right\} = 1\)! Why? Because \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} 0 \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\} = [k = 0]\), whereas \(\sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{k-j} \binom{k}{j} 0^{-1}\) is invalid at \(m = 0\). Either handle \(n = 1\) explicitly (if (n == 1) return k == 1 ? w[1] : 0;) or evaluate \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) and \((n-1)\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n-1 \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\) with proper \(0^0 = 1\).

Algorithm Contract

  • Input: \(n, k \le 2 \times 10^5\) and weights \(w_1, \dots, w_n\).
  • Output: \(C(n, k) \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\right) \bmod 10^9 + 7\).
  • Algebraic Preconditions: If \(k > n\), return \(0\). If \(n = 1, k = 1\), return \(w_1 \bmod p\). For \(n \ge 2\), \(p = 10^9 + 7 > k\).
  • Complexity: \(O(n + k \log p)\) time and \(O(k)\) space.
  • Correctness Invariant: For \(n \ge 2\), the \(j\)-th summand equals \(\frac{(-1)^{k-j}}{j!(k-j)!} (j^n + (n-1) j^{n-1})\), summing over \(0 \le j \le k\) to \(\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\} + (n-1)\left\{\begin{smallmatrix} n-1 \\ k \end{smallmatrix}\right\}\).
